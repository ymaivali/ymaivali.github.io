11  The potential-outcomes framework

Part II built the logic of graded belief; Part III returns to the question that logic is meant to serve. Chapter 4 made the case that a causal claim is a claim about parallel worlds — what would happen to a particular patient if treated, against what would happen to the same patient if not — and that the randomised experiment is the machine that lets us compare those worlds fairly. This chapter turns that picture into notation that says which statistical quantity a study is trying to estimate and what would have to be true for the ordinary machinery of averages and regressions to estimate it. “The effect of the drug” becomes a quantity we can define before we try to compute it. The chapters that follow in this part supply the rest: which variables make a comparison fair (Chapter 12), how to compute the effect once they are known (Chapter 13), and what changes when treatment and follow-up unfold over time (Chapter 14, Chapter 15).

11.1 A second outcome that never happens

Fix a patient and a binary treatment — our blood-pressure drug, \(A = 1\) for treated, \(A = 0\) for not. Write \(Y(1)\) for the stroke outcome this patient would have under treatment and \(Y(0)\) for the outcome the same patient would have without it. These hypotheticals are the potential outcomes: two values attached to one person, only one of which the world will ever reveal. The patient’s individual causal effect is the contrast \(Y(1) - Y(0)\) — which is, in general, unknowable, because we see the patient under one treatment only. The other potential outcome is a counterfactual: real enough to define, impossible to observe. This is Holland’s fundamental problem of causal inference.

Two ways round the fundamental problem

Holland distinguishes two routes past the missing potential outcome.1 The scientific solution observes the same unit under both treatments, one after the other, and treats the second observation as the counterfactual of the first. It is the everyday logic of the laboratory (the same enzyme preparation with and without inhibitor) and it needs three assumptions that are rarely stated: temporal stability (the outcome under a treatment does not depend on when the treatment is applied), causal transience (the first treatment leaves no trace that alters the response to the second), and unit homogeneity when different units are used instead (every unit would respond alike). Where any of the three fails — a patient who has already had one stroke, a plot exhausted by last year’s crop — the second observation is not the counterfactual of the first. The statistical solution, which the rest of this book follows, gives up on the individual contrast, observes many units under one treatment each, and targets an average effect; its price is the exchangeability assumption introduced below, which randomisation buys and observational studies must argue for.

Two assumptions glue this notation to data. The first is consistency: the outcome we actually observe is the potential outcome for the treatment actually received, \(Y = Y(A)\). It sounds trivial, but is not — it presumes the intervention is well defined, that “treated” refers to one thing and not to a bundle of different things with different effects (the dose, route, adherence). The second is no interference (part of SUTVA): one patient’s treatment does not change another’s outcome, so \(Y_i(a)\) depends on patient \(i\)’s own treatment alone. Both can fail — interference is the rule in vaccine and network studies — and when they do, the contrast \(Y(1)-Y(0)\) is no longer the right target and has to be redefined; how depends on which part broke (see below).

In real life the counterfactual column is missing. In our simulated cohort it is not: we could compute each patient’s risk under both treatments. It is worth looking at the thing we almost never get to see.

Everything that follows is about what can be learned about the hidden counterfactual column from the visible one. Before that, two boxes on the fine print of the notation: what licenses \(Y_i(a)\) in the first place, and what happens to the estimand when that licence fails. The terms direct, indirect and total effect in the second box carry their interference sense; Chapter 16 uses the same words for a different decomposition.

SUTVA: what lets us write \(Y_i(a)\) at all

The notation \(Y_i(a)\) — one potential outcome per unit per treatment level — comes from the Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption (SUTVA, Rubin’s term). It has two parts, both of which must hold for the expression even to make sense.

  • No interference. Unit \(i\)’s potential outcomes depend only on \(i\)’s own treatment, so we may write \(Y_i(a)\) rather than \(Y_i(a_1, a_2, \dots)\). This fails whenever one unit’s treatment can influence another — vaccines and herd immunity, infectious disease, treated partners in a household, classroom- or village-level assignment, market-wide effects. Then each unit has a separate potential outcome for every configuration of everyone’s treatment, and the tidy contrast \(Y(1) - Y(0)\) is not even defined.
  • One version of treatment (no hidden variations). “Treated” must name a single, well-specified intervention. If \(A = 1\) secretly bundles many versions with different effects — different doses, formulations, surgeons, or simply different ways of “being treated” — then \(Y(1)\) is an average over an unspecified mixture and changes meaning with it. This is the same demand as consistency: \(Y = Y(A)\) is only informative when \(A = 1\) refers to one thing.

SUTVA is therefore not a box to tick at the end of an analysis; it is the licence to write the estimand in the first place. When meeting it is doubtful, one should sharpen the intervention (a specific dose and schedule) or model the interference explicitly (partial-interference or network designs), rather than press on with an ambiguous \(Y(a)\). It is also why an “effect” of a fixed attribute — the “effect of sex”, say, or “of race” — strains the framework: there is no agreed intervention that sets such a variable, so its potential outcome has no settled referent (contrast a modifiable trait like obesity, where the trouble is the opposite — many different ways one could intervene, with different effects). This is the well-defined-intervention point we return to at the end of the chapter.

How \(Y(1)-Y(0)\) is redefined depends on which half of SUTVA gave way

If the failure is interference, a unit’s outcome depends on the whole pattern of treatment, then the potential outcome gains arguments — \(Y_i(\mathbf{a})\), a function of the entire assignment vector \(\mathbf{a} = (a_1, \dots, a_n)\) rather than of \(a_i\) alone. That is up to \(2^n\) potential outcomes per person instead of two, which is hopeless without structure, so one must impose some. Partial interference assumes units sit in groups — households, classrooms, villages — with spillover only within a group; an exposure mapping assumes further that a unit’s outcome depends on the rest only through a low-dimensional summary, typically the coverage \(c\) (the fraction treated in its group), which collapses the outcome to \(Y_i(a_i, c)\) in own-treatment \(a_i\) and coverage \(c\). The single contrast then splits into a family of well-defined effects:

  • a direct effect — switch my own treatment, holding the group’s coverage fixed;
  • an indirect, or spillover, effect — change the group’s coverage, holding my own treatment fixed (the protection an unvaccinated person gains from a vaccinated community);
  • a total effect — the two together, treated-under-high-coverage versus untreated-under-low; and
  • an overall effect — compare two whole allocation programmes, say 0% against 70% coverage, averaged over everyone.

Which of these is “the” effect? “Should this patient be vaccinated?” asks for the direct effect; “should we fund a 70%-coverage programme?” asks for the overall effect; and — herd immunity being the point of vaccination — the two can diverge sharply. These effects are not mere definitions: once the exposure structure is recorded — who was treated directly, and who only through a treated network member — they are identifiable and routinely estimated in network-randomised trials with standard correlated-data methods.2

If instead the failure is multiple versions of treatment, redefinition means naming the version. The estimand becomes the effect of one specified intervention — a dose, a schedule, a surgical technique — or, when the versions genuinely cannot be pinned down, the effect of a stated distribution over them (a “modified treatment policy” that assigns versions as they arise in practice). Either way the manoeuvre is the same in spirit: replace a vague switch with a contrast of interventions sharp enough that \(Y(\cdot)\) has a single value again — which is, in the end, the lesson of the well-defined-intervention caveat at the close of this chapter.

11.2 The estimands: average effects and their scales

As individual effects are out of reach, we target averages. The average treatment effect is the contrast of potential outcomes over the whole population, \[ \text{ATE} \;=\; E[Y(1)] - E[Y(0)], \] the stroke risk if everyone were treated against the risk if no one were. A close relative is the average treatment effect in the treated, \(\text{ATT} = E[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid A = 1]\), the effect among the patients who actually got the drug — a different and often more policy-relevant quantity whenever the effect varies across people. Its counterpart, the average treatment effect in the untreated, \(\text{ATU} = E[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid A = 0]\), asks what the drug would do for those who did not receive it. This is the estimand behind a decision to extend treatment to them, as against ATT’s question of whether to withdraw it from those who have it. The three are tied by the share treated, \(\text{ATE} = P(A{=}1)\,\text{ATT} + P(A{=}0)\,\text{ATU}\), so once the effect varies across people they part company — and the gap between ATT and ATU is itself a causal signal: it says whether treatment reached the people it helps most. These are estimands: precise targets chosen before estimation, not whatever a model happens to spit out.

A contrast also needs a scale, the choice of which is substantive. The same two risks \(E[Y(1)]\) and \(E[Y(0)]\) can be reported as a risk difference \(E[Y(1)] - E[Y(0)]\), a risk ratio \(E[Y(1)]/E[Y(0)]\), or an odds ratio. They answer different questions — absolute public-health burden versus relative effect — and they are not interchangeable; the odds ratio in particular is non-collapsible, so it can shift when you add covariates even with no confounding (Chapter 25). Our oracle reports all of them.

Risk ratio against risk difference: drink-driving and the population burden

The choice of scale is concrete in road safety. A meta-analysis of 60 studies found that the relationship between blood alcohol concentration (BAC) and crash risk is approximately exponential: at a BAC around 0.05 g/dL the risk ratio for crash involvement is roughly 1.5–2.5 against a sober driver, and at BAC ≥ 0.08 g/dL a prospective responsibility study of injured drivers found an odds ratio of 6.0 (95% confidence interval 3.9–9.8) for having caused the crash.34 The effect is stronger for more serious outcomes and, in studies from the Nordic countries, larger than elsewhere — possibly because drink-driving is rarer there, so impaired drivers stand out against a more uniformly sober driving population. Consider a driver at a BAC of roughly 0.03–0.07 g/dL: above the Estonian legal limit of 0.02 g/dL but below the level most drivers associate with obvious impairment, and in the range where the risk ratio is about 2. Suppose, for illustration, that a sober driver on a 10-km trip in such a country faces an absolute probability of any recorded crash of about 1–2 in 50 000 — the order of magnitude implied by a thousand or so injury crashes a year over several billion vehicle-kilometres. Doubling it gives 2–4 in 50 000. The increment is small enough that nearly all such trips end without incident, which matches the reported experience of most occasional drink-drivers and is not a reasoning error on their part: on the absolute scale the per-trip risk is genuinely low.

The error is in the inference from individual experience to the population. Suppose, again for illustration, that 2% of a driving population of 800 000 occasionally drive after drinking, and that each does so ten times a year: 160 000 such trips annually. At an excess absolute risk of 1–2 per 50 000 trips — the risk difference that a risk ratio of 2 implies at this baseline — the expected number of crashes attributable to this group alone is 3–6 a year, and drivers at higher BACs, or who drive impaired more often or at night, contribute far more. The attributable fraction among the exposed, \((\text{RR} - 1)/\text{RR}\), is one half at a risk ratio of 2: half of the crashes on these trips would not have happened sober. The risk ratio was 2 throughout; the population burden is large not because any one trip is dangerous but because a small risk difference, multiplied by many exposures, produces a substantial expected count. A risk assessment conducted at the individual level, on the ratio scale alone, misses this — which is why the oracle reports both scales, and why the risk difference is the scale of public-health burden.

The averages also conceal a spread across drivers that sharpens both points, and that Part IV names effect modification (Chapter 17). Before any alcohol, the baseline risk of a fatal crash per distance driven varies several-fold with age and sex: in US data a man aged 16–19 is involved in about 3.7 times as many fatal crashes per mile as the average driver (some 9 against 2.5 per 100 million miles), the rate falls to a minimum in middle age, and it rises again in the oldest drivers, among whom fragility — dying in a crash a younger person would survive — contributes alongside any rise in crash frequency.5 The alcohol multiplier is not constant across these groups either. For the more severe outcome of a fatal single-vehicle crash, the risk ratio just above the common 0.08 g/dL limit, against a sober driver of the same age and sex, has been put at about 11 for drivers aged 35 and over but around 50 for men aged 16–20, with each further 0.02 g/dL roughly doubling it.6 Baseline and multiplier vary in the same direction and so compound: the same drink adds far more absolute risk for a young man than for an older driver, though the two figures cannot simply be multiplied, being measured on different crash outcomes. “The risk of drink-driving” therefore has no single value; a risk ratio quoted for the average driver understates the absolute hazard for the group that supplies most of it and overstates it for the safest — the ATE, ATT and ATU distinction above in another guise.

Odds ratios and hazard ratios make poor causal estimands

Of the scales above, the risk difference and risk ratio are collapsible and are on the probability scale a patient or policy-maker actually reasons in. The odds ratio and the hazard ratio are neither, and both should be avoided as causal summaries.

Non-collapsibility. A measure is collapsible when the population (marginal) value is a weighted average of the stratum-specific values. The risk difference and the risk ratio are collapsible; the odds ratio is not. The consequence is unsettling: with no confounding whatsoever, an odds ratio adjusted for a covariate differs from the unadjusted one. Adding to a logistic model a risk factor that affects the outcome but has no link to the treatment drives the odds ratio away from 1, while the same addition leaves the causal reading of the risk difference and risk ratio intact. So the odds ratio depends on which covariates are in the model for reasons that have nothing to do with bias; two correctly specified analyses can report different odds ratios; and a population-average effect cannot be read off a logistic coefficient. Chapter 25 shows the mechanism, with the cohort, and gives the remedy: average the predicted risks over the covariate distribution and form the contrast on the risk scale (standardisation, Chapter 13) rather than exponentiating a coefficient.7 The hazard ratio is non-collapsible in the same way.

Odds are not probabilities. The odds of an event are \(P/(1-P)\), and an odds ratio, \([P_1/(1-P_1)]/[P_0/(1-P_0)]\), approximates the risk ratio \(P_1/P_0\) only when the outcome is rare. Whenever the two differ, the odds ratio lies further from 1 than the risk ratio, so reading an odds ratio as a risk ratio overstates the effect, and for a common outcome it overstates it a lot. Against a baseline risk of 30 %, an odds ratio of 2.0 corresponds to a risk of 46 %: a risk ratio of 1.54, a 54 % increase, not a doubling. Odds answer no question a clinician or a patient actually asks; risks do.

Hazard ratios hide a selection effect. A hazard at time \(t\) is a rate among those still event-free at \(t\). Once treatment has begun to act, the survivors in the two arms are no longer comparable: treatment has selectively kept some high-risk people event-free, so the treated risk set is frailer than the control risk set. The hazard ratio therefore conditions on a post-treatment variable, survival, and blends the genuine effect with a time-varying depletion-of-susceptibles selection.8 A constant hazard ratio need not reflect a constant individual effect, and even a flawless randomised trial can report a hazard ratio with no clean causal interpretation. Chapter 26 gives the closed form of the distortion and the estimands to prefer.

What to report instead. Prefer estimands that are collapsible, on the probability scale, and tied to an intervention: the risk difference and risk ratio at a stated time horizon, survival or cumulative-incidence differences, or the restricted mean survival time. This book targets the ATE on the risk-difference and risk-ratio scales throughout.

On the risk-difference scale the drug averts far more among the treated (ATT \(\approx\) -0.04) than it would among the untreated (ATU \(\approx\) -0.012), about 3.2 times the absolute benefit. On the risk-ratio scale the two are close (0.59 versus 0.635). Both facts hold because the drug cuts risk by a near-constant fraction, and the treated are the sicker patients with the higher baseline risk, so the same relative cut delivers a larger absolute reduction. The confounding by indication that ruins the crude comparison thus has a flip side: treatment was steered toward those with the most absolute risk to lose. Whether “the effect” is larger in the treated is therefore not a fact about the drug but about the scale it is read on (Chapter 17). ATT, ATU and ATE coincide only when the effect is homogeneous on that scale. That the ratio scale is so often the one on which effects are near-homogeneous, while the difference scale varies with baseline risk, is a recurring pattern and a reason the book reports both.9

The absolute effect can be easier to grasp as a number needed to treat (NNT): the number of patients one must treat to prevent a single event over a stated period. It is the reciprocal of the absolute risk difference, \(\text{NNT} = 1/|\text{RD}|\), so it carries the causal content of the RD. An NNT built from the ATE is a population causal quantity; an NNT built from the crude risk difference is as confounded as that difference. From the cohort’s true ATE, \(\text{NNT} \approx\) 37. It is far smaller in the high-risk treated (NNT \(\approx\) 26) than in the lower-risk untreated (NNT \(\approx\) 82), even though the relative effect is nearly the same in both. An NNT is not a property of the drug alone but of the drug applied to a particular baseline risk over a particular time horizon, and it must be reported with both. Turned around, a harmful contrast yields a number needed to harm: taken at face value, this cohort’s crude comparison would pin an NNH of about 43 on a drug that in truth prevents strokes.

11.3 Why the crude contrast is not the effect

The crude contrast estimates the association \(E[Y \mid A=1] - E[Y \mid A=0]\), and an association is a comparison of different patients — those who took the drug versus those who did not. The causal effect is a comparison of the same patients across worlds. The gap between them has an exact algebraic form. Among the treated, what we observe is \(E[Y \mid A=1] = E[Y(1) \mid A=1]\) by consistency; among the untreated, \(E[Y \mid A=0] = E[Y(0) \mid A=0]\). Add and subtract the missing counterfactual \(E[Y(0) \mid A=1]\) — the risk the treated would have run untreated — and the association splits cleanly: \[ \underbrace{E[Y\mid A{=}1] - E[Y\mid A{=}0]}_{\text{association}} =\underbrace{E[Y(1)\mid A{=}1]-E[Y(0)\mid A{=}1]}_{\text{ATT: a real effect}} +\underbrace{E[Y(0)\mid A{=}1]-E[Y(0)\mid A{=}0]}_{\text{confounding bias}} . \] The second term is the whole problem. It compares the untreated risk of the treated with the untreated risk of the untreated — two groups that, if treatment was assigned for a reason, were never alike to begin with. Normally it is invisible, because \(E[Y(0)\mid A=1]\) is counterfactual. The oracle shows it to us.

Show code 
EY0 <- cohort |>
  group_by(drug) |>
  summarise(EY0 = mean(risk0), .groups = "drop")      # untreated risk E[Y(0)] in each arm

EY0_treated   <- EY0 |> filter(drug == 1) |> pull(EY0)   # E[Y(0) | A=1]  -- counterfactual
EY0_untreated <- EY0 |> filter(drug == 0) |> pull(EY0)   # E[Y(0) | A=0]  -- observable

tibble(`E[Y(0)|treated]`   = EY0_treated,
       `E[Y(0)|untreated]` = EY0_untreated,
       confounding_gap     = EY0_treated - EY0_untreated) |>
  kbl(digits = 3, caption = "The confounding term made visible: the untreated risk the treated would have run, against the untreated risk of those actually untreated.") |>
  kable_styling(full_width = FALSE)

The treated, had they gone untreated, would have had a stroke risk of 0.097, about 2.9 times the 0.034 of those actually untreated. The treated were sicker all along (the drug goes to high-severity patients), so the confounding term is large and positive, 0.063, and it swamps the genuinely protective ATT, turning a beneficial drug into an apparently harmful one. This is confounding by indication, seen for once with the counterfactual laid bare rather than inferred.

11.4 What it takes to identify an effect

If the bias is the term \(E[Y(0)\mid A=1] - E[Y(0)\mid A=0]\), the cure is any condition that erases it.

  • Exchangeability. The treated and untreated groups are comparable: \(Y(a) \perp A\). In other words, if we had, counterfactually, given the treatment to the placebo group and vice versa, the causal estimate would not have changed. Marginally this is a strong demand, met by design in randomised trials (Chapter 4). In observational studies we aim for the weaker conditional exchangeability, \(Y(a) \perp A \mid L\): comparability within levels of a set of covariates \(L\). This means that for exchangeability we need to have the full adjustment set, measured correctly, and modelled correctly. Chapter 12 shows how to reason about which variables belong in \(L\) with the help of graphs.
  • Positivity. Every kind of patient could, with some non-zero probability, have received either treatment: \(0 < P(A = a \mid L) < 1\) for every \(L\) that occurs. Without it there are strata with no treated (or no untreated) patients to compare, and nonparametrically no amount of data fills the hole. Two kinds of failure are worth separating. A structural violation — some stratum can never be treated, a contraindication say — is a hole in the question itself, mendable only by restricting the target population or redefining the intervention. A practical violation is mere finite-sample sparsity, and how much it costs depends on the estimator: inverse-probability weighting is the most fragile, near-empty strata throwing up extreme weights and unstable estimates (Chapter 13), whereas an outcome model extrapolates across the gap — which rescues identification only by leaning on its functional form in a region the data barely constrain.10 Either way the assumption does not disappear; modelling merely trades a requirement on the data for a stronger one on the model.
  • Consistency. As above: the intervention is well defined and \(Y = Y(A)\). When “treated” is really a bundle of versions, what gets identified is the effect of the mixture of versions as it arose in this population — a quantity that need not carry over to a population where the mixture differs.11

When the above conditions hold, the counterfactual mean is identified — written entirely in terms of observable quantities: \[ E[Y(a)] \;=\; \sum_{l} E[Y \mid A=a,\, L=l]\; P(L=l). \] This is standardisation, and equivalently the g-formula: estimate the risk within each stratum of \(L\), then average over the population’s mix of \(L\). It is the whole of Chapter 13, and inverse-probability weighting is its mirror image. The formula also exposes the two assumptions: it needs every stratum to contain both treated and untreated patients (positivity), and it only equals the causal effect if \(L\) makes the groups exchangeable — which raises the question, which variables belong in \(L\)? Throw in too few and the bias survives; put in the wrong ones — a mediator, a collider — and you create fresh bias (see Chapter 12).

Conditional exchangeability is not magic; we can watch it work. The confounding gap above was 0.063 over the whole cohort. Compare the treated and untreated within strata of severity and most of it disappears:

But notice what licensed that picture: we read risk0 for the treated — a counterfactual column only the oracle supplies. A real study cannot draw this table, so conditional exchangeability is never something the data confirm; it is an assumption, defended by argument about which confounders were measured and overturned by the one that was not. The disciplined response to that irreducible doubt is a sensitivity analysis, which asks how strong an unmeasured confounder would have to be to explain the effect away; Chapter 20 develops the tools for it, the E-value among them.12

11.5 The do-operator, and the do-calculus

Potential outcomes have a twin notation, due to Pearl, that will briefly reappear when we turn to graphs. Writing \(P\!\left(Y \mid \mathrm{do}(A=a)\right)\) means the distribution of \(Y\) in the world where treatment is set to \(a\) by intervention — as opposed to \(P(Y \mid A=a)\), the distribution among those merely observed to take \(a\). The two differ when there is confounding. The bridge is an identity: \(E[Y(a)] = E\!\left[Y \mid \mathrm{do}(A=a)\right]\) — “the average potential outcome” and “the mean under intervention” are two names for one quantity. Potential outcomes and the do-operator are alternative notations, not rival theories; we use the first for stating estimands and the second when we reason on a diagram.

The do-notation leads to Pearl’s do-calculus, which is based on three rules that rewrite an expression containing \(\mathrm{do}(\cdot)\) into one without it — into ordinary conditional probabilities estimable from observational data — using only the structure of the causal graph (Chapter 12). The adjustment formula of the previous section, \(E[Y \mid \mathrm{do}(A=a)] = \sum_l E[Y \mid A=a, L=l]\,P(L=l)\), is the most familiar thing the rules deliver: the algebraic face of the back-door criterion. When no set of measured variables blocks the back-door paths, the same calculus can sometimes still identify the effect by another route — the front-door formula of Chapter 19 is the classic case. The calculus is complete, if a causal effect can be identified from the assumed graph and observational data by any means. Then the three rules can derive a formula for it. And if they cannot, then the causal effect is unidentifiable in principle without extra assumptions. This book mostly uses potential outcomes and the back-door criterion, which carry us through most applied problems. The do-calculus is the general engine behind them — invoked explicitly when shutting the back door (Chapter 19) and when we simulate interventions wholesale to form a posterior over the causal contrast (Chapter 29).

11.6 A hard case: causal questions about race

Race is the paradigm fixed attribute: there is no agreed action that sets a person’s race, so \(Y(\text{race})\) has no settled referent, and Holland’s slogan — no causation without manipulation — seems to rule the question out of court. Yet discrimination based on race is urgently in need of causal answers. The counterfactual framework’s response is to force a sharper question: intervene on what? Before taking that turn, a dissent is worth registering: a strand of epidemiology holds that the overall association of race or sex with an outcome is itself a legitimate target — a description of how much disadvantage tracks the category, and a natural starting point for disparities research, with the hunt for manipulable pathways coming second.13

Move the intervention from the person to the signal. A hiring manager does not respond to an applicant’s race in some metaphysical sense; she responds to what she perceives at the moment of decision — a name, a photograph, a voice, a postcode. Reframe the estimand as the effect of that perceived signal, assessed at the decision point — the move Greiner and Rubin formalise14 as the causal effect of a perceived immutable characteristic. The potential outcomes — the application that signals one race versus an otherwise identical application that signals another — are now well defined and manipulable.

The hiring case, done cleanly. This is the audit, or correspondence, study. Bertrand and Mullainathan sent employers résumés15 identical but for racially associated names (“Emily and Greg” versus “Lakisha and Jamal”) and measured call-backs. Because the signal is randomised over otherwise identical applications, consistency, no-interference and exchangeability hold by construction: this is a genuine randomised experiment, and the call-back gap is a clean causal estimate. Note carefully what the estimand is and is not. It is the effect of the perceived racial signal on this decision, at this stage, for the applications studied. It is not “the effect of being Black” over a life, and cannot be — but it answers a real question, squarely within the counterfactual paradigm.

Race as a bundle, not an atom. Generalising beyond a single signal, Sen and Wasow16 propose treating race as a bundle of sticks — a composite of manipulable constituents (name, dialect, skin tone, neighbourhood, signalled class) — and studying one stick at a time. “The effect of race” then dissolves into a family of sharper, answerable questions about components, and the analyst must say which.

Disparate treatment, disparate impact. The two notions map onto causal language. Disparate treatment — being handled differently because of race — is naturally a direct effect: the effect of the racial signal not operating through legitimate qualifications. Disparate impact — a neutral rule that falls unequally — is closer to a total effect, or a population disparity. This mapping (developed by VanderWeele and Robinson17) is useful, but it is also where the trouble resides, because a direct effect asks us to hold qualifications fixed while changing race.

The open problem. Kohler-Hausmann argues18 that the counterfactual recipe — change race, hold all else equal — misconstrues what race is. Race is not a detachable treatment applied to an otherwise-fixed person; it is constitutive of the very covariates one would hold constant — the name, the neighbourhood, the schooling, the accumulated qualifications, even how the applicant is perceived. There is then no coherent possible world in which this person is of a different race yet identical in everything race helped produce, so the “all else equal” contrast has no determinate meaning. Discrimination, on this view, is a property of a social and institutional process and of a constructed category, and calls for a constructivist account rather than a swap-and-compare counterfactual. The manipulationist reply — that the audit study still answers a well-posed question about identical applications — does not fully dissolve the objection, because “identical except the signal” is itself a stipulation that brackets all the upstream ways race shaped those résumés to begin with. (Similar critique has been made for sex, by Hu and Kohler-Hausmann.) This debate is not settled; it runs along disciplinary lines — economics and statistics largely at ease with the signal-manipulation estimand, much of law and sociology insisting it misses the structural phenomenon. The machine-learning notion of counterfactual fairness (Kusner et al.) — a decision is fair if it is unchanged in a world where the protected attribute and its descendants were different — bites the bullet19 by positing a full structural model with race as a root cause, and so inherits Kohler-Hausmann’s worry: it must commit to how race causes everything downstream.

Conclusion. The counterfactual framework does not forbid causal questions about race; it disciplines them. It insists you name the manipulable thing — a signal, a perception, a component, or a policy — and then report the effect of that, in a population, rather than an undefined “effect of race”. Sometimes the appropriate quantity is descriptive — how much of a disparity is accounted for by measured factors (a decomposition), not a causal effect at all — and saying so plainly is part of the discipline. The one move the counterfactual framework rules out without exception is regressing the outcome on race alongside a grab-bag of controls and reading the race coefficient as “the effect of race”. That is the effect of no defined intervention, adjusted by whatever happened to enter the model — the Table 2 fallacy (Chapter 22).

11.7 The question, and the road from here

The framework lets us write the master-cohort question without ambiguity. Does the drug prevent stroke? becomes: estimate \(\text{ATE} = E[Y(1)] - E[Y(0)]\) (or its risk ratio), the contrast of two counterfactual worlds. We have seen that the crude comparison estimates the wrong thing, that the error is a specific confounding term, and that three assumptions — conditional exchangeability, positivity, consistency — would license the g-formula to recover the right one. What remains is a division of labour the rest of Part III carries out:

  • Which variables make the groups exchangeable, and which would wreck the estimate if included — Chapter 12 (causal diagrams) answers this by reading the structure off a graph.
  • How to compute \(E[Y(a)]\) from those variables — Chapter 13 does it by hand, with standardisation and weighting, and Chapter 29 does it with a fitted Bayesian model and full posterior uncertainty.

The potential-outcome notation is what makes it possible by stating the target clearly.

A word of caution, stated in full in Chapter 5. This counterfactual apparatus is an operational convenience for reasoning about interventions, not a theory of physical or biological causation, and it is only as meaningful as the intervention it names. For a manipulable treatment, give the drug or not, \(Y(a)\) is crisp. For an attribute that no experiment could assign, \(Y(a)\) can be ill defined, because there is no agreed action that sets it, and different imagined interventions yield different effects. The first question of a causal analysis is therefore not “what is the effect?” but “the effect of which intervention?”; the previous section worked through the hardest case.

The next chapter, Chapter 12, takes the first of these tasks.

11.8 Key points

  • Each unit has two potential outcomes, \(Y(1)\) and \(Y(0)\); their contrast is the individual effect, and only one is ever observed (the fundamental problem).
  • Consistency (\(Y = Y(A)\)) and no interference tie the notation to data and require a well-defined intervention.
  • The targets are estimands chosen in advance — ATE, ATT, ATU — reported on a chosen scale. The book reports the risk difference and risk ratio; the odds ratio and hazard ratio are non-collapsible and conditional on model or on survival, and make poor causal summaries. The number needed to treat is the risk difference turned round and inherits its baseline risk and time horizon.
  • When interference or multiple versions of treatment break SUTVA, the estimand is redefined rather than abandoned: spillover, total and overall effects under partial interference, or the effect of a named version.
  • The crude contrast equals the effect plus a confounding term, \(E[Y(0)\mid A{=}1] - E[Y(0)\mid A{=}0]\).
  • An effect is identified under conditional exchangeability, positivity, and consistency, via standardisation / the g-formula — but the formula cannot tell you which variables to condition on (Chapter 12) or how to estimate it (Chapter 13, Chapter 29), and exchangeability itself is untestable, to be probed by sensitivity analysis (the E-value, Chapter 20).
  • \(E[Y(a)] = E[Y\mid \mathrm{do}(A=a)]\) links potential outcomes to Pearl’s do-operator; the do-calculus is the complete engine for deciding identifiability from a graph, used in Chapter 19 and Chapter 29.
  • For a fixed attribute like race, “the effect of race” is an undefined intervention, but the framework licenses sharper estimands — the effect of a perceived signal (audit studies), of a component (race as a bundle of sticks), or of a policy. Whether such reframing captures structural discrimination is a genuine open problem (Kohler-Hausmann).

11.9 Exercises

  1. Decompose the bias. Verify numerically the identity \(E[Y\mid A{=}1]-E[Y\mid A{=}0] = \text{ATT} + \{E[Y(0)\mid A{=}1]-E[Y(0)\mid A{=}0]\}\) on the cohort (use the oracle for the counterfactual term). By how much does the confounding term have to fall before the crude contrast turns protective?

  2. Positivity, by hand. Find a stratum of severity (say, the top 1%) and check the proportion treated. What happens to the standardisation formula if some stratum has no untreated patients? Relate your answer to why extreme confounding and positivity violations travel together.

  3. Randomise it. Modify the generator so that drug is assigned by a fair coin independent of severity. Recompute the crude contrast and the ATE. Why does marginal exchangeability now hold, and why does the confounding term vanish?

  4. Conceptual: which intervention? A colleague wants “the causal effect of obesity on stroke”. Using consistency and the well-defined-intervention idea, explain why this estimand is ambiguous, and rewrite it as a question with a manipulable treatment.

  5. do and see. In your own words, give an example of two quantities \(P(Y\mid A=a)\) and \(P(Y\mid \mathrm{do}(A=a))\) that are equal, and two that are not, and say what feature of the cohort makes the difference.

  6. Make the question answerable. A team wants “the causal effect of race on hiring.”

    1. Reframe it as the effect of a manipulable signal and sketch an audit study that would estimate it, stating precisely what the estimand does and does not cover.
    2. Explain Kohler-Hausmann’s objection that “change race, hold qualifications fixed” is incoherent, and argue whether your audit estimand escapes it.
