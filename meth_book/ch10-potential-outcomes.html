11  The potential-outcomes framework

Chapter 4 made the case that a causal claim is a claim about parallel worlds — what would happen to a particular patient if treated, against what would happen to the same patient if not — and that the randomised experiment is the machine that lets us compare those worlds fairly. This chapter turns that picture into notation that says, which statistical quantity a study is trying to estimate, and what would have to be true for the ordinary machinery of averages and regressions to estimate it. Thus, “the effect of the drug” becomes a quantity we can define, before we try to compute it.

11.1 A second outcome that never happens

Fix a patient and a binary treatment — our blood-pressure drug, \(A = 1\) for treated, \(A = 0\) for not. Write \(Y(1)\) for the stroke outcome this patient would have under treatment and \(Y(0)\) for the outcome the same patient would have without it. These hypotheticals are the potential outcomes: two values attached to one person, only one of which the world will ever reveal. The patient’s individual causal effect is the contrast \(Y(1) - Y(0)\) — which is, in general, unknowable, because we see the patient under one treatment only. The other potential outcome is a counterfactual: real enough to define, impossible to observe. This is Holland’s fundamental problem of causal inference.

Two assumptions glue this notation to data. The first is consistency: the outcome we actually observe is the potential outcome for the treatment actually received, \(Y = Y(A)\). It sounds trivial, but is not — it presumes the intervention is well defined, that “treated” refers to one thing and not to a bundle of different things with different effects (the dose, route, adherence). The second is no interference (part of SUTVA): one patient’s treatment does not change another’s outcome, so \(Y_i(a)\) depends on patient \(i\)’s own treatment alone. Both can fail — interference is the rule in vaccine and network studies — and when they do, the contrast \(Y(1)-Y(0)\) is no longer the right target and has to be redefined; how depends on which part broke (see below).

SUTVA: what lets us write \(Y_i(a)\) at all

The notation \(Y_i(a)\) — one potential outcome per unit per treatment level — comes from the Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption (SUTVA, Rubin’s term). It has two parts, both of which must hold for the expression even to make sense.

  • No interference. Unit \(i\)’s potential outcomes depend only on \(i\)’s own treatment, so we may write \(Y_i(a)\) rather than \(Y_i(a_1, a_2, \dots)\). This fails whenever one unit’s treatment can influence another — vaccines and herd immunity, infectious disease, treated partners in a household, classroom- or village-level assignment, market-wide effects. Then each unit has a separate potential outcome for every configuration of everyone’s treatment, and the tidy contrast \(Y(1) - Y(0)\) is not even defined.
  • One version of treatment (no hidden variations). “Treated” must name a single, well-specified intervention. If \(A = 1\) secretly bundles many versions with different effects — different doses, formulations, surgeons, or simply different ways of “being treated” — then \(Y(1)\) is an average over an unspecified mixture and changes meaning with it. This is the same demand as consistency: \(Y = Y(A)\) is only informative when \(A = 1\) refers to one thing.

SUTVA is therefore not a box to tick at the end of an analysis; it is the licence to write the estimand in the first place. When meeting it is doubtful, one should sharpen the intervention (a specific dose and schedule) or model the interference explicitly (partial-interference or network designs), rather than press on with an ambiguous \(Y(a)\). It is also why an “effect” of a fixed attribute — the “effect of sex”, say, or “of race” — strains the framework: there is no agreed intervention that sets such a variable, so its potential outcome has no settled referent (contrast a modifiable trait like obesity, where the trouble is the opposite — many different ways one could intervene, with different effects). This is the well-defined-intervention point we return to at the end of the chapter.

How \(Y(1)-Y(0)\) is redefined depends on which half of SUTVA gave way

If the failure is interference, a unit’s outcome depends on the whole pattern of treatment, then the potential outcome gains arguments — \(Y_i(\mathbf{a})\), a function of the entire assignment vector \(\mathbf{a} = (a_1, \dots, a_n)\) rather than of \(a_i\) alone. That is up to \(2^n\) potential outcomes per person instead of two, which is hopeless without structure, so one must impose some. Partial interference assumes units sit in groups — households, classrooms, villages — with spillover only within a group; an exposure mapping assumes further that a unit’s outcome depends on the rest only through a low-dimensional summary, typically the coverage \(c\) (the fraction treated in its group), which collapses the outcome to \(Y_i(a_i, c)\) in own-treatment \(a_i\) and coverage \(c\). The single contrast then splits into a family of well-defined effects:

  • a direct effect — switch my own treatment, holding the group’s coverage fixed;
  • an indirect, or spillover, effect — change the group’s coverage, holding my own treatment fixed (the protection an unvaccinated person gains from a vaccinated community);
  • a total effect — the two together, treated-under-high-coverage versus untreated-under-low; and
  • an overall effect — compare two whole allocation programmes, say 0% against 70% coverage, averaged over everyone.

Which of these is “the” effect?: “should this patient be vaccinated?” asks for the direct effect; “should we fund a 70%-coverage programme?” asks for the overall effect; and — herd immunity being the point of vaccination — the two can diverge sharply. These effects are not mere definitions: once the exposure structure is recorded — who was treated directly, and who only through a treated network member — they are identifiable and routinely estimated in network-randomised trials with standard correlated-data methods.1

If instead the failure is multiple versions of treatment, redefinition means naming the version. The estimand becomes the effect of one specified intervention — a dose, a schedule, a surgical technique — or, when the versions genuinely cannot be pinned down, the effect of a stated distribution over them (a “modified treatment policy” that assigns versions as they arise in practice). Either way the manoeuvre is the same in spirit: replace a vague switch with a contrast of interventions sharp enough that \(Y(\cdot)\) has a single value again — which is, in the end, the lesson of the well-defined-intervention caveat at the close of this chapter.

In real life the counterfactual column is missing. In our simulated cohort it is not: we could compute each patient’s risk under both treatments. It is worth looking at the thing we almost never get to see.

11.2 The estimands: average effects and their scales

As individual effects are out of reach, we target averages. The average treatment effect is the contrast of potential outcomes over the whole population, \[ \text{ATE} \;=\; E[Y(1)] - E[Y(0)], \] the stroke risk if everyone were treated against the risk if no one were. A close relative is the average treatment effect in the treated, \(\text{ATT} = E[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid A = 1]\), the effect among the patients who actually got the drug — a different and often more policy-relevant quantity whenever the effect varies across people. Its counterpart, the average treatment effect in the untreated, \(\text{ATU} = E[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid A = 0]\), asks what the drug would do for those who did not receive it. This is the estimand behind a decision to extend treatment to them, as against ATT’s question of whether to withdraw it from those who have it. The three are tied by the share treated, \(\text{ATE} = P(A{=}1)\,\text{ATT} + P(A{=}0)\,\text{ATU}\), so once the effect varies across people they part company — and the gap between ATT and ATU is itself a causal signal: it says whether treatment reached the people it helps most. These are estimands: precise targets chosen before estimation, not whatever a model happens to spit out.

A contrast also needs a scale, the choice of which is substantive. The same two risks \(E[Y(1)]\) and \(E[Y(0)]\) can be reported as a risk difference \(E[Y(1)] - E[Y(0)]\), a risk ratio \(E[Y(1)]/E[Y(0)]\), or an odds ratio. They answer different questions — absolute public-health burden versus relative effect — and they are not interchangeable; the odds ratio in particular is non-collapsible, so it can shift when you add covariates even with no confounding (Chapter 24). Our oracle reports all of them.

Odds ratios and hazard ratios make poor causal estimands

Of the scales above, the risk difference and risk ratio are collapsible and are on the probability scale a patient or policy-maker actually reasons in. The odds ratio and the hazard ratio are neither, and both should be avoided as causal summaries.

Non-collapsibility. A measure is collapsible when the population (marginal) value is a weighted average of the stratum-specific ones; the risk difference and risk ratio are, while the odds ratio is not. The consequence is unsettling: with no confounding whatsoever, the odds ratio adjusted for a covariate differs from the unadjusted one — adding a risk factor that only causally affects the outcome, but has no causal link to the treatment, to a logistic model does not bias the causal interpretation of RR and RD (while narrowing the CI-s), while it drives the OR away from 1. So the OR depends on which covariates are included in the model for reasons that have nothing to do with bias, two correctly specified analyses can report different ORs, and you cannot read a population-average effect off a logistic coefficient (Chapter 24). This is not confounding but it makes the OR treacherous to interpret and to compare across studies.

Under a logistic risk model the mechanism is explicit. If \(\operatorname{logit} P(Y{=}1 \mid A, L) = \alpha + \beta A + \gamma L\), then \(\beta\) is the log-odds-ratio within a level of \(L\) — a conditional effect. The marginal log-odds-ratio, obtained by averaging the fitted risks over the distribution of \(L\) and only then forming the odds ratio, is smaller in magnitude than \(\beta\) whenever \(\gamma \neq 0\), because the logit is nonlinear and the odds ratio of an average is not the average of the odds ratios. The gap is present even when \(L\) is independent of \(A\), so it is not confounding: the conditional and marginal odds ratios are distinct estimands, and adding a pure outcome predictor to the model increases the magnitude of \(\beta\) while leaving the marginal odds ratio unchanged. A logistic coefficient is therefore specific to its adjustment set and is not comparable across models that condition on different covariates. To obtain a marginal odds ratio from a logistic fit, average the predicted risks over the covariate distribution and form the contrast on the risk scale (standardisation, Chapter 12) rather than exponentiating \(\beta\).2

The hazard ratio is non-collapsible in the same way.

Odds (P/(1 - P)) are not probabilities (P). An odds ratio ((P1/(1 - P1))/(P2/(1 - P2)) approximates the risk ratio (P1/P2) only when the outcome is rare. For purely mathematical reasons, an OR always exaggerates the effect in comparison to RR; for a common outcome it exaggerates a lot: against a \(30\%\) baseline risk, an OR of \(2.0\) corresponds to a just \(46\%\) increase, and to a RR of about \(1.5\). People routinely misread an OR as an RR and so overstate the effect. Odds answer no question a clinician or a patient actually asks; risks do.

Hazard ratios hide a selection effect. A hazard at time \(t\) is a rate among those still event-free at \(t\). Once treatment has begun to act, the survivors in the two arms are no longer comparable — treatment has selectively kept some high-risk people event-free, so the treated risk set is frailer than the control risk set. The hazard ratio therefore conditions on a post-treatment variable (survival), blending the genuine effect with a time-varying depletion-of-susceptibles selection.3 That is why a constant hazard ratio need not reflect a constant individual effect, and why even a flawless randomised trial can report a hazard ratio with no clean causal interpretation. The proportional-hazards assumption that compresses HR to a single number rarely holds either.

What to report instead. Prefer estimands that are collapsible, on the probability scale, and tied to an intervention: the risk difference and risk ratio at a stated time horizon, survival or cumulative-incidence differences, or the restricted mean survival time. This book targets the ATE on the risk-difference and risk-ratio scales throughout.

Show code 
estimands <- cohort |>
  summarise(
    crude_RD = mean(stroke[drug == 1]) - mean(stroke[drug == 0]),
    crude_RR = mean(stroke[drug == 1]) / mean(stroke[drug == 0]),

The true ATE is protective on every scale — a risk ratio of 0.6, about 2.7 strokes prevented per hundred treated. The crude contrast, the thing a naive analysis reports, says the opposite: a risk ratio of 1.57 — the drug looks harmful. Both numbers are computed from the same cohort. Only one is the effect.

The treated and the untreated are not given the same effect, and how they differ is worth a look. Compare the ATT and the ATU on both scales:

Show code 
att_atu <- cohort |>
  group_by(drug) |>
  summarise(RD = mean(risk1 - risk0),                 # E[Y(1) - Y(0)] within the arm
            RR = mean(risk1) / mean(risk0), .groups = "drop")

att <- att_atu |> filter(drug == 1)                   # treated   -> ATT
atu <- att_atu |> filter(drug == 0)                   # untreated -> ATU

att_atu |>
  transmute(group = if_else(drug == 1, "treated (ATT)", "untreated (ATU)"), RD, RR)
# A tibble: 2 x 3
  group                RD    RR
  <chr>             <dbl> <dbl>
1 untreated (ATU) -0.0122 0.635
2 treated (ATT)   -0.0388 0.591

On the risk-difference scale the drug averts far more among the treated (ATT \(\approx\) -0.039) than it would among the untreated (ATU \(\approx\) -0.012) — nearly three times the absolute benefit. Yet on the risk-ratio scale the two are all but identical (0.591 versus 0.635). Both facts hold because the drug cuts risk by a near-constant fraction, and the treated are the sicker patients, with much higher baseline risk, so the same relative cut delivers a larger absolute reduction. The confounding-by-indication that ruins the crude comparison thus has a flip side — treatment was steered toward exactly those with the most absolute risk to lose. Whether “the effect” is larger in the treated is therefore not a fact about the drug but about the scale you read it on (Chapter 16). ATT, ATU and ATE coincide only when the effect is homogeneous on that scale. That the ratio scale is so often the one on which effects are near-homogeneous — genuine interaction being comparatively rare multiplicatively — is a broad empirical regularity;4 the catch is that a constant risk ratio forces a risk difference that climbs with baseline risk, so the more portable scale is not the one a public-health decision actually needs.

The absolute effect can be easier to grasp as a number needed to treat (NNT): the number of patients one must treat, to prevent a single event over a stated period. It is the reciprocal of the absolute risk difference, \(\text{NNT} = 1/|\text{RD}|\), so it carries the causal content of the RD. An NNT built from the ATE is a population causal quantity, while an NNT built from the crude risk difference is every bit as confounded as that difference. From the cohort’s true ATE, \(\text{NNT} \approx\) 38: treat about 40 patients to avert one stroke. In our example, the NNT is far smaller in the high-risk treated (NNT \(\approx\) 26) than in the lower-risk untreated (NNT \(\approx\) 82), even though the relative effect is identical in the two groups. An NNT is not a property of the drug alone but of the drug applied to a particular baseline risk over a particular time horizon, and it must be reported with both. Turned around, a harmful contrast yields a number needed to harm: taken at face value, this cohort’s crude comparison would pin an NNH of about 54 on a drug that in truth prevents strokes.

11.3 Why the crude contrast is not the effect

The crude contrast estimates the association \(E[Y \mid A=1] - E[Y \mid A=0]\), and an association is a comparison of different patients — those who took the drug versus those who did not. The causal effect is a comparison of the same patients across worlds. The gap between them has an exact algebraic form. Among the treated, what we observe is \(E[Y \mid A=1] = E[Y(1) \mid A=1]\) by consistency; among the untreated, \(E[Y \mid A=0] = E[Y(0) \mid A=0]\). Add and subtract the missing counterfactual \(E[Y(0) \mid A=1]\) — the risk the treated would have run untreated — and the association splits cleanly: \[ \underbrace{E[Y\mid A{=}1] - E[Y\mid A{=}0]}_{\text{association}} =\underbrace{E[Y(1)\mid A{=}1]-E[Y(0)\mid A{=}1]}_{\text{ATT: a real effect}} +\underbrace{E[Y(0)\mid A{=}1]-E[Y(0)\mid A{=}0]}_{\text{confounding bias}} . \] The second term is the whole problem. It compares the untreated risk of the treated with the untreated risk of the untreated — two groups that, if treatment was assigned for a reason, were never alike to begin with. Normally it is invisible, because \(E[Y(0)\mid A=1]\) is counterfactual. The oracle shows it to us.

Show code 
EY0 <- cohort |>
  group_by(drug) |>
  summarise(EY0 = mean(risk0), .groups = "drop")      # untreated risk E[Y(0)] in each arm

EY0_treated   <- EY0 |> filter(drug == 1) |> pull(EY0)   # E[Y(0) | A=1]  -- counterfactual
EY0_untreated <- EY0 |> filter(drug == 0) |> pull(EY0)   # E[Y(0) | A=0]  -- observable

tibble(`E[Y(0)|treated]`   = EY0_treated,
       `E[Y(0)|untreated]` = EY0_untreated,
       confounding_gap     = EY0_treated - EY0_untreated)
# A tibble: 1 x 3
  `E[Y(0)|treated]` `E[Y(0)|untreated]` confounding_gap
              <dbl>               <dbl>           <dbl>
1            0.0950              0.0335          0.0615

The treated, had they gone untreated, would have had a stroke risk of 0.095 — nearly three times the 0.034 of those actually untreated. The treated were sicker all along (the drug goes to high-severity patients), so the confounding term is large and positive, 0.061, and it swamps the genuinely protective ATT, turning a beneficial drug into an apparently harmful one. This is confounding by indication, seen for once with the counterfactual laid bare rather than inferred.

11.4 What it takes to identify an effect

If the bias is the term \(E[Y(0)\mid A=1] - E[Y(0)\mid A=0]\), the cure is any condition that erases it.

  • Exchangeability. The treated and untreated groups are comparable: \(Y(a) \perp A\). In other words, if we had, contrafactually, given the treatment to the placebo group and vice versa, the causal estimate would not have changed. Marginally this is a strong demand, met by design in RCTs by randomisation (Chapter 4). In observational studies we aim for the weaker conditional exchangeability, \(Y(a) \perp A \mid L\): comparability within levels of a set of covariates \(L\). This means that for exchangeability we need to have the full adjustment set, measured correctly, and modelled correctly. The next chapter shows how to think of this with the help of graphs (DAGs).
  • Positivity. Every kind of patient could, with some non-zero probability, have received either treatment: \(0 < P(A = a \mid L) < 1\) for every \(L\) that occurs. Without it there are strata with no treated (or no untreated) patients to compare, and nonparametrically no amount of data fills the hole. Two kinds of failure are worth separating. A structural violation — some stratum can never be treated, a contraindication say — is a hole in the question itself, mendable only by restricting the target population or redefining the intervention. A practical violation is mere finite-sample sparsity, and how much it costs depends on the estimator: inverse-probability weighting is the most fragile, near-empty strata throwing up extreme weights and unstable estimates (Chapter 12), whereas an outcome model extrapolates across the gap — which rescues identification only by leaning on its functional form in a region the data barely constrain.5 Either way the assumption does not disappear; modelling merely trades a requirement on the data for a stronger one on the model.
  • Consistency. As above: the intervention is well defined and \(Y = Y(A)\). When “treated” is really a bundle of versions, what gets identified is the effect of the mixture of versions as it arose in this population — a quantity that need not carry over to a population where the mixture differs.6

When the above conditions hold, the counterfactual mean is identified — written entirely in terms of observable quantities: \[ E[Y(a)] \;=\; \sum_{l} E[Y \mid A=a,\, L=l]\; P(L=l). \] This is standardisation, and equivalently the g-formula: estimate the risk within each stratum of \(L\), then average over the population’s mix of \(L\). It is the whole of Chapter 12, and inverse-probability weighting is its mirror image. The formula also exposes the two assumptions: it needs every stratum to contain both treated and untreated patients (positivity), and it only equals the causal effect if \(L\) makes the groups exchangeable — which raises the question, which variables belong in \(L\)? Throw in too few and the bias survives; put in the wrong ones — a mediator, a collider — and you create fresh bias(see Chapter 11).

Conditional exchangeability is not magic; we can watch it work. The confounding gap above was 0.061 over the whole cohort. Compare the treated and untreated within strata of severity and most of it disappears:

Show code 
cohort |>
  mutate(severity_q = cut(severity, quantile(severity, seq(0, 1, 0.2)),
                          include.lowest = TRUE)) |>
  group_by(severity_q) |>
  summarise(`E[Y(0)|treated]`   = mean(risk0[drug == 1]),
            `E[Y(0)|untreated]` = mean(risk0[drug == 0]),
            gap = `E[Y(0)|treated]` - `E[Y(0)|untreated]`, .groups = "drop") |>
  kbl(digits = 3,
      caption = "Within strata of severity the untreated-risk gap nearly closes. The residue is the other measured confounders (age, diabetes).") |>
  kable_styling(full_width = FALSE)

Inside a severity band the treated and untreated would have run nearly the same risk untreated: that is conditional exchangeability appearing in the data. The small remaining gap is the part of the confounding carried by age and diabetes, which a full adjustment set would also have to absorb — the subject of the next two chapters.

But notice what licensed that picture: we read risk0 for the treated — a counterfactual column only the oracle supplies. A real study cannot draw this table, so conditional exchangeability is never something the data confirm; it is an assumption, defended by argument about which confounders were measured and overturned by the one that was not. The disciplined response to that irreducible doubt is a sensitivity analysis — asking how strong an unmeasured confounder would have to be to explain the effect away. The E-value makes the question concrete: the minimum association such a confounder would need with both treatment and outcome to account for the observed effect.7 Chapter 19 takes this up in full.

11.5 The do-operator, and the do-calculus

Potential outcomes have a twin notation, due to Pearl, that will briefly reappear when we turn to graphs. Writing \(P\!\left(Y \mid \mathrm{do}(A=a)\right)\) means the distribution of \(Y\) in the world where treatment is set to \(a\) by intervention — as opposed to \(P(Y \mid A=a)\), the distribution among those merely observed to take \(a\). The two differ when there is confounding. The bridge is an identity: \(E[Y(a)] = E\!\left[Y \mid \mathrm{do}(A=a)\right]\) — “the average potential outcome” and “the mean under intervention” are two names for one quantity. Potential outcomes and the do-operator are alternative notations, not rival theories; we use the first for stating estimands and the second when we reason on a diagram.

The do-notation leads to Pearl’s do-calculus, which is based on three rules that rewrite an expression containing \(\mathrm{do}(\cdot)\) into one without it — into ordinary conditional probabilities estimable from observational data — using only the structure of the causal graph (Chapter 11). The adjustment formula of the previous section, \(E[Y \mid \mathrm{do}(A=a)] = \sum_l E[Y \mid A=a, L=l]\,P(L=l)\), is the most familiar thing the rules deliver: the algebraic face of the backdoor criterion. When no set of measured variables blocks the back doors, the same calculus can sometimes still identify the effect by another route — the front-door formula of Chapter 18 is the classic case. The calculus is complete, if a causal effect can be identified from the assumed graph and observational data by any means. Then the three rules can derive a formula for it. And if they cannot, then the causal effect is unidentifiable in principe without extra assumptions. This book mostly uses potential outcomes and the backdoor criterion, which carry us through most applied problems. The do-calculus is the general engine behind them — invoked explicitly when sutting the back door (Chapter 18) and when we simulate interventions wholesale to form a posterior over the causal contrast (Chapter 25).

11.6 The question, and the road from here

The framework lets us write the master-cohort question without ambiguity. Does the drug prevent stroke? becomes: estimate \(\text{ATE} = E[Y(1)] - E[Y(0)]\) (or its risk ratio), the contrast of two counterfactual worlds. We have seen that the crude comparison estimates the wrong thing, that the error is a specific confounding term, and that three assumptions — conditional exchangeability, positivity, consistency — would license the g-formula to recover the right one. What remains is a division of labour the rest of Part III carries out:

  • Which variables make the groups exchangeable, and which would wreck the estimate if included — Chapter 11 (causal diagrams) answers this by reading the structure off a graph.
  • How to compute \(E[Y(a)]\) from those variables — Chapter 12 does it by hand, with standardisation and weighting, and Chapter 28 does it with a fitted Bayesian model and full posterior uncertainty.

The potential-outcome notation is what makes it possible by stating the target clearly.

A word of caution. This counterfactual apparatus is an operational convenience for reasoning about interventions, not a theory of physical or biological causation, and it is only as meaningful as the intervention it names. For a manipulable treatment — give the drug or not — \(Y(a)\) is crisp. For an attribute that no experiment could assign — the effect of a fixed characteristic, say — “\(Y(a)\)” can be ill defined, because there is no agreed action that sets it, and different imagined interventions yield different effects. When the intervention is vague, the estimand is vague, so that the first question of a causal analysis is not “what is the effect?”, but “the effect of which intervention, in which population?” No case presses that question harder than race.

11.7 A hard case: causal questions about race

Race is the paradigm fixed attribute: there is no agreed action that sets a person’s race, so \(Y(\text{race})\) has no settled referent, and Holland’s slogan — no causation without manipulation — seems to rule the question out of court. Yet discrimination based on race is urgently in need of causal answers. The counterfactual framework’s response is to force a sharper question: intervene on what? Before taking that turn, a dissent is worth registering: a strand of epidemiology holds that the overall association of race or sex with an outcome is itself a legitimate target — a description of how much disadvantage tracks the category, and a natural starting point for disparities research, with the hunt for manipulable pathways coming second.8

Move the intervention from the person to the signal. A hiring manager does not respond to an applicant’s race in some metaphysical sense; she responds to what she perceives at the moment of decision — a name, a photograph, a voice, a postcode. Reframe the estimand as the effect of that perceived signal, assessed at the decision point — the move Greiner and Rubin formalise as the causal effect of a perceived immutable characteristic. The potential outcomes — the application that signals one race versus an otherwise identical application that signals another — are now well defined and manipulable.

The hiring case, done cleanly. This is the audit, or correspondence, study. Bertrand and Mullainathan sent employers résumés identical but for racially associated names (“Emily and Greg” versus “Lakisha and Jamal”) and measured call-backs. Because the signal is randomised over otherwise identical applications, consistency, no-interference and exchangeability hold by construction: this is a genuine randomised experiment, and the call-back gap is a clean causal estimate. Note carefully what the estimand is and is not. It is the effect of the perceived racial signal on this decision, at this stage, for the applications studied. It is not “the effect of being Black” over a life, and cannot be — but it answers a real question, squarely within the counterfactual paradigm.

Race as a bundle, not an atom. Generalising beyond a single signal, Sen and Wasow propose treating race as a bundle of sticks — a composite of manipulable constituents (name, dialect, skin tone, neighbourhood, signalled class) — and studying one stick at a time. “The effect of race” then dissolves into a family of sharper, answerable questions about components, and the analyst must say which.

Disparate treatment, disparate impact. The two notions map onto causal language. Disparate treatment — being handled differently because of race — is naturally a direct effect: the effect of the racial signal not operating through legitimate qualifications. Disparate impact — a neutral rule that falls unequally — is closer to a total effect, or a population disparity. This mapping (developed by VanderWeele and Robinson) is useful, but it is also where the trouble resides, because a direct effect asks us to hold qualifications fixed while changing race.

The open problem Kohler-Hausmann argues that the counterfactual recipe — change race, hold all else equal — misconstrues what race is. Race is not a detachable treatment applied to an otherwise-fixed person; it is constitutive of the very covariates one would hold constant — the name, the neighbourhood, the schooling, the accumulated qualifications, even how the applicant is perceived. There is then no coherent possible world in which this person is of a different race yet identical in everything race helped produce, so the “all else equal” contrast has no determinate meaning. Discrimination, on this view, is a property of a social and institutional process and of a constructed category, and calls for a constructivist account rather than a swap-and-compare counterfactual. The manipulationist reply — that the audit study still answers a well-posed question about identical applications — does not fully dissolve the objection, because “identical except the signal” is itself a stipulation that brackets all the upstream ways race shaped those résumés to begin with. (Similar critique has been made for sex, by Hu and Kohler-Hausmann.) This debate is not settled; it runs along disciplinary lines — economics and statistics largely at ease with the signal-manipulation estimand, much of law and sociology insisting it misses the structural phenomenon. The machine-learning notion of counterfactual fairness (Kusner et al.) — a decision is fair if it is unchanged in a world where the protected attribute and its descendants were different — bites the bullet by positing a full structural model with race as a root cause, and so inherits Kohler-Hausmann’s worry: it must commit to how race causes everything downstream.

Conclusion The counterfactual framework does not forbid causal questions about race; it disciplines them. It insists you name the manipulable thing — a signal, a perception, a component, or a policy — and then report the effect of that, in a population, rather than an undefined “effect of race”. Sometimes the appropriate quantity is descriptive — how much of a disparity is accounted for by measured factors (a decomposition), not a causal effect at all — and saying so plainly is part of the discipline. The one move the counterfactual framework rules out without exception is regressing the outcome on race alongside a grab-bag of controls and reading the race coefficient as “the effect of race”. That is the effect of no defined intervention, adjusted by whatever happened to enter the model — the Table-2 fallacy (Chapter 26).

11.8 Key points

  • Each unit has two potential outcomes, \(Y(1)\) and \(Y(0)\); their contrast is the individual effect, and only one is ever observed (the fundamental problem).
  • Consistency (\(Y = Y(A)\)) and no interference tie the notation to data and require a well-defined intervention.
  • The targets are estimands chosen in advance — ATE, ATT, ATU — reported on a chosen scale (risk difference, risk ratio, odds ratio).
  • The crude contrast equals the effect plus a confounding term, \(E[Y(0)\mid A{=}1] - E[Y(0)\mid A{=}0]\).
  • An effect is identified under conditional exchangeability, positivity, and consistency, via standardisation / the g-formula — but the formula cannot tell you which variables to condition on (Chapter 11) or how to estimate it (Chapters 12, 24), and exchangeability itself is untestable, to be probed by sensitivity analysis (the E-value, Chapter 19).
  • \(E[Y(a)] = E[Y\mid \mathrm{do}(A=a)]\) links potential outcomes to Pearl’s do-operator; the do-calculus is the complete engine for deciding identifiability from a graph, used in Chapters 18 and 24.
  • For a fixed attribute like race, “the effect of race” is an undefined intervention, but the framework licenses sharper estimands — the effect of a perceived signal (audit studies), of a component (race as a bundle of sticks), or of a policy. Whether such reframing captures structural discrimination is a genuine open problem (Kohler-Hausmann).

11.9 Exercises

  1. Decompose the bias. Verify numerically the identity \(E[Y\mid A{=}1]-E[Y\mid A{=}0] = \text{ATT} + \{E[Y(0)\mid A{=}1]-E[Y(0)\mid A{=}0]\}\) on the cohort (use the oracle for the counterfactual term). By how much does the confounding term have to fall before the crude contrast turns protective?

  2. Positivity, by hand. Find a stratum of severity (say, the top 1%) and check the proportion treated. What happens to the standardisation formula if some stratum has no untreated patients? Relate your answer to why extreme confounding and positivity violations travel together.

  3. Randomise it. Modify the generator so that drug is assigned by a fair coin independent of severity. Recompute the crude contrast and the ATE. Why does marginal exchangeability now hold, and why does the confounding term vanish?

  4. Conceptual: which intervention? A colleague wants “the causal effect of obesity on stroke”. Using consistency and the well-defined-intervention idea, explain why this estimand is ambiguous, and rewrite it as a question with a manipulable treatment.

  5. do and see. In your own words, give an example of two quantities \(P(Y\mid A=a)\) and \(P(Y\mid \mathrm{do}(A=a))\) that are equal, and two that are not, and say what feature of the cohort makes the difference.

  6. Make the question answerable. A team wants “the causal effect of race on hiring.”

    1. Reframe it as the effect of a manipulable signal and sketch an audit study that would estimate it, stating precisely what the estimand does and does not cover.
    2. Explain Kohler-Hausmann’s objection that “change race, hold qualifications fixed” is incoherent, and argue whether your audit estimand escapes it.
