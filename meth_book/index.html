1 A statement of purpose

This book is focused on causal modelling in epidemiology, via the Bayesian route. Throughout, I try to show connections between scientific, statistical and causal thinking, which are bound together by probability theory, interpreted as logic.

The general idea behind this lengthy exposition is to demolish walls between scientific thinking, statistical thinking and rational thinking in the real world. However, rationality is here taken in a narrow, technical sense, where we can norm it and check its correctness by mathematical methods. Normatively rational thinking operates in the mental space where assertions have truth value – i.e. the statement (proposition) you made can be either true or false, and nothing in between. Here we have the use of a mathematical framework that tells how to use the evidence to update your beliefs about the world, be it the real world, or the rather more abstract scientific world. As we will see, the truth value requirement requirement can be tricky even in the highly technical domain of counterfactual causal modelling, let alone in everyday decisions on where to go for vacation of whether to eat this piece of chocolate cake. It turns out that we will aquire a fancy toolbox for making data-driven inferences about practically everything, from said cake to medical treatments, while in most practical cases the tools work only so-so, because how we talk about the world cannot be precisely encoded into mathematically tractable language. In the end, we have to be content with doing the best job we can, using tools that are extremely well understood and work optimally under ideal conditions, while conceding that in actual scientific work, or in real life, this might not be good enough in any given use case.

The moral of this is twofold. Firstly, whatever we do in terms of fancy statistics and modelling, we should always try to understand, why we do it the way we do it. Any math that comes up in our work is in the service of some non-mathematical scientific argument, and if we cannot understand this relationship, then the math is spurious and should be considered a window-dressing to our limitations as scientists and human beings. The second moral is simple: show humility! After perusing this book, you should take it for granted that mathematical precision in epidemiological work is illusory (not only the point estimates, but our estimates about the uncertainty of these, are inherently uncertain) and no numerical probability value can carry over straight to our scientific arguments. This is why epidemiological papers have sections “Limitations of the Study” – to show the discrepancy between assumptions behind their calculations and what they actually suspect to be the case.

To make it short: (i) we do things for a reason and (ii) we are humble about it.

The book covers both experimental (randomized) strudies and observational (epidemiological) studies and focuses on practical workflows rather than theoretical exposition. It experimental itself, as it’s written largely by AI (mostly Opus 4.7-5 models in Claude Code and Claude Science harnesses) and the AI-generated text is only lightly edited for readability, not that much for style. The references were checked by AI either from pubmed or from the web directly. The reader should note that the facts were generally not checked by human for accuracy. You are reading a book that is, in a sense, still in search of its author. I do hope that it will be read it with the help of AI, including for fact checking, as model progression will make it easier to identify errors of previous models. I will not include solutions to excercises, in the hope that for it will be pedagogically advantageous to elicit these with the help of AI.

The reader is encouraged to report errors and offer suggestions directly to me at ymaivali@gmail.com

2 Introduction

2.1 Scientific method: the short version

Science has a single goal: to produce useful knowledge. “Useful” does not necessarily mean “monetized”, as there is more to human experience than business. It means any kind of knowledge that is interesting for any reason. Scientific product is used in technology, in medicine, in business, in mass murder, in politics, in music, and, frankly, in all human affairs. Without science everything would be different.

Scientists build and test theories on data. This means that science is a belief system, where existing beliefs are continually updated on new evidence. Science is evidence-based, but its more than that. It’s like a spider’s web that is continuously re-spun. Every piece of new data causes ripples across this world wide web of beliefs, and thus the web is never static, never finished. The main goal of methodology, the science of method, is to ensure optimal updating of beliefs.

2.2 Rationality: the short version

We live in an uncertain world. While the world itself may or may not behave with true randomness, our attempts to collect data on it are hampered by measuring noise, random variation, and bias. Thus any attempt on making sense of the world must come to grips with these sources of uncertainty.

What makes a rational entity? Perhaps its goal is making the most of limited, noisy and biased information to formulate degrees of belief; and as a step further, to convert these beliefs into optimal decisions. We can make sense of rational beliefs by thinking of a spectrum of uncertainties, beginning with radical uncertainty, where we have no relevant information to limit the possibilities and, on the other end of the continuum, completely understood uncertainty exemplified by games of chance. If we think of uncertainty in terms of the risk of something bad happening, then we immediately see the deep unpleasantness of the radical uncertainty side of the continuum, as the risks are essentially unknowable – they can be very large, very small, or anything in between. In contrast, the games of chance are much more satisfying, as their rules are perfectly understood and we can from simple models to calculate the exact long-run relative frequencies of any conceivable outcome.

Somewhere on this continuum lies every scientific question that we might want to get an answer on empirical data. What scientists often do is to try to identify interesting questions that are as far away as possible from the radical uncertainty side of things, and then apply simple stochastic models that are close to those employed for the games of chance; all in the hope that the modeling is somehow relevant to the real world. Every statistical model that uses binomial distribution to predict binary outcomes (dead/alive, etc.) is an example of this approach to rationality.

Example: Covid-19 in the spring of 2020. The first epidemiological models of spread of COVID-19 in Europe (and in Estonia) used imperfect and scanty data and assumed that the Chinese containment measures would be replicated in Europe; and the models themselves were created on flu epidemics. These models failed on two levels: (i) their predictions were wrong, and (ii) their uncertainty intervals were too narrow, disregarding actual uncertainty. Models appropriate for the known uncertainty side of the spectrum were (mis)used on a problem that was rather close to radical uncertainty.

Covid-19 in the spring of 2021. With a years worth of good data and appropriately tweaked model structures, everything works much better. Now the problem is quite far from radical uncertainty and modeling has become very useful indeed.

What are the assumptions behind rational thought?

Very general assumptions:

  1. The constancy of Nature. The mechanisms of Nature do not change over time, neither does nature try to deceive us.

  2. The consistency of explanation: any true description of Nature is self-consistent, meaning that a claim cannot be true and false at the same time, and that two mutually inconsistent claims cannot be both true (but they can be both false).

  3. The consistency of inference: we have identified the full set of mutually exclusive and exhaustive states of the world (or hypotheses describing these states of the world, a.k.a. sample space, a.k.a. hypothesis space). The rules of inference guarantee that on correct application the conclusions will always be the same (regardless on which particular way they were reached).

  4. Methodological reductionism: the great diversity of observed phenomena is generated by a relatively small number of underlying causal mechanisms. That is, we can get meaningful insight into nature by studying these elementary causal mechanisms.

These assumptions can never be proven to be satisfied.

General modeling assumptions:

  1. Stationarity: the data generating process that is being modeled does not change during data collection.

  2. Exchangeability - the order on which the data was collected has no influence on the results.

  3. Our models are relevant to processes that generated the data used to fit these models. Only then can the fitted model be used to meaningfully study the natural processes that actually interest us.

2.3 Bayesian inference: the short version

We want to find out, whether Madame Marie has extra-sensory perception (ESP). We present her with playing cards and let her guess, what is on each card. We compare her data with simulated datasets that assume no ESP and see that our data are significantly different from the simulated data. Lets say that only 1 in 10 000 simulated datsets gives a fraction of TRUE guesses that is equal or greater than Madame Marie’s (\(p=10^{-4}\)). We can interpret this result as showing that the data are inconsistent with the hypothesis that Marie picks cards completely randomly. But can we interpret this as weighty evidence for ESP? And, if so, does this mean that we should now put high confidence on this hypothesis? The answer is “it depends”. It depends on:

  1. Our prior beliefs on whether Marie is clairvoyant. If we think this very unlikely (our prior probability is a lot less than \(10^{-4}\)), then the evidence obtained is clearly not enough to change our minds. To arrive at a prior probability, we should ask both about how likely is ESP as a general mechanism, and then, whether we know something about the individual that would make her more or less likely than average to have such abilities. This is complicated, and there is no guarantee that two reasonable people would arrive at the same prior probabilities. This does not mean that we should ignore our prior beliefs because we find it hard to precisely quantify them. It does mean that we introduce a new source of uncertainty, which means that in practical analysis we might want to try out a range of priors to see how robust our inference is to the choice of prior. For any uncertainty in our data, models and priors, we must strive to carry it forward all the way into our conclusions, rather than to pretend that it does not exist.

  2. the inference from data also depends on how we choose to divide the hypothesis space into testable hypotheses. The hypothesis space is the set of all mutually exclusive and exhaustive hypotheses, exactly one of which must be true. So if we define our hypothesis space as consisting of two hypotheses, one of which (H1) is that “Marie has ESP”, then the other one (H2) must be that “Marie has no ESP”. If we start by giving H1 the prior probability of 1/100 000 then that means that the H2 must have a prior probability of 1 - 1/100 000. The probabilities always sum to unity across the hypothesis space. This means that if, after taking account of the data, the posterior probability of H1 is determined as 1/10, then the P(H2) must be 9/10.1 But what happens when we slice the cake differenty, into three hypotheses: H1 (ESP), H2 (no ESP) and H3 (bias), the last one encompassing all mechanisms that would shift the results away from completely random card picking?2 Some of these will be conscious (fraud) and some unconcious (the experimenter might give involuntary signals to Marie), so the H3 is itself a hodgepodge of very different hypotheses.3 The the priors would change: P(H1) = 1/100 000, P(H3) = 1/10, say, and P(H2) = 1 - 0.1 - 1/100 000. Now it becoes crucial how well data corresponds to not only H2 (very poorly), but also to H3 (quite well). And if data is equally compatible with H1 and H3, and the prior probability of H3, P(H3) >> P(H1), then the same data turn out quite useless in promoting H1 for us. And again, the posterior probabilities will sum to unity across the 3 hypotheses.

For the philosophically minded, re-slicing the hypothesis space would be akin to a Kuhnian paradigm shift where it becomes possible, and indeed necessary, to re-evaluate what the data says.

These points are logical necessities, which have the following consequences:

  1. Evidence is always relative - it is a quantitative measure of how much more data supports one hypothesis over another hypothesis.4 There is no sense in collecting data, if you only have a single hypothesis to check it against.

  2. The weight of evidence depends on the data and the data alone contributes to evidence, but to form rational beliefs on the truth of hypotheses you also need quantified prior beliefs, which are independent of data in hand.

  3. How you divide the hypotheses space can make a big difference, and if a relevant hypothesis is excluded from consideration, that could easily invalidate your conclusions. Sadly, in science we can never be sure that we have even an inkling of what the true hypothesis looks like. The rational thought is not a truth machine – it only shows how to optimally combine information that you have. Thus, if given poor-quality data, models or hypotheses, it in no way guarantees a march towards truth.

Here’s the general workflow of updating ones beliefs, presented as a recipe for a witches brew. The ingredients: one hypothesis space, appropriately granulated prior knowledge, a smattering of data, an assortment of statistical models. The recipe: start by chopping the hypothesis space into at least two hypotheses, then pin prior probabilities to each of your hypotheses (these sum to one), throw in the data, and let the whole thing simmer in the the model, until the mcmc chains run out of steam. Now, take a sip of the updated posterior probabilities. If you did not like the taste, simply add more data or improve the model and do another round. Repeat, until satisfied.

Imagine the priors as made of sticky stuff, holding back the amount of change in the probability from prior to posterior effected by data. This is needed, so that each piece of favorable new evidence would not lead to extreme fluctuations in your belief system. Importantly, the stickiness depends on the numerical value of the prior. Thus, the exact same evidence can have a dramatically smaller effect on the posterior, when the prior probability of your hypothesis is very large (close to one) or very small (close to zero). Remember the saying: extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence – this is it! The prior probability of 1/2 has the least stickiness in terms of holding back how much the data changes the probability of the hypothesis in question. This means that to effect a prior-to-posterior change of a percentage point, you need to collect dramatically more evidence if your prior is far from 1/2. It also means that if you want your data to have the same effect on all the hypotheses in your hypothesis space, then you should use numerically identical priors for all the hypotheses. For instance, if you partitioned your hypothesis space into 5 hypotheses, then for each of them the prior probability will have to be 1/5. Such priors we call “non-informative” or “flat”.

2.4 Decision theory: the short version

Narrow rationality can be separated into two interlocking parts:

  1. epistemic rationality maps our beliefs to reality.

  2. instrumental rationality uses these beliefs to chart an optimal course of action. Technically speaking, it maximizes expected utility.

Maximizing expected utility entails: (1) mapping of all possible actions that are under considerations; (2) evaluation of the consequences of each possible action in each possible state of the world.5

Obviously, some people are more rational than others. Psychologists believe that a rationally thinking person is characterized by a specific type of thinking: he collects information before making up his mind, he seeks various points of view, he thinks extensively about a problem, he calibrates the degree of strength of his opinion to the degree of evidence available, he thinks about future consequences before taking action, he explicitly weighs pluses and minuses of situations before making a decision, and he seeks nuance and avoids absolutism.6

2.5 Causality: the short version

Causal hypothesis means that we believe that some specified cause produces or helps to produce some effects. Causal effects may be produced by the cause either always (think of colliding billiard balls), or with some probability/frequency (think of smoking causing cancer). Also, causes can be direct or indirect and a given effect may have several causes, which must either operate all together or in some combination to produce this effect. So it is often not a trivial task to squeeze the world into a causal mold.

Causal thinking seems to be how our brains impose order onto universe. At the very least, it is a major tool of scientists and conspiracy theorists. However, the concept of causality is metaphysical: we cannot be sure whether or not it corresponds to anything real in nature (if you are in doubt about this, ask a quantum physicist). Nevertheless, by mutual consent, we do have an operational view of causation. This view is not meant to solve the metaphysical conundrums of causality, but merely to provide an agreed convenience definition, which allows to incorporate causal thinking into epidemiological thinking in a more-or-less formal way. This is the counterfactual principle, according to which removing a cause should also remove (or at least modify) the effect. So, if we modify a hypothetical cause by experimental treatment and nothing changes in the purported effect, then we can (perhaps) say, that we have falsified a causal hypothesis.

“Counterfactual” means that a cause cannot be present and absent at the same time, unless we live in a multiverse. Thus, to test a causal hypothesis, we must employ a model wherein we can generate in silico both states of the world, and by comparing the predicted value of the “effect” at different values of the “cause” we can modify our beliefs on the hypothesis.

Example 1: By doing a randomized placebo-controlled drug study and employing a simple model outcome = intercept + slope x drug=1/0 + error we can compare the modeled outcome for each group and if the drug causally influences it, these model predictions will be substantially different.

Example 2: Was an extreme storm caused by global warming? We build a complicated model and run it many times under a global warming scenario, and in an alternative model specification that lacks global warming. If the global warming scenario produces storms like this with twice the frequency of the sans warming scenario, then we can conclude that the particular storm was caused by global warming with the probability of 2/3.

2.6 Modeling: the short version

Methodology does not deal directly with genesis of theories, which is an art more than it is science. This means that a methodologist does not try to build a full-fledged robot scientist. Rather he tries to build a simple robot that thinks simple thoughts. Naturally, our robots do not really “think” in any meaningful sense, but it behooves us to pretend that they do, so that we can more intuitively use their output. Our simple-minded but quick robot we call the regression model and our major concern with it is that we must learn to see the world through its severely limited perspective, in order to be able to properly use its insights in the context of our scientific inference. So we must learn to think like an utterly alien idiot-savant, and somehow learn to to enjoy this! For example, the class of models most often used to crunch simple treatment-vs-control experiments sees the world as consisting of normally distributed variables that have linear dependencies on perfectly randomized experimental treatments, where no sample is small enough not to reflect the population from which it was collected, and no logically possible experimental effect or data variation is too big or too small, so the robot will believe in them all with equanimity. The scientist, of course, should know better and this should be reflected in how she uses the model and its predictions. This implies that all the modeling in the world cannot convert science into an objective endeavor.

Behind every model is our understanding of the data generating process, which is the collection of causal elementary mechanisms that generated the data, which goes into fitting the model. The data generating process comprises the laws of nature, the experimental design (controls and such), the data collection (whether it is collected in multiple centres, etc.), and every other mechanism that can affect how the data come out. A major assumption of modeling is that our system is stationary, meaning that the data generating mechanism does not change during the collection of data. This does not mean that the accumulating data cannot systematically change over time or between study centers, only that the underlying mechanisms do not change. For example, if we want to model the prize of gas, the model loses stationarity as soon as a new factor affecting the prize emerges (electric cars).

Our models should strive to reflect some relevant aspect(s) of the data generating mechanism, and by building several of them and comparing the results, there is a chance that we will learn something useful about the whole complex mechanism that is nature. What the models should not strive for, is to be “true” in the sense that they would precisely describe and/or predict the workings of the world. To understand why, think of a map versus the landscape. Any map that is “true” would be as big and as complex as the landscape it depicts, which means that basically it would have to be identical to it. Of course, this kind of map, or model, would be equally impractical and impossible to produce.

2.7 A philosophical aside: knowledge and belief

Suppose we know that a coin is fair (P(heads) = 0.5) and that it will be tossed N = 10 times. Then we fully understand the experiment, and its results, albeit probabilistic, are also fully predictable: the probability of any number of heads can be calculated before a single toss (Chapter 5 does the calculation). This is the deductive use of probability theory that, while making the implications of the coin tossing game explicit, generates no new knowledge, which is not already present in the rules of the game. The general properties of the data that might happen are already implicitly present in those rules. We cannot say exactly, which data we will see, but we know exactly the probability of getting a head, from which we deduced the probability of getting k heads (or “k or more heads”, etc.) out of N tosses.

In classical philosophy there are two ways of understanding the world. One is knowledge (episteme), which is by definition true. Another is belief (doxa), which comes to us in degrees. Knowledge is the domain of mathematicians and philosophers, while belief is what everybody else has to survive on. Knowledge we can get deductively from axioms and beliefs we get inductively from the physical world. In the coin tossing experiment, where we knew the data generating system, we calculated the probability distribution to get knowledge. But in the real world we never get to have knowledge in this sense, which means that we need to use probabilities differently.

This fundamentally different belief-generating usage of probability theory uses the results of N experiments (data) to guess the rules of the game. In particular, we would like to estimate the probability of getting heads.7 This means creating new knowledge which is not implicit in data. As we cannot create knowledge in this way, we have to make do with creating new beliefs. These new beliefs still come in the form of a probability distribution. To make this inductive leap we must go from the probability \(P(data \vert rules)\) to \(P(rules \vert data)\), for which we use the Bayes theorem (Chapter 8). Interestingly, from the data alone, it is clearly not possible to reach the correct rules, because our data are always consistent with a great many rule sets (if not to say, infinitely many). Here we are reduced to compare different hypotheses (hypothetical rule sets) with the data and trying to find which ones are the more consistent with data. This means that we must already have the correct answer in mind, before we see the data. Sa we will see later, it also means that we must be able to put numerical probabilities on different hypotheses, so that we would not overestimate the data-driven posterior probability of a unlikely hypothesis that happens to be a little closer to the data than a much more likely hypothesis. And, of course, should our hypothesis set lack the correct hypothesis, then it makes absolutely no difference how carefully we weigh on the data the hypotheses we did put into it – the data cannot help us, lest we guess the truth in advance!
