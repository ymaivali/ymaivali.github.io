22  Multiple regression: adjustment

Chapter 20 ended with a regression that answered its question correctly and the causal question wrongly, and with the two remedies for that: randomise the treatment, or measure the variables that stand in the way of correct causal inference and put them in the model. A typical such model looks like this: \(y= \beta_0 + \beta_1x_1 + \beta_2x_2 + \dots + \beta_kx_k + Normal(0, \sigma)\) where X1 is the treatment variable and all other predictors are needed for adjustment.

What changes once the model has more than one predictor is adjustment: a coefficient is now a contrast at fixed values of the other predictors, which is what allows a back-door path to be blocked, and which is also what makes the coefficient easy to misread. This chapter is about that one idea – what a coefficient means when the others are held constant, which coefficients of a fitted model can be read causally and which cannot, and what adjustment does and does not buy in an observational study and in a randomised one.

Two things the additive model \(\mu_i = \alpha + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \beta_2 x_{i2} + \dots + \beta_K x_{iK}\) takes for granted are deferred. That each predictor’s effect is the same at every value of the others is a substantive claim rather than a neutral default, and removing it with an interaction term \(\beta_{int}x_1x_2\) is the subject of half of Chapter 23; that a predictor contributes a straight line is a second such claim, and curves – polynomials, and then splines – are the subject of the other half. Between them, Chapter 22 sets out the Bayesian workflow by which any of these models is checked, compared, and turned into a causal contrast.

22.1 Adding predictors

We now introduce a second index. Let \(k = 1, \dots, K\) run over the predictors, keeping \(i\) for the observations, so that \(x_{ik}\) is the value of predictor \(k\) measured on subject \(i\). (Chapter 27 adds a third, \(j\), for groups. Holding these three apart – \(i\) observations, \(k\) predictors, \(j\) groups – is what keeps the multilevel notation readable.)

\[y_i \sim normal(\mu_i, \sigma)\]

\[\mu_i = \alpha + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \beta_2 x_{i2} + \dots + \beta_K x_{iK} = \alpha + \sum_{k=1}^{K} \beta_k x_{ik}\]

with priors for \(\alpha\), each \(\beta_k\), and \(\sigma\). Read the subscripts again: an \(i\) appears on the data (\(y_i\), \(x_{ik}\)) and on the predicted mean \(\mu_i\), and nowhere else. There is one coefficient per predictor, shared by all subjects, and one \(\sigma\) for all of them.

Why add a predictor at all? There are three different reasons, each with different rules:

  • we want the causal effect of \(x_1\) on y, and \(x_2\) is a confounder we must adjust for – the reason that dominates Part III of this book, and the one where a DAG decides which covariates belong in the model (Chapter 11);

  • we want to predict y as accurately as possible, in which case any predictor that carries information is welcome, whether or not it causes anything (Chapter 26). This reason is incompatible with the first one, as the variables whose inclusion in the model destroys our causal inference are often useful for predicting y;

  • \(x_2\) is a competing cause of y that we are not interested in but which, once accounted for, shrinks \(\sigma\) and so sharpens the estimate of the effect we do care about. This reason is perfectly compatible with the first one.

What the additive structure above encodes is a separate assumption, and it is not an assumption about the predictors being unrelated to each other. It is that the effect of one predictor does not depend on the level of the other: moving \(x_1\) by one unit shifts \(\mu_i\) by \(\beta_1\) units, regardless of the value of \(x_2\). Correlated predictors are perfectly admissible in an additive model – indeed, dealing with correlated predictors is what adjustment is for. In contrast, when the effect of one predictor on y actually does depend on the level of another, we need an interaction term.

So what does a slope coefficient of an additive model mean, once there is more than one?

The slope of \(x_1\) tells us how much mean y changes if we change \(x_1\) by 1 unit and change \(x_2\) by 0 units (keep \(x_2\) constant). Equivalently, the slope of \(x_2\) tells us how much mean y changes if we change \(x_2\) by 1 unit and keep \(x_1\) constant. Note that the math of the regression model does not distinguish between different kinds of predictors (treatment var, confounder, and so on) and the interpretations of different slope parameters are therefore symmetrical. While arithmetic of the regression model is symmetrical, the causal reading is not. If the model was built to estimate the effect of \(x_1\), the covariates that make \(\beta_1\) interpretable are generally the wrong set for \(\beta_2\): when \(x_2\) is a confounder it acts on y partly through \(x_1\), so holding \(x_1\) constant leaves \(\beta_2\) a direct effect rather than a total one, and \(\beta_2\) may still be confounded by variables that were never included because they were irrelevant to \(x_1\). Reading every coefficient of a fitted model as the effect of its own predictor is common enough to have a name: the Table 2 fallacy.1

There is another way of interpreting multiple regression betas: \(\beta_1\) tells us, what extra information on predicting the y value we get from \(x_1\), once \(x_2\) has already been used to predict y. And conversely, what extra value in predicting y do we get from \(x_2\), once \(x_1\) has already been used. Note what this does not say. It is a statement about what is left over, not about mechanism: \(\beta_1\) is the slope of y on the part of \(x_1\) that \(x_2\) cannot account for.2 Neither predictor need cause y for it to hold. For instance, we can predict someone’s height from the length of his right foot, but if we already know the prediction from the left foot, the right foot gives us (almost) nothing. And since including it in the model leaves very little covariation with height that is not also covariation of the left foot with height (and vice versa), then most of the predictive information that is included in the \(R^2\), is not included in the respective betas (which consequently have extremely wide CI-s). This is collinearity.

The predictive reading and the causal reading of the same coefficient address different questions, and it is worth being precise about how they differ. Association is transmitted along every path between \(x_1\) and y that the conditioning leaves open, in either direction along the arrows. A causal effect is carried only by the directed paths from \(x_1\) to y: all of them for a total effect, the single path for a direct effect. Adjustment is the operation that closes the remaining open paths without closing the one(s) we wish to study. Prediction is therefore not causal inference with more pathways included, and a predictive model is not a causal model plus extras.3 A barometer reading predicts a storm and has no directed path to it at all; a variable measured downstream of y can be the best predictor available while having no effect on y. This is the sense in which the second reason for adding a predictor, given above, is incompatible with the first.

Some further points.

  • There is no reason to think that if we compare the models \(\mu_i = \alpha + \beta x_{i1}\) and \(\mu_i = \alpha + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \beta_2 x_{i2}\), then \(\beta\) and \(\beta_1\) will be similar, or even have the same sign. Which of the two is the quantity you want depends on the role \(x_2\) plays in the causal graph (Chapter 11), and the possibilities do not all point the same way:

    • if \(x_2\) is a mediator on the path from \(x_1\) to y, then \(\beta\) estimates the total effect and \(\beta_1\) the direct effect (given no confounding, no interaction, and no exposure-induced confounding of the mediator – Chapter 15);

    • if \(x_2\) is a confounder of \(x_1\) and y, the labels swap: the adjusted \(\beta_1\) is the estimate of the total effect, and the unadjusted \(\beta\) is biased;

    • if \(x_2\) is a collider on a path between \(x_1\) and y, adjusting for it introduces bias, and neither coefficient estimates an effect;

    • if \(x_2\) is simply another cause of y, unrelated to \(x_1\), both estimate the same total effect, and the adjusted one is more precise because \(\sigma\) is smaller.

    The regression arithmetic is identical in all four cases. Only the DAG distinguishes them. This is why the adjustment set is settled before the fit and cannot be recovered from it, a point Chapter 22 returns to with a numerical demonstration.

  • If you have several predictors in your regression, then for easier comparison of the betas it makes sense to standardise them. A standardised beta is the change in mean y per one sd of that predictor as it varies in this sample, so the comparison is a comparison of how much each predictor contributes to the observed variation in y here – not of how large its causal effect is. A predictor that was measured over a narrow range in this study will have a small standardised beta even if its effect per unit is large.

  • If you have only continuous predictors, use x_st = (x - mean(x))/sd(x). Then all your predictors have mean = 0 and sd = 1, the intercept is the value of y when all predictors are at their mean values (which is now 0), and each slope gives the change in mean y if the relevant predictor changes by one standard deviation, all other predictors remaining unchanged.

  • If you have both continuous and binary predictors, you get approximate comparability of the betas with x_st2 = (x - mean(x))/(2*sd(x)), which rescales a continuous predictor to a two-sd change rather than a one-sd change. The reason for the factor of 2 is comparability with an untransformed 0/1 indicator: a binary variable with p = 0.5 has an sd of 0.5, so switching it from 0 to 1 is already a two-sd change. Dividing the continuous predictors by twice their sd therefore puts both kinds on the same footing.4 As before, all other X-s are held constant and the intercept refers to the mean values of all of them.

  • Centring and scaling a continuous predictor that enters an interaction or a product term is not optional in practice: on the raw scale the product term is nearly collinear with its parents and the sampler struggles. The cohort work below uses age in decades centred at 62 for this reason.

22.2 Adjustment in the master cohort

Chapter 20 fitted sbp ~ drug in the master cohort and got a slope far from the truth, because severity and age are unbalanced between the treated and the untreated and both raise blood pressure. Those variables are measured here, so they can go into the model.

Show code 
df <- simulate_cohort(n = 50000, seed = 2024)
df$age_c <- (df$age - 62) / 10
oracle  <- attr(df, "oracle")
ate_sbp <- mean(oracle$sbp1 - oracle$sbp0)      # the answer key: -12 mmHg

The priors follow the same reasoning as before. The intercept is mean systolic pressure in a treated hypertensive cohort, which is in the region of 140 mmHg. The slopes are now four different quantities – the effect of the drug, of a unit of latent severity, of a decade of age, of diabetes – and a single \(normal(0, 10)\) prior on the b class covers all of them: it says that no one of these predictors is expected to move mean pressure by much more than 20 mmHg per unit, while leaving the direction open.

Show code 
pri_sbp <- c(prior(normal(140, 15), class = "Intercept"),
             prior(normal(0, 10),   class = "b"),
             prior(normal(12, 6),   class = "sigma"))

m_sbp_crude <- brm(sbp ~ drug, data = df, family = gaussian(),
                   prior = c(prior(normal(140, 15), class = "Intercept"),
                             prior(normal(0, 20),   class = "b"),
                             prior(normal(15, 7),   class = "sigma")),
                   file = "models/m_sbp_crude", file_refit = "on_change")

m_sbp_adj <- brm(sbp ~ drug + severity + age_c + diabetes,
                 data = df, family = gaussian(), prior = pri_sbp,
                 file = "models/m_sbp_adj", file_refit = "on_change")

The coefficients recover the simulation’s own structural equation: severity raises pressure by 6.97 mmHg per unit against a true 7.00, a decade of age by 1.49 against a true 1.50, and the drug lowers it by 11.94 against a true 12.00. Diabetes gets a coefficient of -0.024, correctly: in the simulation diabetes raises blood pressure only by raising severity, and severity is already in the model.

Reported as a marginal contrast rather than as a coefficient – which in this additive model gives the same number, as it did in Chapter 20 – the estimate is:

Adjustment has done its job here, and it is worth being clear about why it worked: severity is fully observed in this version of the cohort, and the graph of Chapter 20 says that severity and age together block every back-door path from drug to sbp. Adjustment is not a statistical operation that removes confounding in general. It removes exactly the confounding carried by the variables you adjust for, and the claim that those variables suffice comes from the graph. Chapter 19 is about what to do when they do not, and later chapters use the cohort’s partly hidden severity to show the difference.

22.2.1 What adjustment buys under randomisation

Chapter 20 promised numbers for a claim it did not demonstrate: that randomisation does nothing for precision, and that adjusting for prognostic covariates in a randomised study still sharpens the estimate even though it is not needed to remove bias. Both models can be fitted to the randomised variant of the cohort.

Show code 
params_rct <- modifyList(default_params(),
                         list(d0 = 0, d_sev = 0, d_dm = 0, d_pref = 0))
df_rct <- simulate_cohort(n = 50000, seed = 2024, params = params_rct)
df_rct$age_c <- (df_rct$age - 62) / 10

m_sbp_rct <- brm(sbp ~ drug, data = df_rct, family = gaussian(),
                 prior = c(prior(normal(140, 15), class = "Intercept"),
                           prior(normal(0, 20),   class = "b"),
                           prior(normal(15, 7),   class = "sigma")),
                 file = "models/m_sbp_rct", file_refit = "on_change")

m_sbp_adj_rct <- brm(sbp ~ drug + severity + age_c + diabetes,
                     data = df_rct, family = gaussian(), prior = pri_sbp,
                     file = "models/m_sbp_adj_rct", file_refit = "on_change")
The same randomised cohort analysed with and without baseline covariates. The estimate does not move; the uncertainty does.
analysis effect (mmHg) posterior sd interval width sigma
randomised, crude -11.986 0.108 0.423 11.787
randomised, adjusted -11.949 0.072 0.277 8.016

Both estimates are within a few hundredths of the truth, as they should be: with random assignment there is no back-door path and nothing to adjust away. What adjustment changes is \(\sigma\), which falls from 11.8 to 8 mmHg, because severity and age account for a large part of the person-to-person variation in blood pressure. The posterior sd of the effect falls in step, by about 34%. This is the third of the three reasons for adding a predictor listed above, and in a randomised trial it is the only one that applies. Note also that the covariates must be baseline covariates – measured before assignment – for this to be free. Adjusting for something measured after treatment is a different operation with a different consequence, as Chapter 22 shows.

22.3 Key points

  • A coefficient in a multiple regression is a contrast at fixed values of the other predictors. Whether that is the quantity you want depends on the role each other predictor plays in the graph: confounder, mediator, collider, or competing cause. The arithmetic is identical in all four cases; only the DAG distinguishes them.
  • Because the arithmetic is symmetrical, the causal reading is not. The covariates that make \(\beta_1\) interpretable are generally the wrong set for \(\beta_2\), so reading every coefficient of a fitted model as the effect of its own predictor is a mistake with a name: the Table 2 fallacy.
  • Adjustment removes the confounding carried by the variables adjusted for, and only that. In the cohort, severity and age block every back-door path and the adjusted estimate recovers the answer key.
  • Under randomisation, adjusting for baseline prognostic covariates does not change the estimate but lowers \(\sigma\) and sharpens it. This is the only one of the three reasons for adding a predictor that applies to a randomised study.
  • The predictive reading of a coefficient – what \(x_1\) adds once \(x_2\) has been used – is a statement about what is left over, not about mechanism. Association is transmitted along every open path; a causal effect only along directed paths. Prediction is therefore not causal inference with more pathways included.
  • A standardised beta is the change in mean y per one sd of that predictor as it varies in this sample. It compares contributions to the observed variation here, not causal effect sizes.

22.4 Exercises

  1. Adjustment is only as good as the graph. In the master cohort, refit the adjusted model leaving severity out, so that only age_c and diabetes remain. Report the marginal effect against the truth of -12 mmHg. Then check whether any posterior predictive check on the observed variables reveals the problem – Chapter 22 sets out how such a check is run. What does the answer imply about model-checking as a defence against unmeasured confounding, and which chapter takes up the alternative?

  2. Standardised betas, and what they compare. Refit the cohort’s adjusted model with all continuous predictors divided by twice their sd, as the chapter describes, and rank the predictors by the size of their standardised coefficients. Now double the range of age in the simulation without changing its per-decade effect, and repeat. Which ranking changed, and which quantity was it ever a ranking of?

  3. Frisch-Waugh-Lovell by hand. Regress age_c on severity and keep the residuals. Now regress sbp on those residuals alone, with severity nowhere in the model, and compare the slope with the age_c coefficient from the two-predictor fit. Then repeat with severity and age_c swapped. What does the exercise show about which part of a predictor a multiple-regression coefficient is computed from?

  4. The second coefficient. Take the adjusted cohort model and write down, for each coefficient in turn, the causal quantity it would have to be for a Table 2 reading to be correct, and the adjustment set that quantity would require. For how many of them is the model’s own adjustment set the right one? Which coefficients are direct effects rather than total ones, and why?
