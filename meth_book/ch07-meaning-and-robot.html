8  What probability means, and the robot

The calculus chapter, Chapter 6, laid down the rules of probability as axioms and put them to work — conditional probability, the classical definition, the binomial distribution — while deferring two questions. First, what does the probability value \(P(A)=0.1\) mean: a frequency in the world, or a degree of belief in the reasoner? Second, why these rules and no others — what is the connection of these axioms to rational belief, and what licenses attaching a probability to a hypothesis, which has no frequency to count?

The first half of the chapter asks what probability means. It separates the epistemic reading (probability grades a state of knowledge) from the aleatoric reading (probability as a physical property of a repeatable set-up), sets out why we take the epistemic road, and distinguishes two epistemic schools — subjective and objective Bayesians — who agree on the calculus but divide over where prior probabilities come from.

The second half asks, why these rules? It returns to the robot of Chapter 3, weakens one of its two design assumptions, and shows that a reasoner required only to be consistent is forced to the very same sum and product rules the counting approach of Chapter 6 already produced. That two independent routes — counting and consistency — deliver one calculus is the main reason for taking the calculus seriously as a logic of science. The chapter closes by pushing the robot to its limit, the universal thinker, and marking what even that ideal provably cannot do — which is where the modern question of artificial reasoning enters.

8.1 Understanding uncertainty

There is a pandemic coming our way, and we need to model its course. To do this we need, among other things, to fix the value of model input called R0 (the number of people infected on average by a single infected person). Initially, we will have little data to estimate the R0 on, which makes R0 a known unknown. So we estimate it on the data that we have, only to end up with wide error margins, which will be carried over into our prediction of the course of the epidemic. This way we transfer uncertainty from data to model to prediction to interpretation to policy. Surely the policy makers deserve to know that there is a large but quantified level of uncertainty about how many hospital beds are needed within a month or so, as this would allow them to implement policies that err on the safe side. Or, alternatively, we could just pick a couple of point values for the R0 from thin air and run a couple of models that result in predictions with much narrower error margins. As everybody loves certainty, this is much what happened in the spring of 2020, when the COVID-19 models guiding policy in different countries gave predictions that differed from one another by far more than their stated uncertainty allowed, for reasons — poor input data, wrong modelling assumptions, selective reporting — that a post-mortem of epidemic forecasting has catalogued.1 This is how model-based artificial certainty is manufactured: by banishing the residual uncertainty into the murky realm of unknown unknowns. We only become aware of the presence of unknown unknowns from failure, and as we cannot really understand, much less quantify them, they are really scary.2 The moral of our story is that the aim of the scientist should be to tame as much of the wild uncertainty that they can, by turning it into quantifiable known unknowns. It is not to convert uncertainty into certainty, which is logically impossible, as much as building a perpetuum mobile is physically impossible, and as often leads to grief.

In any decent model of a biological process there are different types of uncertainty that include

  1. the uncertainty arising from biological variation

  2. the uncertainty arising from sampling error

  3. the uncertainty arising from the model fitting algorithm

  4. the uncertainty arising from the model structure (linearity, normality, links, and whatnot)

  5. the uncertainty arising from the data structure (missingness, data transformations, measurement quality, what is actually measured, etc.)

  6. the uncertainty arising from what is actually modelled vs. what we want to know based on this modelling.

Taken together, these motley sources of uncertainty pretty much guarantee that our predictions of complex phenomena are over-confident. This holds for both formal and informal predictions. In April 2020, Spiegelhalter and colleagues asked 140 UK experts and more than 2,000 members of the public for point forecasts of four quantities describing the pandemic by the end of the year, among them the number of COVID-19 deaths, together with a range each respondent believed had a 75% chance of containing the truth.3 The experts were better calibrated than the public, yet they substantially underestimated the pandemic’s eventual size, and their 75% intervals contained the outcome for fewer than two of the four quantities on average; the public did worse. An interval meant to hold the truth three times in four that holds it fewer than two times in four is a large underestimate of uncertainty, made by people who did not realise they were making it and who had rated values near the truth as unlikely. A major problem with formal modelling of uncertainty is that it gives false confidence about uncertainty.

Let us assume, for the sake of argument, that we live in a fully deterministic world where there is no uncertainty concerning the past, present, or future. In this world it is possible, in principle, to predict exactly when a butterfly flips its wings a thousand years from now. The deterministic world is fully described by a set of rigid cause-effect relations, and there is no stochasticity in it. Objectively, in there nothing is uncertain. But still, there are humans in the ointment, and humans tend to be fallible. Even when they measure a quantity with a perfectly calibrated instrument, there is always some measurement error. And that error is enough to introduce uncertainty into our deterministic world. This uncertainty is subjective, or epistemic – it belongs to us, not to the thing that was measured – and it propagates to any conclusions based on these measurements. In such a world, even if in principle we could say that patient X dies within a week, because of this kind of propagated error we can only say that it is plausible that patient X dies within a week, or that there is a risk that patient X dies within a week.

Of course, things can be more or less plausible and the risk can be higher or lower, which means that we need a language for grading plausibility numerically. That language is the probability calculus of Chapter 6, whose scale from 0 to 1 and whose rules we now take as given; the question here is what the number means. If we denote the hypothesis “patient X dies within a week” by A, then \(P(A)\) is the plausibility of A being true. After the week is up, \(P(A)\) can only be 0 or 1 (unless we lost track of X); until then it moves between those extremes, subject to X’s condition and our knowledge of it.

Now, suppose that we live in a world, where truly stochastic stuff happens (be it in a quantum level or in weather prediction, or wherever). This world is only partially causal, meaning that (i) cause-effect relations can come with built-in randomness (sometimes they work, sometimes they don’t, like smoking causing cancer), or (ii) some relations are inherently acausal (completely unpredictable, like radioactive decay) Does the meaning of P(A) change? The answer is, no. In this world we still have measurement uncertainty, which we try to reduce as much we can, and on top of this is the real, irreducible uncertainty, which comes from how the world itself works. They converge, and can never be disentangled, in our final probability P(A), which is still epistemic.

Another example: the weather report says that P(rain) is 60%. What does it mean? It either rains or it doesn’t, so P(rain) cannot describe a physical feature of the world. “60% rain” cannot be objective in the sense of “being really there”. Usually P(rain) is calculated by running a weather model many times with slightly differing input data and counting the relative fraction of runs where the model predicts rain. If 6 models out of 10 predict rain, then the weatherman says that “P(rain) = 0.6, or there’s a 60% chance of rain”. Although the 60% was derived from a relative frequency, this does not matter for the interpretation – the probability is still epistemic. As a recent development we now have AI-driven weather forecasts, such as DeepMind’s GraphCast4 and GenCast, which do not solve the physical equations of the atmosphere when producing a forecast. They are neural networks trained on historical atmospheric states, which were themselves produced by physics-based models. GenCast produces an ensemble of stochastic forecasts by repeatedly sampling from a learned distribution, and P(rain) is read off that ensemble as the fraction of members that produce rain.5 Although this is the same operation the traditional weather model performed above, the source of the number is different: perturbed physics in one case, samples from a learned distribution in the other. But the interpretation is not: in both cases P(rain) is a degree of belief about a single, non-repeatable event, and it remains epistemic. The network’s distribution is still the forecaster’s distribution, learned from past data, not a physical property of tomorrow’s atmosphere.

This bears saying because students are often taught that probability is a long-run relative frequency of events that really happen, or at least could happen in principle, and that therefore P(A) is “objective” and “a real thing”. It has been understood for a century that this thinking is fundamentally flawed, as it ascribes reality to fictional objects.6 So aleatoric probability is a bit like a unicorn: a beautiful fiction. The “objective” interpretation is more objectively called “aleatoric”, and the box below offers a deeper dive into it.

The aleatoric interpretation of probability: chance, positivism, and frequentist statistics

The position taken above is epistemic: \(P(A)\) grades a state of knowledge, and even a number read off a frequency — the weather ensemble, a shuffled deck — is interpreted that way. The rival interpretation is aleatoric (from Latin alea, dice): probability is a physical property of a repeatable setup, a feature of the world that holds whether or not anyone is counting events. On this reading the probability of heads is a constant of the whole coin tossing arrangement (the coin, the tosser, the laws of physics, and so on) – an objective fact to be found out rather than a number to be assigned.

Probability emerged around 1660 with both aspects already present — one concerned with degrees of belief and evidence, the other with stable frequencies in games of chance — and the aleatoric aspect is the one this book sets aside.7 It is set out here because it is the philosophical basis of the frequentist statistics most readers met first, and its motivation explains both its appeal and its limits.

The principal aleatoric interpretation is the frequency theory: the probability of an attribute is the limiting relative frequency with which it occurs in an infinitely long sequence of trials. Venn gave the first systematic statement; von Mises and Reichenbach made it precise, von Mises basing it on the collective (Kollektiv) — an idealised infinite sequence in which the limiting frequency exists and does not change under any admissible rule for selecting a subsequence.8 Probability so defined is estimated, it is a quantity one converges on by observation. A second aleatoric interpretation, Popper’s propensity theory, treats probability as a physical disposition (propensity) of the experimental arrangement to produce outcomes in a fixed proportion, introduced partly to give single, non-repeated events an objective probability, which the frequency theory denies them.9 However, the propensity move has a price. As propensity is a causal disposition, attaching it to \(P(E \mid C)\) imports the directionality of cause-effect relationship into a calculus that has none: Bayes’ theorem freely inverts \(P(E \mid C)\), the tendency of a condition \(C\) to produce a later outcome \(E\), into \(P(C \mid E)\), which the propensity reading would then have to construe as a tendency of the effect to produce its own cause. Humphreys used this to show that propensities cannot satisfy the probability axioms and therefore are not probabilities.10

The frequency theory fitted the philosophy of science that dominated the early twentieth century, which saw science as an objective endeavour at least in principle, and so answered a demand of positivism. Comte’s positivism admitted as legitimate only knowledge grounded in observable phenomena and their law-like regularities, excluding metaphysical and psychological entities; its twentieth-century successor, the logical empiricism of the Vienna Circle and the allied Berlin group, held that the meaning of a term is fixed by its method of empirical verification. A degree of belief, being not directly observable, is suspect on this standard; a limiting frequency, defined by counting observable outcomes, is not. The frequency theory is in this sense the positivist account of probability — it makes probability an empirical science of mass phenomena rather than a logic of partial belief. The link was not incidental: von Mises, the theory’s chief architect, belonged to the movement and wrote a treatise defending positivism itself.11 It reverses the position of the section on the two epistemic schools below: where de Finetti holds that objective probability in the world does not exist, the frequentist holds that only the frequency is real and the degree of belief is the fiction.

This reading is the foundation of frequentist inference — Fisher’s significance tests and p values, and the hypothesis tests and confidence intervals of Neyman and Pearson.12 Its defining feature follows directly from the aleatoric commitment: probability attaches to procedures evaluated over hypothetical repetitions of the sampling, never to a hypothesis or a parameter, because a fixed unknown constant has no frequency and \(P(\text{hypothesis} \mid \text{data})\) is therefore undefined. A 95% confidence interval reports a property of the interval-construction procedure — that 95% of the intervals it generates across repeated samples would contain the true value — not a 95% probability that the parameter is in the one interval computed; this is the source of the near-universal misreading of the interval. The same commitment fixes both the scope of the theory and its limit. Where a genuine repeatable setup exists the aleatoric number is well defined, and the two readings agree; but for a one-off event or a fixed parameter there is no frequency to be had, and it is there — the single case of the weather forecast and the dying patient at the start of this chapter — that the aleatoric probability falters while the epistemic probability doesn’t miss a step. The frequentist machinery, and the null-hypothesis logic built on it, are taken up in Appendix G; this book takes the epistemic road for exactly the reason that it keeps working where the frequency reading stops. For a proponent of the epistemic interpretation, when all the relevant background information consists of relative frequencies the frequentist number is perfectly fine, and it is quietly subsumed into ordinary epistemic probability; by Occam’s razor there is no need to bring a further entity into the world to guide the work. As for making science itself objective through statistics, a truth-generating machine, two centuries of probability theory have shown this to be a pipe dream; the robot sections later in this chapter take up why.

8.1.1 Knowledge and belief: two uses of probability

Suppose we know that a coin is fair (\(P(\text{heads}) = 0.5\)) and that it will be tossed \(N = 10\) times. Then we fully understand the experiment, and its results, albeit probabilistic, are fully predictable: the probability of any number of heads can be calculated before a single toss (Chapter 6 did the calculation). This is the deductive use of probability theory. It makes the implications of the coin-tossing game explicit but generates no knowledge that is not already present in the rules of the game: we cannot say which data we will see, but we know the probability of a head, and from it we deduced the probability of \(k\) heads (or “\(k\) or more”) in \(N\) tosses.

In classical philosophy there are two ways of understanding the world. One is knowledge (episteme), which is by definition true. The other is belief (doxa), which comes in degrees. Knowledge is the domain of mathematicians and philosophers, while belief is what everybody else has to survive on. Knowledge we get deductively from axioms; beliefs we get inductively from the physical world. In the coin-tossing experiment, where we knew the data-generating system, we calculated the probability distribution and so had knowledge. In the real world we never get to have knowledge in this sense, which means that we must use probabilities differently.

This second, belief-generating use of probability theory takes the results of \(N\) experiments — the data — and guesses at the rules of the game; in particular, it estimates the probability of heads.13 That would be creating knowledge not implicit in the data, and since knowledge cannot be created this way, we make do with creating new beliefs, which still come in the form of a probability distribution. To make the inductive leap we must go from \(P(\text{data} \mid \text{rules})\) to \(P(\text{rules} \mid \text{data})\), for which we use Bayes’ theorem (Chapter 9). From the data alone the correct rules cannot be reached, because any dataset is consistent with a great many rule sets, if not infinitely many. We are reduced to comparing hypotheses — candidate rule sets — against the data and asking which are the more consistent with it, which means that the correct answer must already be among the candidates before the data arrive. It also means, as the next chapter shows, that we must be able to put numerical probabilities on the different hypotheses, so as not to overrate the posterior probability of an unlikely hypothesis that happens to lie a little closer to the data than a far more likely one. And should the set of hypotheses lack the correct one, it makes no difference how carefully the data are weighed against those we did include: the data cannot help us unless we have guessed the truth in advance. The next section makes this dependence on the hypothesis space exact.

8.1.2 What probability attaches to: the sample space and the hypothesis space

The pebble picture of Chapter 6 and the epistemic reading of this chapter differ at the choice of primitive object. In the pebble world the primitive is the outcome: the experiment fixes the bag of pebbles once and for all, one and only one pebble is the true outcome once the experiment has run, and probability is mass resting on the pebbles. On the epistemic reading the primitive is the proposition — a statement that is true or false, though we may not know which — and probability is a two-place relation: the plausibility of one proposition given another, \(P(A \mid I)\), where the background information \(I\) is never empty and is never silently dropped. Strictly speaking there is no such thing as “the probability of A”; there is only the probability of A given what the reasoner currently takes as known. Two agents holding different information assign different probabilities to the same proposition, and neither has made an error, because neither is describing the coin — each is describing their own knowledge of the coin. Treating the number as a physical property of the coin itself is what Jaynes called the mind-projection fallacy.14

Because probability attaches to propositions, hypotheses can carry probability from the outset: “the coin is head-biased”, “the drug prevents stroke”, “model \(M_1\) rather than \(M_2\) generated the data” are all propositions, and inference is the conditioning of their plausibilities on evidence. Write

\[\mathcal{H} = \{H_1, H_2, \dots\}\]

for the set of explanations the analyst is prepared to entertain: the hypothesis space. It differs from the sample space in who fixes it. The sample space is fixed by the experiment — what could the measuring apparatus have shown? The hypothesis space is fixed by the analyst — which explanations am I willing to consider? Within a problem \(\Omega\) is treated as closed and complete (itself a commitment, as the callout below notes), while \(\mathcal{H}\) is a provisional list, drawn up by a finite mind with finite imagination, and no procedure guarantees that it is complete.

A hypothesis, note, is not an event. An event is a handful of pebbles; a hypothesis is a candidate assignment of masses to the pebbles — a probability law on the sample space, one level above the bag. A single \(\Omega\) therefore supports any number of hypotheses: “the dice are fair”, “the dice are loaded towards sixes”, and “the second die copies the first” are three different mass assignments over the same 36 pebbles. Chapter 7 made the same point from the other side: choosing the probability distribution over a sample space is a scientific act, not a logical one.

Bayes’ theorem is the subject of Chapter 9, but one structural feature of it belongs here. Written with nothing suppressed, the posterior probability of hypothesis \(H_i\) given data \(D\) is

\[P(H_i \mid D, \mathcal{H}, I) = \frac{P(D \mid H_i, I)\, P(H_i \mid \mathcal{H}, I)}{\sum_j P(D \mid H_j, I)\, P(H_j \mid \mathcal{H}, I)},\]

and the denominator displays the dependence: the sum runs over the hypotheses in \(\mathcal{H}\) and no others. The theorem redistributes probability among the explanations supplied to it; it has no way of reporting that the correct explanation was not on the list.

The dependence is not a formality; it can flip a conclusion. A coin is flipped 8 times and shows 8 heads. One analyst entertains two hypotheses with equal priors: \(H_F\), the coin is fair (\(\theta = 0.5\)), and \(H_B\), the coin is head-biased (\(\theta = 0.9\)). A second analyst, who has met a conjurer, admits a third before seeing any data: \(H_T\), the coin is two-headed (\(\theta = 1\)).

Show code 
lik <- c(0.5^8, 0.9^8, 1)              # fair, head-biased, two-headed
post2 <- lik[1:2] / sum(lik[1:2])      # equal priors cancel
post3 <- lik / sum(lik)
tibble(hypothesis = c("fair", "head-biased", "two-headed"),
       `posterior, two-hypothesis space`   = c(sprintf("%.3f", post2), "not entertained"),
       `posterior, three-hypothesis space` = sprintf("%.3f", post3)) %>%
  kable()

Same data, same likelihoods, same rules of probability (Table 8.1) — yet the first analyst reports “head-biased, probability 0.99” and the second “two-headed, probability 0.70”. Neither has computed anything wrong; the difference is carried entirely by \(\mathcal{H}\), because the normalising sum in Bayes’ theorem runs only over the hypotheses present. Every posterior probability is a statement of relative plausibility within a hypothesis space, never an absolute verdict on the world. (Chapter 9’s Madame Marie example develops the same flip with the full odds-form machinery.) Note also what a further flip showing a tail would do: \(P(\text{tail} \mid H_T) = 0\), and \(H_T\) is eliminated by ordinary conditioning — the pebble discarded and the survivors rescaled. A hypothesis can die by conditioning. The next example shows that a hypothesis can be born only by a different operation.

Suppose instead that the 8 flips come out HTHTHTHT — perfect alternation. Within the two-hypothesis space the arithmetic is untroubled: \(P(D \mid H_F) = 0.5^8 \approx 0.0039\), \(P(D \mid H_B) = 0.9^4 \times 0.1^4 \approx 6.6 \times 10^{-5}\), and the posterior reports the coin fair with probability 0.98. Yet anyone looking at the sequence suspects what neither hypothesis expresses: a deterministic alternating mechanism. Both hypotheses treat the flips as exchangeable, so the order of the data — the striking thing about them — carries no evidence within this space. The analysis is not wrong; it is complacent, because the truth is not in the space. The remedy is not more conditioning but a revision of the space, \(\mathcal{H} \to \mathcal{H}'\): add \(H_A\), “the outcomes alternate deterministically”, with \(P(D \mid H_A) \approx 1\) given the first flip. Even a sceptical prior of \(0.01\) on \(H_A\), the remaining mass split equally, is enough:

Show code 
lik   <- c(fair = 0.5^8, biased = 0.9^4 * 0.1^4, alternating = 1)
prior <- c(0.495, 0.495, 0.010)
tibble(hypothesis = names(lik),
       prior      = prior,
       likelihood = signif(lik, 2),
       posterior  = round(lik * prior / sum(lik * prior), 3)) %>%
  kable()

The newly admitted hypothesis dominates at once (Table 8.2). Jaynes discusses this pattern — data driving the analyst to mint a hypothesis that no conditioning inside the old space could have produced — in his treatment of multiple hypotheses.15 Chapter 6 flagged the one-way character of conditioning in the sample space: information there only removes pebbles and rescales the rest. Revision of the hypothesis space is the operation that has no pebble-world counterpart, and the posterior predictive checks of later chapters are its organised form: a systematic way of asking whether the data are indicating that \(\mathcal{H}\) needs a new member.

Can the hypothesis space be folded into a sample space?

Formally, yes. Build a grand bag of super-pebbles, one for each pair (hypothesis, outcome), with masses

\[m(H_i, x) = P(H_i)\, P(x \mid H_i).\]

For the trick-coin example this is 3 hypotheses \(\times\) \(2^8\) sequences \(= 768\) super-pebbles. Observing the data \(D\) is then literal pebble conditioning in the grand bag — discard every super-pebble whose outcome part differs from \(D\), rescale the survivors — and the rescaled masses are the posterior probabilities \(P(H_i \mid D)\). Bayes’ theorem is Chapter 6’s discard-and-rescale, executed one level up. The construction is nothing more than the joint distribution over (hypothesis, outcome), and it is the reason Bayesian inference fits inside ordinary Kolmogorov probability (the isomorphism noted in Chapter 6’s footnote on events as propositions); for continuous parameters the same construction goes through with mud in place of pebbles.

Three caveats keep the distinction between the two spaces alive — they are this section’s points in formal dress.

  1. Every hypothesis must bring a likelihood. A super-pebble needs a mass, so each \(H_i\) must specify \(P(x \mid H_i)\) for every outcome. The catch-all “some explanation I have not thought of” specifies no likelihood, receives no pebble, and cannot be embedded. The grand bag gives no protection against an incomplete \(\mathcal{H}\).

  2. The grand bag is frozen together with \(\mathcal{H}\). It is built from the current list of hypotheses. When data force a revision \(\mathcal{H} \to \mathcal{H}'\), the bag is demolished and rebuilt with fresh priors. Any given hypothesis space admits a sample space; there is no once-and-for-all \(\Omega\) containing every hypothesis an analyst might ever introduce.

  3. Even the base \(\Omega\) encodes commitments. “The sum is 13” is impossible only because \(\Omega = \{1,\dots,6\}^2\) was chosen; hand the analyst a trick die with a hidden seventh face and the observed data land outside \(\Omega\), forcing a revision of the sample space itself. The demarcation between the spaces is one of role and degree — \(\Omega\) is chosen once and rarely questioned, \(\mathcal{H}\) is chosen and routinely questioned — and both are conditional on the background information \(I\).

A closing connection: leaving a hypothesis out of \(\mathcal{H}\) is operationally the same as including it with prior probability zero — in both cases no conditioning can revive it, since a zero mass rescales to zero forever. This principle is Cromwell’s rule.16 It is the formal reason revision of the hypothesis space is a genuine step outside Bayes’ theorem, not something conditioning could have done on its own.

8.1.3 Subjective and objective Bayesians: de Finetti and Jaynes

Within the epistemic camp there are two schools, and the difference between them concerns how strongly the available information constrains the prior probability P(A). Both deny that P(A) is a physical property of the world, and both accept the rules of probability that this chapter derives. They part on one question: must two rational agents who hold the same information assign the same probability to P(A)?

The subjective (or personalist) school — Ramsey, de Finetti, Savage17 — answers no. Here P(A) is a personal degree of belief, measured operationally as the price at which the agent is willing to bet on A, and the only requirement that rationality imposes on it is coherence: the agent’s probabilities, taken together, must obey the rules of the calculus, so that no combination of bets the agent accepts as fair produces a guaranteed loss (the Dutch book argument, developed later in this chapter). Coherence constrains the relations between probabilities, not their starting values: two coherent agents may price the same proposition at 0.2 and at 0.6 without either making an error. What the subjectivist offers instead of initial agreement is convergence: agents who begin from different priors and condition on the same accumulating data end up with similar posterior probabilities, provided neither assigned probability zero to possibilities the other takes seriously. Consensus is then an output of inference, not a precondition for it. De Finetti’s representation theorem (1937) supplies the school’s substitute for “objective chance”: if an agent judges a sequence of events exchangeable — the order in which the observations arrive carries no information — then their beliefs are mathematically equivalent to beliefs about an unknown limiting frequency with a prior distribution over its values. The “true probability of heads” can therefore be used as a calculating device by someone who denies that any such quantity exists.

When posteriors need not merge: a point null

The proviso is not a technicality; it settles real cases. Take a single parameter \(\theta\) and the point null \(H_0: \theta = 0\). One analyst places a continuous prior on \(\theta\) — a density, with no point mass anywhere. A second places a spike-and-slab prior: a lump of probability \(\pi_0\) on the single value \(\theta = 0\), with the remaining \(1 - \pi_0\) spread over a continuous slab. These are not two distributions over the same space; they disagree about whether \(\theta = 0\) is a possibility at all. The first assigns the event \(\{\theta = 0\}\) probability zero, the second assigns it \(\pi_0 > 0\).

Bayes’ rule makes every posterior absolutely continuous with respect to its prior, so an event of prior probability zero retains posterior probability zero whatever the data. The first analyst therefore has \(P(H_0 \mid \text{data}) = 0\) at every sample size: the point null is never in play for them, and they would assess it only through an interval around zero, or a region of practical equivalence. The second analyst has a posterior that moves — \(P(H_0 \mid \text{data})\) rises towards \(1\) as data accumulate if \(\theta = 0\) holds and falls towards \(0\) if it does not, the quantity reported as a local false discovery rate, the posterior probability of the null at the observed value. The two assessments of the point null never converge, because the two priors are mutually singular on the event \(\{\theta = 0\}\) itself: each gives zero weight to what, for the other, carries the whole question.18 This is the proviso failing in the most direct way, and the resulting non-convergence is what the convergence result predicts, not a counterexample to it.

The divergence is confined to that one point. On any interval the two posteriors agree in the limit — \(P(|\theta| < \varepsilon \mid \text{data}) \to 1\) under both priors when \(\theta = 0\) is true — and their predictions for future observations merge as the general result requires. What does not merge is the atom, which is the whole of the disagreement. The two analysts are not running one model with different settings; they hold different hypothesis spaces in the sense of the previous section — different sets of entertained alternatives, part of the prior information on which every probability is conditioned. For one, \(\theta = 0\) is a distinguished hypothesis with standing of its own; for the other it is a value no different from any neighbouring one. Which to adopt is a question about the science — whether an effect can be a true zero — and not one the data can settle. The same point mass produces the Jeffreys–Lindley paradox, in which a result significant by a frequentist test can leave the point-null posterior favouring the null as the sample grows.19 It also marks the limit of the convergence consolation: posteriors merge for parameters within a shared model, but a structural commitment — a point mass, a model indicator, the dimension of the model — need not merge, and a conclusion that turns on it stays prior-dependent at every sample size.

The objective school — Jeffreys and Cox before him, Jaynes most explicitly — answers yes. Here probability is the degree of belief that a rational agent ought to hold given specified information, and it is a function of that information alone: two agents with the same information must assign the same P(A), and a disagreement between them indicates either an error or an undisclosed difference in information. The calculus is therefore supplemented with rules for translating information into a unique assignment: symmetry arguments (information that does not distinguish between cases assigns them equal probability — desideratum 3.3 of the next section), invariance arguments (the assignment must not change under a mere relabelling of the problem), and the principle of maximum entropy, which selects, among all distributions compatible with the stated constraints, the one that assumes nothing beyond them.20 “Objective” does not mean physical. The probability still describes a state of knowledge; the claim is that the state of knowledge, fully stated, fixes the number, so that probability assignments become interpersonal in the way that logic is interpersonal. This sets the objective probability against both of the other readings at once: where the frequency interpretation treats the probability as a physical constant to be estimated from data, and the subjectivist treats it as a personal degree of belief the agent is free to assign, the objectivist holds it to be derived — computed from the stated information by a rule, and so neither measured against the world nor chosen at will.

The subjective and objective schools do not weigh rationality on the same scale. For the subjectivist it is coherence: any internally consistent belief is rational, so two agents having the same information may differ on priors and both be beyond reproach; a miscalibrated agent is merely unlucky, not irrational. For the objectivist, rationality includes a second clause — the prior must be the unique function of the information — so systematically different priors from identical information means that one agent has made an inferential error.21

The schools differ on more than the division of labour. The objective programme presupposes that stated information fixes a single prior; the subjectivist holds that in general it does not. The rules for converting information into a distribution — symmetry, invariance, maximum entropy — return a unique answer only relative to further choices that the information does not itself determine, so two analysts who agree on everything they know can still derive different “objective” priors.22 The disagreement the objectivist set out to remove reappears at an earlier step. On this view the subjective position is not the concession that the correct prior is hard to find; it is the claim that, in general, there is no unique correct prior to find.

There is also a positive advantage to the subjective prior, though it must be set against the right target. Maximum entropy is not a device for producing an uninformative prior: it returns the most noncommittal distribution consistent with the stated constraints, and where those constraints encode substantial knowledge — a known mean, a conservation law, a symmetry — the resulting prior is informative to the same degree. The Jaynesian objectivist therefore uses subject-matter information as fully as the subjectivist does; the two part on whether that information fixes a unique distribution, not on whether it is used. The subjectivist’s advantage over this position is one of expression: an elicited prior can carry partial, qualitative, or comparative knowledge — earlier studies, biological limits, the range a quantity has taken in comparable settings — that does not reduce to the moment constraints and symmetry groups the formal rules require, and it does not presuppose that a unique prior exists. The prior so obtained is also the one a coherent agent’s own preferences already imply (the Savage construction, below). Its cost is that it must be defended rather than read from a rule, which is the work of elicitation, examined below.

A different practice also travels under the name “objective”, and should not be run together with the Jaynesian programme. Reference priors and the weakly informative default priors of applied Bayesian work are constructed to limit the information the prior contributes, so that the data dominate and an analysis is reproducible between investigators.23 Their justification is pragmatic — regularisation, stable computation, a sensible prior predictive distribution — rather than the claim that information fixes the prior; “noninformative” is in any case a misnomer, since a prior flat in one parametrisation is informative in another, the difficulty already noted. These default priors occupy a position between the two schools: with the subjectivist they accept that no unique correct prior exists, and with the objectivist they try to keep the analyst’s particular beliefs out of the result.

The disagreement has practical consequences at one point in the workflow: the prior (Chapter 9 and Chapter 10). The subjectivist elicits a prior — it records what someone actually believes, and its defence is that it has been stated in public and can be varied. The objectivist derives a prior — from invariance or maximum entropy24 — and its defence is that anyone accepting the same constraints obtains the same distribution. Neither programme closes the gap completely. The objectivist must still choose the constraints, the parametrisation, and the reference measure before the formal machinery can run, so judgement enters one step earlier rather than not at all. The subjectivist working in science cannot in practice hold an arbitrary prior, because a published prior must survive the criticism of colleagues and sensitivity analysis. This book takes the working position on which the two schools converge: probability is epistemic; assignments should follow from stated information as far as the information goes; and the prior is a declared component of the model, open to criticism and revision like every other component.

Elicitation: the subjective prior made public

Elicitation is the procedure the subjective programme uses to build a prior: a structured interview that converts what an expert knows into a probability distribution, with steps designed to counter the cognitive biases catalogued earlier — overconfidence, anchoring — and to check that the expert’s stated quantiles are mutually coherent.25 The first of those biases sets the size of the problem the procedure exists to solve: asked directly for an interval at a stated confidence level, experts and laypeople alike produce one far too narrow — nominal 90% ranges contain the truth 30% to 60% of the time, and the stated level barely affects the width (Chapter 2). An elicited prior taken at face value is therefore tighter than the expert means it to be, which is why the elicitation literature insists on formats that ask for the bounds first and the confidence afterwards. It takes the personalist definition of probability at face value: the prior is someone’s degree of belief, and elicitation makes that belief explicit, recorded, and open to revision.

Pooling several experts can look like a passage from the subjective to the objective, and in one sense it is. It raises the intersubjectivity of the prior — agreement across assessors — without making the prior a function of the evidence alone. A consensus prior is still a subjective prior; it is the degree of belief of a group rather than of one person. The two senses of “objective” are distinct: elicitation can deliver agreement, not evidence-determination. Aggregation also introduces choices of its own. Combining distributions by a weighted average (linear pooling) and by a weighted geometric mean (logarithmic pooling) give different results with different properties, and the weights assigned to experts are a further judgement; the subjectivity is relocated, not removed.26

There is nonetheless a real gain. If the experts’ errors are partly independent, their combination can be better calibrated than any single assessment — the averaging effect that also improves a forecast built from many models. This is objectivity in the calibration sense, agreement with observed frequencies, obtained empirically, and it is the sense in which a pooled prior may be “more objective” than its parts. A well-run elicitation represents the experts’ disagreement rather than concealing it: wide disagreement should yield a wide prior, which a later sensitivity analysis can probe. Elicitation is in this way the working compromise between the two schools — a subjective prior made public, checkable, and often better calibrated, at the price of new judgements about whom to consult and how to combine them.

QBism: subjective probability where chance seemed safest

The discussion of a stochastic world earlier in this chapter allowed one candidate for irreducible, physical chance: quantum mechanics. There is a school of physics that removes the exception. QBism (from “quantum Bayesianism”), proposed by Caves, Fuchs, and Schack and developed by Fuchs, Mermin, and Schack, interprets the probabilities of quantum theory — including those delivered by the Born rule, the rule that converts a quantum state into outcome probabilities27 — as an agent’s personal degrees of belief, in de Finetti’s sense, about the outcomes of her own future measurements.28 On this reading the quantum state is not a property of the physical system but a representation of the agent’s information about it, their personal degrees of belief about the consequences of their own actions on the world. The Born rule is an additional coherence requirement: a constraint, supplementing the Dutch book standard, that a rational agent’s betting odds on measurement outcomes must satisfy. “Collapse” is just Bayesian updating on a new experience; there’s no observer-independent fact about an outcome. The probability that a given nucleus decays within the hour becomes, on the QBist reading, epistemic like every other probability.
QBism goes further than de Finetti’s programme applied to classical problems – it is arguably the most thoroughgoing application of de Finettian personalism ever attempted. For de Finetti, the probability is personal but the underlying fact — whether it rains tomorrow — is agent-independent; in QBism even a measurement outcome is an experience of the agent who makes it. The standard objection is that this amounts to instrumentalism: a theory that organises an agent’s expectations without attempting to describe the world. QBists reply that the position is a realism of an unusual kind, which Fuchs calls participatory realism: the world is real and constrains the agent’s experience; other agents are real; and the fact that the Born rule — this rule and not some other — is the coherence norm that succeeds empirically is an objective discovery about the world, which the quantum formalism therefore characterises indirectly.29 Whether this reply secures enough realism is an open dispute. QBism is a minority interpretation among physicists; it is included here not as settled physics but as evidence of reach: the subjective programme can be stated coherently even in the domain where objective chance appeared unavoidable.

8.2 Road 1: probability as the logic of belief

8.2.1 The robot returns

In Chapter 3 the robot had two design specifications: bivalence (every proposition is TRUE or FALSE) and truth-functionality (a compound’s value depends only on its parts’ values). We now weaken the first — not about the world, but about the robot’s information. Propositions are still either true or false; the robot, however, usually does not know which, and must grade its confidence instead of declaring a verdict. The question is what rules the graded confidence must follow.

We define rationality narrowly as the best way of converting evidence into beliefs, and beliefs into decisions. Probability theory deals with the first part of this definition. Philosophically speaking, one could think of probability theory as a model of rational thinking, rather than the real thing, thus admitting that there might well be more to rationality than is captured by it, while stressing that probability theory does capture an important part of what it means to be rational.

How would you describe the thinking of a narrowly rational robot? What would we want to be the basic rules of reasoning, or desiderata, of such a robot, which we could then formalise into a mathematical description of thinking under uncertainty? In other words, can we present a full list of norms of common sense, from which we can derive a rational thinking machine?30 These rules should be such that a rational person would not want to consciously violate any of them.

The robot reasons about unambiguous bivalent propositions, which have truth values (but we don’t need to actually know, whether a proposition is true or false, it suffices that it in principle has this truth-property).31 So it cannot help with much of ordinary human communication, to which unchanging truth values cannot be assigned.

8.2.2 The desiderata

The robot reasons by assigning degrees of plausibility to propositions, based on evidence given to it. Each piece of new evidence leads to shifting of these plausibilities. The desiderata:

  1. Degrees of plausibility are represented by real numbers. We represent a greater plausibility with a greater number.

  2. Qualitative correspondence with common sense — if the robot gets information that supports hypothesis A, then the plausibility of A increases, and vice versa.

  3. Consistency:

    3.1. If a conclusion can be reasoned in many ways, then each must lead to the same result.

    3.2. The principle of total information — all relevant information must be taken into account when shifting plausibilities. Also note that while causal physical influences propagate only forward in time, logical information works in both directions, so it does not matter when the evidence was collected in relation to the phenomenon it describes.

    3.3. Equivalent states of knowledge are represented by equivalent plausibility numbers.

Surprisingly, these rules are enough to ensure a rational brain. Mathematical probability theory is fully consistent with these desiderata and requires no more than them.

8.2.3 The rules the desiderata force

Cox proved that any system of graded plausibilities satisfying the desiderata can be rescaled, without loss of information, into numbers between 0 and 1 that obey two rules:32

  • The sum rule. \(P(A) + P(\lnot A) = 1\). The plausibilities of a proposition and its negation are not independent quantities; fixing one fixes the other.

  • The product rule. \(P(A \land B) = P(A) \cdot P(B \mid A)\). The plausibility that both A and B are true is the plausibility of A, multiplied by the plausibility of B on the assumption that A is true. The quantity \(P(B \mid A)\) is the conditional probability of B given A.

The proof works by turning each desideratum into a functional equation and solving for the only functions that satisfy them; it is beyond the mathematical level of this book, and we take the result as given. What matters here is its shape: the rules of probability are not one convention among many. Given the desiderata, any calculus of graded belief either is probability theory (after rescaling) or violates one of the desiderata somewhere. This is the sense in which probability theory is the unique consistent extension of the propositional logic of Chapter 3 to reasoning under uncertainty. The qualifier is not decoration: the extension inherits that logic’s expressive ceiling along with its rules, a point taken up in the section on the robot’s limits below.

8.2.4 No sure loss: the betting road to the same rules

The desiderata route may look abstract, so we now reach the same two rules by the operational argument promised in Chapter 3. There, an assertion was a contract: a penalty of one unit, payable if the asserted proposition turns out false, and inconsistency meant a guaranteed loss. The generalisation under uncertainty replaces all-or-nothing assertion with a graded commitment: your probability for A is the price \(P(A)\), between 0 and 1, at which you are willing to trade a ticket that pays 1 unit if A turns out true and 0 if it turns out false. A price of 1 reproduces full assertion, a price of 0 full denial; the prices in between are the new territory. A set of prices is coherent when no combination of trades, each acceptable at your stated prices, produces a guaranteed loss.

Suppose you price a ticket on A at 0.70 and a ticket on NOT A at 0.50. The prices sum to 1.20, and a bookie sells you both tickets (Table 8.4):

Show code 
tf_money <- function(x) sprintf("%+.2f", x)
buy_A    <- c(1 - 0.70, 0 - 0.70)   # pay 0.70; ticket pays 1 if A true
buy_notA <- c(0 - 0.50, 1 - 0.50)   # pay 0.50; ticket pays 1 if A false
tibble(world = c("A true", "A false"),
       `buy ticket on A at 0.70` = tf_money(buy_A),
       `buy ticket on NOT A at 0.50` = tf_money(buy_notA),
       `net payoff` = tf_money(buy_A + buy_notA)) %>%
  kable()

Whichever way the world turns out, one ticket pays and the other does not: you collect 1 unit, having paid 1.20 — a guaranteed loss of 0.20, which is the amount by which your prices violate the sum rule. Had the prices summed to less than 1, the bookie would have bought both tickets from you instead. Only prices with \(P(A) + P(\lnot A) = 1\) leave no guaranteed loss. This is the penalty table of Chapter 3 with graded stakes; the sure-loss argument is the same, and only the prices have become adjustable.

The product rule is forced by the called-off bet, foreshadowed in the historical aside of Chapter 3. A conditional ticket on “B given A” at price \(q\) pays 1 unit if A and B are both true, pays nothing if A is true and B false, and is called off — the price \(q\) is refunded — if A is false. The called-off bet is the operational meaning of conditional probability: a commitment about B that is only activated when A turns out true.

Now compare the conditional ticket with a portfolio of unconditional tickets. In every possible world, the conditional ticket’s payoff equals that of holding one ticket on “A AND B” plus \(q\) tickets on “NOT A”. Check the three cases: if A and B are both true, the joint ticket pays 1 and the NOT-A tickets pay nothing; if A is true and B false, every position pays nothing; if A is false, the refund \(q\) equals the payoff of the \(q\) NOT-A tickets. Two positions with identical payoffs in every world must have the same price, otherwise a bookie trades the dear one against the cheap one and pockets the difference risk-free. Suppose your prices are \(P(A) = 0.5\) and \(P(A \land B) = 0.2\), so the matching conditional price is \(0.2 + q \cdot 0.5\), i.e. \(q = 0.4\) — but you price the conditional ticket at \(q = 0.6\):

Show code 
q <- 0.6; p_A <- 0.5; p_AB <- 0.2
buy_cond   <- c(1 - q, 0 - q, q - q)                    # refund row: pay q, get q back
sell_joint <- c(p_AB - 1, p_AB - 0, p_AB - 0)           # receive 0.20; pay 1 if A and B
sell_notA  <- q * c((1 - p_A) - 0, (1 - p_A) - 0, (1 - p_A) - 1)  # q tickets at 0.50 each
tibble(world = c("A and B", "A and not-B", "not-A"),
       `buy "B given A" at 0.60` = tf_money(buy_cond),
       `sell "A AND B" at 0.20` = tf_money(sell_joint),
       `sell 0.6 tickets on "NOT A" at 0.50 each` = tf_money(sell_notA),
       `net payoff` = tf_money(buy_cond + sell_joint + sell_notA)) %>%
  kable()

The loss is 0.10 in every row (Table 8.5) — the gap between your conditional price (\(0.6 \times 0.5 = 0.30\) per unit stake on A) and your direct price for the joint event (0.20). Coherence requires \(P(B \mid A) \cdot P(A) = P(A \land B)\), which is the product rule. Taken together: a person whose graded commitments violate the sum rule or the product rule can be made to lose money with certainty, by a finite set of trades each of which they consider fair. This result is the Dutch book argument.33 Inconsistency is a sure loss under certainty (Chapter 3); incoherence is a sure loss under uncertainty.

8.2.5 Promises kept: the syllogisms

The logic chapter, Chapter 3, required the expanded logic to reduce to classical logic under certainty and to license the weak syllogisms. Both promises can now be kept with the two rules in hand.

Reduction. Set the probabilities to their extremes. If \(P(B \mid A) = 1\) and \(P(A) = 1\), the product rule gives \(P(A \land B) = 1\), so \(P(B) = 1\): modus ponens. If \(P(B \mid A) = 1\) and \(P(B) = 0\), then \(P(A \land B) = 0\), and since \(P(A \land B) = P(A) \cdot 1\), we get \(P(A) = 0\): modus tollens. At the endpoints 0 and 1 the calculus is the propositional logic of Chapter 3.

The weak syllogisms. Suppose A entails B, so that \(P(B \mid A) = 1\), and suppose \(0 < P(A)\) and \(P(B) < 1\). Writing the product rule in both directions, \(P(A \mid B) \cdot P(B) = P(A \land B) = P(A) \cdot P(B \mid A) = P(A)\), so

\[P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A)}{P(B)} \geq P(A).\]

Observing B raises the plausibility of A, and by a definite factor, \(1/P(B)\): the less expected the evidence, the larger the boost. The first weak syllogism is now a theorem, with a number attached. The second — learning NOT A makes B less plausible — follows by a similar two-line argument (Exercise 2). Chapter 9 develops the bookkeeping of such evidence shifts (the likelihood ratio as the weight of evidence) into the engine of Bayesian inference.

The fundamental principle of all probabilistic inference: to judge the truth of proposition A, calculate the probability that A is true, conditional on all the evidence on hand: \(P(A \vert E_1E_2…)\).

8.2.6 What the desiderata do not force

Both derivations establish the same rules — the sum rule, the product rule, and Bayes’ theorem built from them — and they stop there. They fix the relations a set of probabilities must satisfy, not the values placed into those relations. This is where the two epistemic schools described earlier divide, and the division can be located in the desiderata themselves. The calculus follows from desideratum 3.1 — a conclusion reached by several routes must come out the same — together with 1 and 2; a subjectivist grants these without reservation, because they govern the consistency of an assignment and not its starting point. Desideratum 3.3 is the additional clause. Read narrowly, as the requirement that one agent not attach different numbers to relabelled versions of a single problem, it is uncontroversial. Read strongly, as the requirement that any two agents with the same information attach the same numbers, it is the objectivist uniqueness thesis, and it is what licenses the symmetry and maximum-entropy arguments of the objective school. The strong reading is detachable from the rest: adopting the robot commits a reader to the calculus, not to objectivism. Whether the strong form can be met is the open question raised earlier — symmetry and maximum entropy do not single out a prior without a further choice of parametrisation and reference measure.

Two grades of desideratum 3.3

There are two grades of the consistency requirement. The coherence grade says that logically equivalent propositions receive the same number; violating it is a Dutch book, so both schools accept it, and because a Dutch book is made against one person’s book, never against a pair of people, coherence is a one-person predicate. The indifference grade says that symmetric information leads to equal probabilities — information being symmetric under a transformation when the transformation maps the hypothesis space onto itself and leaves the state of knowledge \(I\) invariant. This is Jaynes’s bridge to maximum entropy, and the next section shows that it is contestable even for one agent; read across agents, it would force agreement. The subjectivist seeks agreement between agents as an instrumental good — a committee must act on one distribution, results should be reproducible, pooling partly independent judgements improves calibration — and reaches it in two ways: convergence by conditioning on shared data, in which the prior washes out, and elicitation or pooling, which is a negotiated consensus. Disagreement is then a coordination inconvenience and a signal to collect more data, not a defect of rationality; the subjectivist can call a badly reasoned prior poorly calibrated or unwise, but reserves “irrational” for incoherence. Many find that taxonomy too thin, while the objectivists make agreement a norm and cannot supply the unique prior the norm requires. The impasse is what drives some past the dichotomy altogether, replacing subjective and objective with virtues both camps can own: transparency about assumptions, calibration, robustness.34

8.2.7 Symmetric information and the limits of indifference

The symmetry argument needs a precise account of what “symmetric information” is. The principle of indifference, in its crude form, says: if there is no reason to favour one possibility over another, assign them equal probability. Keynes and others found this embarrassing, because “no reason to favour” is vague and generates contradictions. Jaynes’s contribution was to replace the crude version with a precise, group-theoretic one, and to derive it rather than postulate it.

Information is symmetric with respect to a transformation when (i) the transformation permutes the possibilities, mapping the hypothesis space onto itself, and (ii) the state of knowledge \(I\) is left invariant by it — after relabelling, the problem is word-for-word the same, and nothing in \(I\) distinguishes the original from the transformed version. The consistency desideratum 3.3 then forces the conclusion: if a transformation carries possibility \(A\) into possibility \(B\) while leaving \(I\) unchanged, \(P(A \mid I) = P(B \mid I)\). If the transformations form a group that can carry any outcome into any other, every outcome receives the same probability, and the uniform distribution follows. The principle of indifference becomes a theorem about invariant information, not a bare postulate.

Two extensions complete the bridge to maximum entropy:

  • For a continuous parameter, “equal probability for related cases” becomes “a prior invariant under the group.” A location parameter, for which the problem is unchanged by shifting the origin, forces a uniform prior; a scale parameter, unchanged by multiplication by a positive constant, forces \(p(\sigma) \propto 1/\sigma\). These are Jaynes’s transformation-group priors.
  • With only partial symmetry — some constraints, such as a known mean, but no full invariance — maximum entropy generalises the construction: take the distribution that is as non-committal as possible subject to the constraints, measured against the invariant base. With no constraints it returns the uniform answer; with constraints, the least-committal distribution consistent with them.

The chain is therefore: symmetric information, through desideratum 3.3, gives equal probabilities; transformation groups extend this to continuous parameters; maximum entropy extends it again to the constrained case.

The construction is contestable even for one actor, because it depends on naming the right transformation, and what counts as “symmetric” is relative to how the problem is parametrised. A state of knowledge that is invariant in one parametrisation is not invariant in another, because a non-linear change of variable distorts the measure (the Jacobian is not constant). “I know nothing about \(\theta\)” gives a uniform prior on \(\theta\); but the same ignorance equally licenses a uniform prior on \(\theta^2\) or on \(\log\theta\), and these are different distributions. Nothing in the phrase “I have no information” fixes the coordinate in which ignorance is to equal uniformity. No second agent is needed: one agent, contemplating one state of ignorance, finds that “symmetric information” points to several incompatible priors according to the description chosen.

The cleanest illustration is van Fraassen’s cube factory.35 A factory makes cubes with side length somewhere in \([0,1]\), and nothing further is known. What is \(P(\text{side} \le \tfrac12)\)?

  • Indifference on side length: \(P = \tfrac12\).
  • Indifference on face area (also ranging over \([0,1]\)): side \(\le \tfrac12 \Leftrightarrow\) area \(\le \tfrac14\), giving \(P = \tfrac14\).
  • Indifference on volume (\([0,1]\)): side \(\le \tfrac12 \Leftrightarrow\) volume \(\le \tfrac18\), giving \(P = \tfrac18\).

Three monotone reparametrisations of the same quantity, the same ignorance, three answers (\(\tfrac12\), \(\tfrac14\), \(\tfrac18\)); the principle of indifference does not choose among them. Bertrand’s problem, in the box below, makes the same point in a geometric setting.

Bertrand’s paradox: ‘no information’ has no single answer

The symmetry argument assumes that “symmetric information” picks out one distribution; it need not. Bertrand’s problem asks for the probability that a chord drawn “at random” in a circle of radius \(R\) is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle, which has length \(R\sqrt{3}\). A chord at distance \(d\) from the centre has length \(2\sqrt{R^2 - d^2}\), so it is longer than the side just when \(d < R/2\), and every construction below agrees on that. They disagree on what “at random” means. Drawing the two endpoints uniformly on the circumference gives \(1/3\); placing the midpoint uniformly along a radius gives \(1/2\); placing the midpoint uniformly over the area of the disk gives \(1/4\). Each is a uniform distribution, but over a different parametrisation of the space of chords, and because the maps between those parametrisations are non-linear, uniform in one is not uniform in another.36

So “no information favouring any chord” does not fix a probability; it fixes one only after a parametrisation has been chosen, and indifference does not choose it. Jaynes argued that the problem becomes well posed once the answer is required to be invariant under the full group a physical chord-generating process would respect — rotation, translation, and rescaling — which singles out the \(1/2\) solution. The rescue works, and in doing so makes the point: uniqueness is recovered by adding symmetries that the bare problem does not state, so “symmetric information” determines a prior only relative to a group that must itself be supplied. Maximum entropy inherits the same dependence, since on a continuous space it is defined only against a base measure — a stipulation of what counts as uniform.

8.3 The two roads meet

A small example shows the two roads computing the same number by different routes. A clinic has 10 patients, of whom 3 carry an infection; 2 patients are selected at random for a follow-up visit. What is the probability that both selected patients are carriers?

Road 2 (counting). Count the equally possible cases: there are \(\binom{10}{2} = 45\) ways to select 2 patients out of 10, of which \(\binom{3}{2} = 3\) select 2 of the 3 carriers. The probability is \(3/45 = 1/15\).

Road 1 (the robot). The robot updates plausibilities sequentially with the product rule. Before the first selection, its information is symmetric over the 10 patients, so desideratum 3.3 (equivalent states of knowledge receive equivalent plausibilities) forces \(P(\text{first is a carrier}) = 3/10\). On the assumption that the first selected patient is a carrier, the same symmetry over the remaining 9 patients forces \(P(\text{second is a carrier} \mid \text{first is}) = 2/9\). The product rule gives \(3/10 \times 2/9 = 1/15\).

A Monte Carlo simulation arbitrates (Table 8.6):

Show code 
set.seed(2024)
counting <- choose(3, 2) / choose(10, 2)
robot    <- (3 / 10) * (2 / 9)
mc       <- mean(replicate(1e5,
              sum(sample(rep(c(1, 0), c(3, 7)), 2)) == 2))
tibble(route = c("Road 2: counting the cases",
                 "Road 1: product rule, sequential update",
                 "Monte Carlo (100,000 draws)"),
       `P(both carriers)` = round(c(counting, robot, mc), 4)) %>%

The agreement is not a coincidence, and it shows where counting fits inside the belief reading: counting is what the robot’s calculus reduces to when the available information is symmetric over a finite set of cases. Desideratum 3.3 converts “I have no reason to distinguish the cases” into equal probabilities, and from there the favourable-over-possible ratio of the classical definition follows. The counting road is, on this view, a special case — but a special case of enormous practical importance, because experiments are deliberately built (by randomisation) to make information symmetric. Chapter 6 took the counting road to the binomial distribution.

8.4 The same calculus? The fine print

The two roads agree on the working rules — sum rule, product rule, and everything derived from them. The book uses the rules freely from either direction. The agreement nevertheless has fine print, and stating it is part of the deal.

First, the roads disagree about what carries probability. On the belief road, probabilities attach to propositions, given a state of knowledge; it is meaningful to ask for the probability of a single event — that this patient dies within a week, that it rains tomorrow — and the section on understanding uncertainty above already used probabilities this way. On the counting road, probability is most naturally a property of a repeatable setup: a ratio over a sample space of cases. A single-case probability then needs an auxiliary device (a hypothetical infinite population of tomorrows, of which a fraction are rainy), and the weather example earlier in this chapter shows how strained that device can become. The two roads can price the same calculations while telling different stories about what was calculated.

Second, the formal agreement is not a perfect overlap. Cox’s theorem requires technical assumptions beyond the verbal desiderata (notably that plausibilities vary smoothly enough for the functional equations to have unique solutions); making the argument fully rigorous is its own research literature.37 And the agreement covers Kolmogorov’s axioms only in their finite form: the belief-road arguments — desiderata and Dutch book alike — force additivity over finitely many mutually exclusive alternatives, but do not force Kolmogorov’s countable additivity, the extension to infinite collections that underpins the measure-theoretic limit theorems. De Finetti, the author of the Dutch book argument in its developed form, regarded countable additivity as a useful convention rather than a requirement of coherence. For everything done in this book — finite data sets, finite simulations, finite parameter grids — the distinction has no practical consequence, and we will not mention it again.

The position taken in this book is the one already implicit in this chapter’s opening: probability is epistemic — degrees of belief constrained to be coherent — and counting and long-run frequency are the special cases that arise when information is symmetric or data are plentiful. When the two readings both apply, they agree; when only one applies (single cases, unique hypotheses, parameters that have one true value), the belief reading keeps working. That is the practical reason this book is Bayesian, ahead of any philosophical one.

8.5 The robot as a universal reasoner, and its limits

The robot handles its information consistently, and Cox’s theorem says it does so in the only way that is consistent. It is natural to push the construction to its limit and ask what it would become. Give the robot a prior over every hypothesis it could state, the rules of Chapter 6 to update that prior, and unlimited time to compute. Do we then obtain a perfect thinking machine — a universal reasoner, in the sense in which a Turing machine or von Neumann’s stored-program computer is a universal calculator, able to reach any thought that inference can reach?

The limit is a definite mathematical object. A prior over all computable hypotheses, each weighted by \(2^{-K(h)}\) — where \(K(h)\), the Kolmogorov complexity of \(h\), is the length of the shortest program that generates it — is Solomonoff’s universal prior; conditionalising it on data is Solomonoff induction, and its decision-theoretic extension to an agent that also acts is AIXI (Hutter).38 The weighting is Occam’s razor written as a probability distribution: a simpler hypothesis, having a shorter description, receives more prior mass. The optimality is not merely asserted — this predictor provably dominates every computable predictor and converges on the truth, in a precise sense, faster than any of them. So the intuition that an optimal updating rule fed rich enough information yields the best possible inference is not naive; it is a theorem about a specific object. The universal thinker exists, as mathematics.

The same theory that defines it also states three things that dismantle “perfect thinking machine”, and they fail at different depths. A fourth objection arrives from a different direction — from logic rather than from computability — and bears on the robot in its everyday working form, not only on its idealised limit.

1. Coherence-optimal is not truth-optimal. Cox guarantees that the robot’s handling of information is consistent — that it cannot be Dutch-booked, that it extends Boolean logic uniquely. It guarantees nothing about convergence to the truth. A perfectly coherent Bayesian working with a misspecified model updates optimally, confidently, and coherently towards the wrong answer: when the truth lies outside the hypothesis space, the posterior concentrates not on the truth but on the pseudo-true value — the member of the model class closest to reality in Kullback–Leibler divergence, the information-theoretic measure of how far one distribution is from another.39 “Handles its information in the best possible way” is true, but silently indexed to the model class the robot was given. The optimality is conditional, and the condition is supplied from outside the calculus.

2. Convergence presupposes that the truth is already in the space. The results that vindicate “rich data washes out the prior” — posterior consistency, the Bernstein–von Mises theorem, and the merging of opinions cited earlier in the point-null discussion — each require the true data-generating process to lie in the support of the prior (for merging, that the two priors be mutually absolutely continuous).40 Cromwell’s rule, met in the hypothesis-space section of this chapter, states the barrier in one line: a hypothesis assigned prior probability zero keeps posterior probability zero whatever the evidence. So “if the information is rich enough” already presupposes the hard part. Conditionalisation is a horizontal operation: it redistributes probability within a fixed set of possibilities. It contains no rule for the vertical move of enlarging that set — of inventing “germ”, “natural selection”, or “the curvature of spacetime”, concepts that were not points in the prior space at all. This is the problem of new hypotheses in confirmation theory, met at small scale in the alternating-coin example: neither Bayes’ rule nor Cox’s desiderata contain a procedure for extending their own space of possibilities.41 Run forever on rich data, the ideal machine finds only the best hypothesis it could already state.

3. The universal-computer analogy imports the wrong universality — and the right one cannot be built. Turing and von Neumann universality concerns what is computable given a program, in principle, ignoring resources. Two things must be dropped to get from there to a universal thinker. The program is exogenous — it is the analogue of the prior, so universality relocates the question of which thought to think rather than answering it — and universality says nothing about tractability. Here the conclusion is decisive: Solomonoff induction is uncomputable, because Kolmogorov complexity is uncomputable (evaluating it reduces to the halting problem); AIXI is uncomputable; and exact Bayesian inference over a rich hypothesis space is intractable even where it is computable, the evidence integral being #P-hard or worse.42 The moment “perfect universal Bayesian thinker” is made precise, the proof of its optimality arrives bundled with a proof that no physical machine can be it. The gap between a real reasoner and the ideal is therefore not an engineering shortfall to be closed; it is a theorem. Every actual thinker is a resource-bounded approximation to an uncomputable ideal.

4. The calculus grades propositions; it cannot express relations among objects. The first three limits concern what the robot can compute and what its prior can reach. This one concerns what its language can say, and it is visible in the derivation rather than in the ideal. Both routes to the calculus take a Boolean algebra of propositions as given and define probability as a function on it: the calculus supplies the grading, and the algebra supplies everything else. A Boolean algebra is precisely the algebraic content of the propositional logic of Chapter 3, so the robot inherits that logic’s expressive ceiling along with its rules. Propositional logic cannot reason from a generalisation to an instance — which is why the Socrates syllogism had to be handled one level down in Chapter 3 — and neither, by itself, can probability theory.

Notation hides the gap. Write \(P(\text{disease} \mid \text{exposure}) = 0.4\) and the intended reading is a generalisation: any person who is exposed has probability 0.4 of being diseased. The formalism contains no “any”. To probability theory, “exposure” is an opaque label for an event that either obtains or does not; it cannot look inside the label to see that a person is being quantified over, still less that two or three distinct individuals stand in a relation. Predicate logic supplies what is missing, with quantifiers and bound variables — \(\forall x: P(\text{disease}(x) \mid \text{exposure}(x)) = 0.4\) — where the binding of \(x\) does work the vertical bar cannot do. Most of that expression is logic; only the \(P\) is probability.43 Statistical practice has always known this without saying it, because the missing quantifier is written down somewhere else: the for (i in 1:N) loop in a model file is a universal quantifier, the plate in a graphical model is that quantifier drawn as a box, and the subscript in \(y_i \sim \text{Normal}(\mu_i, \sigma)\) binds a variable over units. None of this is in the sum and product rules, and none of it is derivable from them. Formalising the combination is a research field in its own right, in which measures over the sentences of a first-order language have been constructed and relational probabilistic languages are in practical use; what the field lacks is not a system but a canonical one with tolerable complexity.44

Two consequences matter for this book. The first is a warning about diagnosis: when a probabilistic analysis goes wrong, the error is often not in the probability. The arithmetic is right and the software ran, and what failed was the logical bookkeeping — which unit, which time point, which contrast, quantified how — carried out informally because the calculus offered nowhere to write it down. The second is that the structure Part III adds is precisely the structure the calculus lacks. A causal diagram is not a function of the joint distribution: distinct graphs can imply the same joint distribution and different consequences under intervention, so the graph carries information the probabilities do not contain (Chapter 12). Potential-outcome notation is predicate notation, \(Y_i(a)\) taking a unit and a treatment value as its arguments, and its central assumption is an explicitly quantified sentence — for every unit \(i\), if \(A_i = a\) then \(Y_i = Y_i(a)\) (Chapter 11). On this reading Part III is not an application of the robot’s calculus but a supplement to it, supplying in a second formalism the relational structure that graded belief cannot state. The robot is the right account of how evidence should move belief; it was never an account of how to say what the belief is about.

Two further limits are more specific but equally real. The first is logical omniscience. Classical Bayesianism assumes the agent already assigns probability 1 to every logical truth — that it knows all consequences of its own beliefs and has proved every theorem its axioms imply. No bounded reasoner is like this: we do not know whether Goldbach’s conjecture follows from the axioms of arithmetic, and a machine reasoning about unproved mathematics must be genuinely uncertain about claims that are either necessarily true or necessarily false. This logical uncertainty is a gap in the Cox–Jaynes ideal, which leaves no room for being unsure whether a conjecture is a theorem; recent work tries to supply the missing piece.45 The second limit returns to causation. Even unlimited passive observation leaves causal structure underdetermined: a passive updater conditionalising on given data cannot climb from association to intervention (Part III), so “rich enough information” must mean the right kind of information — data from interventions — which a pure observer does not generate for itself. This is why the universal agent AIXI must act and experiment, not merely predict; and it raises a question probability theory does not answer, of which, among the infinitely many true propositions a machine could compute, are worth computing. Bayes says how to update a degree of belief; it does not say which question deserves the effort.

Modern AI systems sharpen these limits rather than escape them. A large pretrained model is arguably richer than the bare robot, because pretraining installs an enormous implicit hypothesis space, and in-context learning behaves like Bayesian model-averaging over it.46 But the space is fixed at pretraining: the model cannot place posterior weight on a concept absent from what it learned, and it remains a resource-bounded approximation to the uncomputable ideal. The wall has moved outward; it has not disappeared. The forecasting networks met earlier in this chapter make the same point from the other side — a trained model’s \(P(\text{rain})\) is the forecaster’s distribution, learned from past data, not a property of tomorrow’s atmosphere.

The cleanest statement of the whole result is this. The robot is the ideal engine for moving through a space of hypotheses; it is not the faculty that makes the space. Probability theory governs updating within a stated set of possibilities — which is why this book treats the prior and the hypothesis space as declared components of a model, open to criticism and revision like any other component. (The point-null example earlier in this chapter is the same fact in miniature: two analysts whose hypothesis spaces differ over a single value need never converge, however much data arrives.) The strongest objection to the distinction is that a sufficiently rich universal prior — Solomonoff’s, over all computable hypotheses — already contains every conceivable hypothesis as a point of non-zero mass, so that discovery collapses into ordinary updating within an already-universal space. The objection is correct about the mathematics, and that is precisely why it matters that the object which makes it correct is the uncomputable one: the completeness that would dissolve the problem of discovery is available only in the machine that provably cannot be built.

8.6 Challenges to the Bayesian position

The position this chapter has taken — probability is epistemic, the prior is a declared component of the model open to criticism, and counting and frequency are the special cases that arise when information is symmetric or data are plentiful — has been attacked from several directions, and stating it fairly means setting the attacks out. Appendix H does so at length; in brief, there are four.

  • A single-event probability “says nothing.” As a claim about meaning this imports the frequency definition and then notes that a single case has no frequency, which assumes the conclusion. The real point concerns evaluation: one forecast cannot be checked against one outcome, and a forecaster’s probabilities are validated over a whole record by their calibration (Chapter 2).
  • Radical uncertainty. Knight, Keynes and their successors hold that for many real questions no prior can be set at all. Savage’s representation theorem answers that a coherent agent’s preferences already contain a prior — but Ellsberg’s urn exhibits stable, widely shared preferences for known odds that no single prior represents, a violation of the sure-thing principle that survives reflection.
  • Sets of priors. The response that keeps the calculus carries a set of priors rather than one — imprecise probability, or robust Bayes — and reports lower and upper probabilities, with a decision rule such as maxmin expected utility. Its price is dilation: with a set of priors, conditioning on an observation can widen an interval rather than narrow it, and one cannot claim both deep ignorance and immunity from dilation.
  • Is ambiguity aversion irrational? It resists the reflection that dissolves the biases of Chapter 2, and it has normative axiomatisations of its own, so it is better read as a rival standard than as a mistake.

None of these refutes the calculus; each bounds the scope of one way of using it. For the work in this book — finite data, models with declared structure, priors that must survive criticism and sensitivity analysis — a single prior, varied and checked, is adequate, and the apparatus of sets of priors is held in reserve for the cases, flagged where they arise, in which a single prior would assert more than is known. The aim is unchanged from the chapter’s first page: to quantify the uncertainty that can be quantified, and to mark plainly the uncertainty that cannot.

8.8 Key points

  • The calculus is forced twice over, by two independent routes. Chapter 6 reached the sum and product rules by counting; this chapter reaches the same rules from the robot’s desiderata (Cox) and from the requirement of no sure loss on graded commitments (the Dutch book). That counting and consistency deliver one calculus is the main reason to take it seriously as the logic of science.
  • The chapter’s first half concerns what the number means: probability is read as epistemic (a degree of belief) rather than aleatoric (a physical property of a repeatable set-up), because the epistemic reading keeps working for single cases and unique hypotheses where the frequency reading falls silent.
  • Probability attaches to propositions, so it is always the two-place \(P(A \mid I)\); reading the number as a property of the world is the mind-projection fallacy. The sample space is fixed by the experiment; the hypothesis space \(\mathcal{H}\) — the set of explanations the analyst entertains, each a probability law over \(\Omega\) rather than an event in it — is fixed by the analyst, and every posterior is a relative plausibility within the current \(\mathcal{H}\), never an absolute verdict. Conditioning redistributes mass within \(\mathcal{H}\) and can kill a hypothesis (zero likelihood), but only revision of \(\mathcal{H}\) can create one; embedding \(\mathcal{H}\) in a grand sample space of (hypothesis, outcome) pairs makes Bayes literal pebble-conditioning but freezes with the list it was built from. Model criticism is how the hypothesis space learns.
  • The whole calculus is two rules: the sum rule \(P(A) + P(\lnot A) = 1\) and the product rule \(P(A \land B) = P(A)\,P(B \mid A)\).
  • The called-off bet is the operational meaning of conditional probability: a commitment about B activated only when A turns out true — the three-valued structure of Chapter 3’s historical aside, put to work.
  • The calculus keeps Chapter 3’s promises: at probabilities 0 and 1 it reproduces the strong syllogisms, and it licenses the weak ones quantitatively — if A entails B, then \(P(A \mid B) = P(A)/P(B) \geq P(A)\).
  • Counting is the symmetric special case of the belief calculus: when information does not distinguish a finite set of cases, desideratum 3.3 makes them equally probable, and probability reduces to favourable-over-possible.
  • The fine print of the agreement: Cox’s theorem needs technical assumptions, and the belief road forces only finite — not countable — additivity. The roads also differ on what carries probability (propositions and single cases vs repeatable setups). None of this affects the working rules used in this book.
  • Within the epistemic reading, the subjective school (Ramsey, de Finetti, Savage) requires only coherence and expects agreement to emerge from shared data, while the objective school (Jeffreys, Jaynes) requires that the same information yield the same probability, and supplies symmetry, invariance, and maximum-entropy rules for the assignment. The dispute concerns where prior probabilities come from; both schools deny that probability is physical.
  • Pushed to its ideal limit — a prior over all computable hypotheses, updated without resource limit — the robot becomes Solomonoff induction (and, for an agent that also acts, AIXI): a provably optimal predictor that is nonetheless (i) only coherence-optimal, not truth-optimal — a misspecified model converges confidently to the pseudo-true value; (ii) able to update within a hypothesis space but never to enlarge it (the problem of new hypotheses); and (iii) uncomputable, so every real system — a large pretrained model included — is a resource-bounded approximation. The robot moves through a space of hypotheses; it does not make the space.
  • A fourth limit is about expression rather than computation. Probability grades propositions, and inherits the expressive ceiling of the propositional logic it extends: it cannot state a relation among several objects. A conditional probability contains no “for all”, so the quantifier is supplied informally by the reader, or written down elsewhere — in a for loop, a plate, a subscript over units. The structure Part III adds — causal graphs, and potential outcomes indexed by unit — is precisely the relational structure that graded belief cannot express.
  • Uncertainty has many sources — biological variation, sampling, the fitting algorithm, the model’s structure, the data’s structure, and the gap between what is modelled and what is wanted — and together they make predictions of complex phenomena over-confident, experts’ included. The scientist’s task is to turn unknown unknowns into quantified known unknowns, not to manufacture certainty.
  • The desiderata force the relations, not the values. The sum and product rules follow from consistency (desideratum 3.1) with 1 and 2; desideratum 3.3 read narrowly is uncontroversial, read as agreement across agents it is the objectivist thesis, and adopting the robot commits a reader to the calculus, not to objectivism.
  • Symmetric information fixes a prior only relative to a parametrisation. Indifference is a theorem about invariant information, extended by transformation groups and maximum entropy, but the cube factory and Bertrand’s chord show that “no information” yields different priors in different coordinates; uniqueness returns only when a group is supplied that the bare problem does not state.
  • The Bayesian position faces genuine challenges as well as misunderstandings (set out in full in Appendix H). A single-event probability is meaningful but is validated only over many forecasts, through calibration. Under radical uncertainty a sharp prior may not be elicitable, and Ellsberg’s experiment exhibits reproducible ambiguity aversion that violates Savage’s sure-thing principle, so no single prior represents the preferences. The disciplined response carries a set of priors (imprecise probability, robust Bayes) with a decision rule such as maxmin expected utility — at the cost of dilation, in which conditioning can widen rather than narrow the interval. These bound the scope of the single-prior workflow without refuting the calculus.

  6. The called-off bet, reversed. An agent prices \(P(A) = 0.5\), \(P(A \land B) = 0.2\), but the conditional ticket on “B given A” at \(q = 0.3\) (the coherent price is 0.4). The Jeffreys prior is invariant under reparametrisation — that is its purpose — but is computed from the Fisher information, a feature of the sampling model, so it depends on which experiment produced the data rather than on the parameter alone. Coherence. The priors these rules produce are frequently improper, and impropriety can generate the marginalisation paradox, in which two valid derivations of the same posterior quantity disagree (Dawid AP, Stone M, Zidek JV, “Marginalization Paradoxes in Bayesian and Structural Inference,” J R Stat Soc Series B 1973;35:189-233 (DOI)). The attempt to fix a prior by rule can therefore conflict with the coherence requirement both schools accept. None of this makes objective priors useless: where the information carries a genuine symmetry they are well defined and command wide agreement. It shows only that “the information fixes the prior” holds in special cases, not as a general rule.↩︎

  24. Two examples show what “deriving” a prior means. Invariance. Suppose the parameter \(\sigma\) is a scale — a standard deviation, say — and the stated information does not include the units of measurement. A prior that carries no information about scale must then have the same form whether \(\sigma\) is recorded in days or in hours, i.e. its shape must not change when \(\sigma\) is multiplied by a positive constant. The only density with this property is \(p(\sigma) \propto 1/\sigma\), which is a flat prior on \(\log \sigma\). Jeffreys generalised this style of argument into a rule — the Jeffreys prior, proportional to the square root of the Fisher information — which returns the same posterior whichever smooth transformation of the parameter the analyst works with (Jeffreys H, “An invariant form for the prior probability in estimation problems,” Proc R Soc Lond A Math Phys Sci 1946;186:453-461 (DOI)). Maximum entropy. The entropy of a distribution is a single number measuring how spread out the distribution is — equivalently, how little it commits to beyond what is stated. The rule is: express the available information as constraints, then adopt the distribution with the largest entropy among all distributions satisfying those constraints; any other choice encodes information that was not stated. Three standard results. With no constraints beyond a finite list of possible outcomes, the maximum-entropy distribution is the uniform — the symmetry argument recovered as a special case. For a positive quantity with a stated mean, it is the exponential distribution. For a quantity on the real line with stated mean and variance, it is the normal distribution. (These distributions are introduced in Chapter 7.)↩︎
