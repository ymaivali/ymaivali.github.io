Appendix D — brms formula syntax: a map

This appendix is a map of the brms model formula, checked against brms 2.23.0, the version installed when the book was built. It is not a substitute for the package documentation — ?brmsformula, ?addition-terms, ?autocor-terms and the vignettes (vignette(package = "brms")) are the reference, and they change with the package — but it collects in one place the constructs the book uses, says where each is used, and records what has changed in brms since the first draft of this appendix was written. Worked, runnable examples with priors and post-processing are in Appendix C; the correlation structures for repeated measures have their own appendix, Appendix E.

D.1 The formula in one line

response | addition_terms ~ population_terms + (group_terms | grouping) + special_terms

The left of ~ names the response and, after |, any addition terms that say something about how it was observed (a known standard error, a censoring indicator, a binomial denominator). The right of ~ has population-level terms, which brms also calls “fixed” effects and this book calls population-level; group-level terms in parentheses, which vary across the levels of a grouping factor and which the book calls varying effects; and special terms — smooths, Gaussian processes, autocorrelation, missing-value and monotonic effects — that live on the right-hand side but are not ordinary predictors. bf() (short for brmsformula()) wraps a formula and lets other parts of the model be given their own formulas: bf(y ~ x, sigma ~ x) models the mean and the residual standard deviation at once, and any distributional parameter of the family — sigma, shape, phi, zi, hu and the rest — can be treated this way, on its natural link scale (log for positive parameters, logit for probabilities).

D.2 Twenty-five model patterns

The mathematical form is given for the first patterns and left implicit after that; y is the response, x1 and x2 predictors, g, school, class, year grouping factors, and \(\varepsilon \sim N(0, \sigma)\) throughout unless sigma is modelled.

  1. y ~ 1 — \(y = \alpha + \varepsilon\). Intercept only: the population mean and the individual-level SD, each with a posterior.
  2. y ~ x1 — \(y = \alpha + \beta_1 x_1 + \varepsilon\). One predictor; \(\sigma\) the same at every value of \(x_1\).
  3. y ~ x1 + x2 — \(y = \alpha + \beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2 x_2 + \varepsilon\). Additive: parallel slopes, common \(\sigma\).
  4. bf(y ~ x1 + x2, sigma ~ x1) — as 3, but \(\log \sigma = \gamma_0 + \gamma_1 x_1\): the spread varies with \(x_1\) (distributional regression).
  5. y ~ x1 * x2 — \(y = \alpha + \beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2 x_2 + \beta_3 x_1 x_2 + \varepsilon\), equivalently a slope of \(x_1\) that is itself \(\beta_1 + \beta_3 x_2\). Interaction (Chapter 24).
  6. y ~ 0 + x1:x2 — for two unordered factors, one mean per cell of their cross-classification, with common \(\sigma\) (cell-means coding; Chapter 24 gives the cautions).
  7. bf(y ~ 0 + x1:x2, sigma ~ 0 + x1:x2) — one mean and one SD per cell.
  8. y ~ x1 + (1 | g) — varying intercepts: \(y = (\alpha + u_g) + \beta_1 x_1 + \varepsilon\), with the \(u_g\) drawn from a common distribution whose SD is estimated (partial pooling, Chapter 28).
  9. y ~ x1 + (0 + x1 | g) — varying slopes with a common intercept.
  10. y ~ x1 + (x1 || g) — varying intercepts and slopes, their correlation fixed at zero.
  11. y ~ x1 + (x1 | g) — the same, with the intercept–slope correlation estimated.
  12. bf(y ~ x1 + (x1 | g), sigma ~ x1 + (x1 | g)) — intercepts and slopes vary by group for the mean and for \(\log \sigma\); the two sets of group effects are uncorrelated.
  13. bf(y ~ x1 + (x1 | p | g), sigma ~ x1 + (x1 | p | g)) — as 12, but the shared label p between the bars says that all group-level terms carrying it, across both formulas, share one correlation matrix, so the correlation between a group’s mean effects and its spread effects is estimated too.
  14. y ~ (1 | school/class) — nested grouping; brms expands it to (1 | school) + (1 | school:class).
  15. y ~ school + (1 | school:class) — school as a population-level factor, classes varying within schools.
  16. y ~ x + (x | school/class) — nested, with intercepts and slopes varying at both levels.
  17. y ~ (x1 | school) + (x2 | class) — two predictors whose effects vary at different levels.
  18. y ~ x * school + (x | school:class) — slope and intercept differ by school as population-level effects, and vary among classes within schools as group-level ones.
  19. y ~ (1 | school) + (1 | year) — crossed grouping factors: every school appears in every year.
  20. y ~ x + (1 | school) + (1 | class) — crossed, with a predictor.
  21. y ~ x + (0 + x | school) + (1 | class) — the slope varies by school, the intercept by class.
  22. y ~ x1 * x2 + (x1 * x2 | school) — a global intercept, two main effects and an interaction; a school-level deviation for each of the four; the six correlations among those deviations; and the variance parameters. y ~ x1 + x2 + (x1 + x2 | school) is the simpler model to fit beside it.
  23. y ~ time + (time | g) + ar(time = time, gr = person, p = 2) — a varying time trend plus AR(2) residual autocorrelation within person (Appendix E).
  24. y ~ (1 | mm(g1, g2)) — multi-membership: each unit belongs to two groups at once, with mmc(x1, x2) for a covariate that differs between the memberships.
  25. y ~ sex + (1 | gr(school, by = sex)) — separate group-level SDs (and correlations) for the sub-populations defined by sex; gr() also takes cor = FALSE (the || form) and id = (the |p| form).

Three further families of pattern use bf() with more than one formula. Non-linear models name the parameters and give each its own formula: bf(y ~ a1 - a2^x, a1 ~ 1, a2 ~ x + (x | g), nl = TRUE); priors on the non-linear parameters are mandatory (vignette("brms_nonlinear")). Multivariate models either share one right-hand side, bf(mvbind(y1, y2) ~ x + (1 | p | g)), or add formulas, bf(y1 ~ x) + bf(y2 ~ z) + set_rescor(TRUE), where set_rescor() says whether the residuals are correlated — the form Chapter 18 uses for the two-equation instrumental-variable system and Chapter 30 for imputation (with set_rescor(FALSE)). And missing values in a predictor are modelled with mi() on both sides: bf(y ~ mi(x) + z) + bf(x | mi() ~ z) + set_rescor(FALSE); x | mi(sdy = se) ~ z adds known measurement error (Chapter 30).

D.3 Nested and crossed grouping factors

A grouping factor is nested in another when each of its levels occurs within exactly one level of the other — patients within one clinic, classes within one school — and crossed when its levels recur across the other’s — patients seen by several doctors, schools observed in several years. Nesting is written (1 | school/class), which brms expands to (1 | school) + (1 | school:class); the interaction term is what makes “Class 1” in one school a different group from “Class 1” in another. Crossed factors are written as separate terms, (1 | doctor) + (1 | patient), and may be fully crossed (every patient sees every doctor) or partially. The two can appear together: (1 | hospital/patient) + (1 | doctor) for patients nested in hospitals and doctors working across them. The rule is that grouping factors that are not nested are crossed, and whether they are nested is a fact about the design rather than something the data can be trusted to reveal: a class label reused across schools looks crossed to the software unless the nesting is written out. Chapter 28 fits the book’s clustered cohort, patients within clinics; Appendix E handles repeated measures within patients.

D.6 Priors

Every parameter class in a brms model can carry a prior, and the book declares them rather than accepting the defaults (Appendix C has the worked examples). The classes that recur:

prior(normal(0, 1),      class = b)                 # all population-level slopes
prior(normal(0, 1),      class = b, coef = drug)    # one slope
prior(normal(140, 20),   class = Intercept)         # the intercept (centred; see below)
prior(exponential(1),    class = sd)                # group-level standard deviations
prior(lkj(2),            class = cor)               # group-level correlation matrices
prior(exponential(1),    class = sigma)             # residual SD

default_prior(formula, data = , family = ) (the alias get_prior() still works) lists every prior the model would use, class by class, with the brms defaults filled in — the quickest way to see what a model’s parameters are called before writing priors for them — and prior_summary(fit) reports the priors a fitted model actually used. The lkj(2) prior on correlation matrices, which Chapter 28 uses, places mild mass away from perfect correlation; lkj(1) is uniform over correlation matrices, and larger values concentrate on independence.

D.7 The intercept is centred

By default brms centres the population-level design matrix around its column means before sampling and restores the intercept afterwards. The estimate reported for the intercept is unaffected, but a prior placed on class = Intercept applies to the centred intercept — the expected response when every predictor is at its sample mean — not to the response at zero. With a 0/1 treatment predictor and equal group sizes, for example, the centred intercept is the grand mean, not the control-group mean, and a prior meant for the control mean is off by half the treatment effect (Chapter 21). The difference matters when the prior is tight or the predictors are far from zero. To place a prior on the raw intercept, write it as an ordinary coefficient, y ~ 0 + Intercept + x, so that class = b, coef = Intercept addresses it directly; or pass center = FALSE to bf(). Both are somewhat less efficient to sample than the default.

D.8 What has changed in brms since this appendix was first drafted

The first draft of this appendix (2021) reproduced the ?brmsformula help page of the time. brms has moved on; Table D.5 lists the changes a reader of older code or older tutorials is most likely to meet, with the release that made them, from the package’s NEWS file.

Two further conventions of the book are not brms syntax at all but follow from it. Effects are read from the fitted model with marginaleffects::avg_comparisons() as contrasts of predictions, never from coefficients (Chapter 21); and every fit is cached with file = "models/…" and file_refit = "on_change", so that a chapter re-renders quickly and refits only when its data or model change.
