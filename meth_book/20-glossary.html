Appendix I — Glossary

Each entry gives the sense in which the book uses the term and the chapter that owns the definition. Where a term has more than one sense in the book, the entry says which is which. The entries are short by design; the owning chapter carries the argument.

Adjustment set. A set of covariates \(L\) that blocks every back-door path from treatment to outcome and contains no descendant of the treatment. It is read off the DAG, never chosen by model fit or by a change in the estimate. Owner: Chapter 12.

Average treatment effect (ATE), ATT, ATU. \(E[Y(1)] - E[Y(0)]\) in the whole population; the same contrast among the treated (ATT) or the untreated (ATU). The three coincide only when the effect is homogeneous on the chosen scale. Owner: Chapter 11.

Back-door path; back-door criterion. A path from treatment to outcome that starts with an arrow into the treatment. The criterion: a set \(L\) identifies the total effect if it blocks every back-door path and contains no descendant of the treatment. Owner: Chapter 12.

Bayes’ theorem; odds form. \(P(H \mid d) \propto P(d \mid H)\,P(H)\); in odds form, posterior odds \(=\) prior odds \(\times\) likelihood ratio, and in logarithms the pieces add. Owner: Chapter 9.

Bayesian workflow. Prior predictive check, fit, MCMC diagnostics, posterior predictive check, model comparison within a fixed adjustment set, then the report as a standardised contrast. Owner: Chapter 23.

Bounding factor; E-value. In sensitivity analysis, the largest factor by which an unmeasured confounder of given strength could move a risk ratio; the E-value is the minimum strength, on the risk-ratio scale, that both confounder associations would need to explain the observed ratio away. Owner: Chapter 20.

Calibration. Of a forecaster: stated probabilities match observed frequencies (Chapter 2). Of a risk model: the predicted risks match observed risks across their range, judged on held-out data; the book ranks it above discrimination. Owner: Chapter 27.

Case-control study; outcome-dependent sampling. All cases and a sample of non-cases. Only the intercept of a logistic model changes under such sampling, which is why logistic regression is its analysis; what the odds ratio then estimates depends on how controls were drawn. Owner: Chapter 25.

Censoring. Follow-up ends before the event is observed; the time is known only to exceed the censoring time. Owner: Chapter 26.

Central limit theorem. The mean of many independent draws with finite variance is approximately normal, whatever the draws’ distribution. Owner: Chapter 7.

Collider; collider bias. A variable with two arrows into it. Conditioning on a collider, or on a descendant of one, opens a path and creates an association with no causal source. Selection bias is collider bias. Owner: Chapter 12.

Confounding; confounding by indication. A common cause of treatment and outcome makes the treated and untreated non-exchangeable, so the crude contrast mixes the effect with the difference between the groups. Confounding by indication is the clinical case in which the sicker patients receive the treatment. Owners: Chapter 4 (definition) and Chapter 5 (by indication).

Consistency. The observed outcome equals the potential outcome under the treatment actually received, \(Y = Y(A)\), which requires a well-defined intervention: one version of treatment, not a bundle. Owner: Chapter 11.

Counterfactual. What would have happened to the same unit under a different treatment. The book treats the counterfactual model as an operational device for reasoning about interventions, not a theory of what causation is (the metaphysical firewall). Owners: Chapter 4 and Chapter 5.

Credible interval. An interval holding a stated share of the posterior probability. Not a confidence interval, which is a frequentist statement about a procedure’s long-run coverage. Owner: Chapter 10.

Cumulative incidence. The risk of the event by a stated time, allowing for competing events where they exist; the book’s preferred time-to-event contrast alongside the restricted mean survival time. Owner: Chapter 26.

DAG (directed acyclic graph). Nodes for variables, arrows for direct causal effects, no cycles. A missing arrow is the strong claim. Owner: Chapter 12.

Data-generating process. The set of mechanisms that produced the data; what a model is a model of, and what a simulation makes explicit. Owner: Chapter 1.

Depletion of susceptibles. Under a protective treatment the frailer patients survive longer in the treated arm, so the two arms’ survivors are no longer comparable and a hazard ratio changes over time without any change in the effect. Owner: Chapter 26.

Difference-in-differences. Change in the treated group minus change in a control group, identified under parallel trends. Owner: Chapter 19.

Discrimination; AUC. How well predicted risks rank cases above non-cases, summarised by the area under the ROC curve. A property of predictions, silent on causation. Owners: Chapter 25 and Chapter 27.

do-operator. \(P(Y \mid \mathrm{do}(A = a))\) is the distribution of the outcome when treatment is set to \(a\) by intervention, as opposed to observed at \(a\). It equals \(E[Y(a)]\) and equals \(P(Y \mid A = a)\) only without confounding. Owner: Chapter 11.

Doubly robust estimation. An estimator that combines an outcome model with a treatment model and is consistent if either is right; AIPW and TMLE are the forms named. Owner: Chapter 29.

d-separation. The graphical rule for reading which conditional independences a DAG implies: a path is blocked by conditioning on a non-collider on it, or by not conditioning on a collider or its descendants. Owner: Chapter 12.

Effect modification versus interaction. Effect modification: the effect of one exposure differs across levels of another variable. Interaction: two exposures act jointly beyond their separate effects. Both are scale-dependent; a product term is an interaction on the link scale only. Owner: Chapter 17.

Estimand, estimator, estimate. The quantity a study targets, the rule that computes a value for it from data, and the value obtained. The book states the estimand first, in a named population and on a named scale. Owner: Chapter 11.

Exchangeability. Causal: the potential outcomes are independent of treatment received, marginally (\(Y(a) \perp A\)) or given covariates (\(Y(a) \perp A \mid L\)); an assumption, untestable from the data. Statistical (de Finetti): a sequence whose joint distribution is invariant to reordering, the basis for treating observations as draws from a common process. Owners: Chapter 11 and Chapter 9.

Exponential family; canonical link. The family of distributions on which the generalised linear model is built; the canonical link maps the mean to the family’s natural parameter. Canonical describes the algebra, not the scientific relevance of a measure. Owners: Chapter 7 and Chapter 25.

Fork, chain, collider. The three elementary structures of a DAG: a common cause, a mediated path, a common effect. Conditioning blocks the first two and opens the third. Owner: Chapter 12.

Front-door criterion. Identification through a mediator that carries the whole effect and is itself unconfounded, when the back door cannot be closed. Owner: Chapter 19.

Fundamental problem of causal inference. Only one of a unit’s potential outcomes is ever observed, so individual causal effects are unknowable and the target is an average. Owner: Chapter 11.

g-computation; g-formula; standardisation. Predict every unit’s outcome under each treatment, average, contrast: \(E[Y(a)] = \sum_l E[Y \mid A = a, L = l]\,P(L = l)\). By hand with strata in Chapter 13; with a fitted Bayesian model, carrying the posterior through, in Chapter 29.

g-methods. The family of estimators for time-varying treatment with treatment-confounder feedback: inverse-probability weighting of marginal structural models, the parametric g-formula, and g-estimation of structural nested models. Owner: Chapter 15.

Generalised linear model; link function. A distribution for the outcome, a linear predictor, and a link \(g(\mu) = \eta\) joining them. The link commits the model to a scale on which effects add; the report is a standardised contrast on the risk scale whatever the link. Owner: Chapter 25.

Hazard; hazard ratio. The rate of the event among those still event-free at time \(t\); the ratio of two such rates. Because it conditions on survival, a post-treatment quantity, the hazard ratio is not a clean causal contrast. Owners: Chapter 26 and Chapter 11.

Hypothesis space. The set of hypotheses over which probability is distributed, as distinct from the sample space of possible data. Owner: Chapter 8.

Identification versus estimation. Whether the estimand is determined by the observable distribution under the stated assumptions, and, separately, how well a finite sample pins it down. A model can estimate precisely what it does not identify. Owner: Chapter 11.

Immortal time. Follow-up during which the outcome could not occur by construction, misassigned to a treatment group when time zero and treatment assignment are not aligned. Owner: Chapter 14.

Instrumental variable. A variable that affects treatment (relevance), affects the outcome only through treatment (exclusion), and shares no cause with the outcome (independence); with monotonicity it identifies the effect among compliers. Owner: Chapter 18.

Intention-to-treat; per-protocol. The effect of being assigned a strategy, against the effect of following it; the second needs adjustment for the reasons people deviate. Owner: Chapter 14.

Interference. One unit’s treatment affects another’s outcome, so \(Y_i(a)\) depends on more than unit \(i\)’s own treatment and the estimand has to be redefined. Owner: Chapter 11.

Inverse-probability weighting (IPW). Weighting each unit by the inverse of the probability of the treatment it received given covariates, creating a pseudo-population in which treatment is unconfounded; stabilised weights use the marginal treatment probability in the numerator. Owners: Chapter 13 and Chapter 15.

Likelihood. \(P(d \mid H)\) read as a function of the hypothesis for fixed data; not a probability distribution over hypotheses. Owner: Chapter 9.

Linear probability model. An identity link and a normal likelihood on a 0/1 outcome; its slope is a risk difference and its predictions can leave the unit interval. Owner: Chapter 25.

loo; elpd. Leave-one-out cross-validation estimated from the posterior; the expected log predictive density is its score. It compares functional forms within a fixed adjustment set and scores only the outcomes that were observed. Owner: Chapter 23.

Marginal structural model. A model for the potential-outcome mean as a function of treatment history, fitted by inverse-probability weighting. Owner: Chapter 15.

Marginal versus conditional effect. The contrast of population-average risks, against the contrast at fixed values of the covariates. They coincide for the risk difference and risk ratio under a constant effect and differ for the odds ratio even then. Owners: Chapter 23 and Chapter 25.

MCMC; R-hat; divergences. The sampling algorithms behind brms; the convergence diagnostic comparing chains; the transitions that signal a posterior geometry the sampler cannot follow. Owner: Chapter 23.

Mediator; mediation. A variable on the causal path from treatment to outcome. Adjusting for it removes part of the total effect; the direct and indirect effects need their own identification conditions. Owner: Chapter 16.

Missing data: MCAR, MAR, MNAR. Missingness independent of everything, dependent on observed variables only, or dependent on the unobserved value. The book draws the missingness indicator as a node on the DAG and lets the graph choose the method. Owner: Chapter 30.

Monotonicity. Two senses: in the sufficient-cause framework, no unit is harmed by the exposure; for an instrument, no defiers. Owners: Chapter 17 and Chapter 18.

Multilevel model; partial pooling. Group-level parameters drawn from a common distribution, so that small groups borrow strength from the rest. Owner: Chapter 28.

Negative control. An exposure known not to cause the outcome, or an outcome known not to be caused by the exposure; an association with either signals bias. Owner: Chapter 19.

Non-collapsibility. A measure is collapsible if the marginal value is a weighted average of the stratum-specific values. The risk difference and risk ratio are; the odds ratio and hazard ratio are not, so adjustment changes their estimand even without confounding. Owner: Chapter 25.

Number needed to treat. The reciprocal of the risk difference; depends on baseline risk and time horizon and must be reported with both. Owner: Chapter 11.

Odds; odds ratio. \(p/(1-p)\); the ratio of two odds. Approximates the risk ratio only for rare outcomes, is non-collapsible, and is treated in the book as an intermediate quantity to convert before reporting. Owners: Chapter 11 and Chapter 25.

Offset. A term in the linear predictor with coefficient fixed at 1, offset(log(t)), that turns a model of counts into a model of rates. Owner: Chapter 25.

Overdispersion. Variance exceeding what the Poisson allows; the negative binomial adds the second parameter. Owners: Chapter 7 and Chapter 25.

p value. The probability, under a null hypothesis, of data at least as extreme as those observed. The book explains the machinery and does not use it for inference. Owner: Appendix G.

Person-time; rate. Time at risk summed over units; events divided by person-time. Owner: Chapter 26.

Poisson working model. family = poisson() fitted to a 0/1 outcome to obtain a log-link risk model when the log-binomial does not sample; a working model for the mean, with mildly conservative intervals. Owners: Chapter 13 and Chapter 25.

Positivity. Every covariate pattern that occurs has a non-zero probability of each treatment. A structural violation is a hole in the question; a practical one is sparse data, whose severity depends on the estimator. Owner: Chapter 11.

Posterior; prior. The probability distribution over hypotheses or parameters after and before the data; the prior is stated in the units of the predictors and checked by a prior predictive simulation. Owners: Chapter 10 and Chapter 21.

Posterior predictive check; prior predictive check. Simulating data from the fitted model, or from the prior alone, and comparing with the data observed or with what is plausible. Owner: Chapter 23.

Potential outcome, \(Y(a)\). The outcome a unit would have under treatment level \(a\). Owner: Chapter 11.

Prevalent-user bias. Comparing current users with non-users selects users who tolerated and survived the treatment; the new-user design starts everyone at initiation. Owner: Chapter 14.

Propensity score. \(P(A = 1 \mid L)\), the probability of treatment given covariates; the basis of weighting and matching. Owner: Chapter 13.

Regression discontinuity. Identification from a treatment assigned by a threshold on a running variable, comparing units just either side of it. Owner: Chapter 19.

Risk difference; risk ratio. \(E[Y(1)] - E[Y(0)]\) and \(E[Y(1)]/E[Y(0)]\). The book’s two reported effect measures: the difference for decisions, the ratio for transport between populations. The risk ratio is often called the relative risk elsewhere. Owner: Chapter 11.

Selection bias. Bias from conditioning, by design or by analysis, on a common effect of treatment and outcome or of their causes; collider bias under another name. Owner: Chapter 12.

Sensitivity analysis. Quantifying how strong an unmeasured confounder, or another departure from the assumptions, would have to be to change the conclusion. It describes robustness; it does not detect bias. Owner: Chapter 20.

Sequential exchangeability. Conditional exchangeability at each visit given the history of treatment and covariates to that point; the assumption behind the g-methods. Owner: Chapter 15.

Splines. Flexible curves built from basis functions with a smoothness penalty, the penalty acting as a prior; the book’s default for a continuous confounder. Owners: Chapter 24 and Appendix F.

Static versus dynamic treatment strategy. A strategy fixed in advance (always treat, never treat) against one that responds to the evolving covariates (treat when indicated). Owner: Chapter 14.

Sufficient-component causes. Rothman’s causal pies: an outcome occurs when any one sufficient set of component causes is complete, which gives interaction a mechanistic meaning. Owners: Chapter 5 and Chapter 17.

SUTVA. The stable unit treatment value assumption: no interference and one version of treatment, which together let one write \(Y_i(a)\). Owner: Chapter 11.

Table 2 fallacy. Reading the coefficients of covariates other than the exposure as effects of those covariates, in a model whose adjustment set was chosen for the exposure. Owner: Chapter 22.

Target trial emulation. Specifying the randomised trial that would answer the question (eligibility, time zero, strategies, follow-up, estimand) and emulating each component with observational data. Owner: Chapter 14.

Time zero. The moment from which follow-up is counted, which must coincide with eligibility and with treatment assignment. Owner: Chapter 14.

Time-varying confounding; treatment-confounder feedback. A confounder that is also affected by earlier treatment, so that adjusting for it blocks part of the effect and leaving it out leaves confounding; the case that needs the g-methods. Owner: Chapter 15.

Weight of evidence. The logarithm of the likelihood ratio; weights of independent pieces of evidence add to the log-odds, which is also what a logistic regression’s linear predictor expresses. Owner: Chapter 9.
