7  Two roads to probability: Jaynes and Kolmogorov

Chapter 3 ended with a specification for an expanded logic: it must reduce to classical logic under certainty, be non-monotonic and inductive, quantify uncertainty numerically, license the weak syllogisms, and protect its user from sure loss when commitments come in degrees. This chapter builds the calculus that meets the specification — and builds it twice, by two independent routes. The first route extends the logic of Chapter 3 directly: the robot returns, one of its two design assumptions is weakened, and the rules of probability are forced, both by desiderata on degrees of belief and by the no-sure-loss argument. The second route starts somewhere else entirely: in gamblers’ questions about counting, formalised three centuries later as axioms about sets. The two routes deliver the same working rules. Whether they deliver the same thing is the question the chapter closes with.

7.1 Understanding uncertainty

There is a pandemic coming our way, like a lurching sea monster from the deep pit, and we need to model its course. To do this we need, among other things, to fix the model input that is called the R0 (the number of people infected on average by a single infected person). Initially, we will have little data to estimate the R0 on, which makes R0 a known unknown. So we estimate it on the data that we have, only to end up with wide error margins, which will be carried over into our prediction of the course of the epidemic. This way we transfer uncertainty from data to model to prediction to interpretation to policy. Surely the policy makers deserve to know that there is a large but quantified level of uncertainty about how many hospital beds are needed within a month or so, as this would allow them to implement policies that err on the safe side. Or, alternatively, we could just pick a couple of point values for the R0 from thin air and run a couple of models that result in predictions with much narrower error margins. Of course, everybody loves certainty. Indeed, this is pretty much what happened in March-April 2020 with a dozen or so covid models in the UK, US, and Estonia, which gave wildly different predictions with non-overlapping quite narrow errors. In some cases these models resulted in policy decisions that clearly failed. This way we have manufactured model-based certainty, by incurring the cost of banishing the residual uncertainty into the murky realm of unknown unknowns. We only become aware of the presence of unknown unknowns from absence of success, and as we cannot really understand, much less quantify them, they are really scary.1 The moral of our story is that the aim of the scientist should be to tame as much of the wild uncertainty that she can, by turning it into quantifiable known unknowns. It is not to convert uncertainty into certainty, which is logically impossible, as much as building a perpetuum mobile is physically impossible, and often leads to grief.

In any decent model of a biological process there are different types of uncertainty that include

  1. the uncertainty arising from biological variation

  2. the uncertainty arising from sampling error

  3. the uncertainty arising from the model fitting algorithm

  4. the uncertainty arising from the model structure (linearity, normality, and whatnot)

  5. the uncertainty arising from what is actually modelled vs. what we want to know based on this modelling.2

Taken together, these motley sources of uncertainty pretty much guarantee that our predictions of complex phenomena are over-confident. This holds for both formal and informal predictions. For example, in April 2020, David Spiegelhalter and his colleagues asked 140 UK experts and >2,000 non-experts for prediction on the number of Covid-19 deaths by the end of the year. Experts median estimate was 30 000, whereas the non-experts was 20 000. The true number was 75 000, being inside only a third of the experts’ prediction intervals and in only a tenth of the non-experts’. If people estimate a 95% prediction interval for something and reality falls into this interval in only 30% of the cases, then on average they underestimated uncertainty very greatly; while not only realizing it, but rejecting even the possibility of values similar to the true value as very unlikely. A major problem with formal modeling of uncertainty is that it gives false confidence about uncertainty!

Let’s assume, for the sake of argument, that we live in a fully deterministic world where there is no uncertainty concerning the past, present, or future. In this world it is possible, in principle, to predict exactly when a butterfly flips its wings a thousand years from now. The deterministic world is fully described by a set of rigid cause-effect relations, and there is no stochasticity in it. Objectively, in there nothing is uncertain. But still, there are humans in the ointment, and humans tend to be fallible. Even when they measure a quantity with a perfectly calibrated instrument, there is always some measurement error. And that error is enough to introduce uncertainty into our deterministic world. This uncertainty is subjective, or epistemic – it belongs to us, not to the thing that was measured – and it propagates to any conclusions based on these measurements. In such a world, even if in principle we could say that patient X dies within a week, because of this kind of propagated error we can only say that it is plausible that patient X dies within a week, or that there is a risk that patient X dies within a week.

Of course, things can be more or less plausible and the risk can be higher or lower, which means that we need a language that allows numerical quantification of these things. This language is probability theory. The rules of probability are defined later in this chapter; here it suffices to say that probabilities are real numbers between 0 and 1 (although not every scale 0…1 is a probability scale). If we denote the hypothesis “patient X dies within a week” with “A”, then P(A) is the probability of the hypothesis A being true (patient X really dying).

  • P(A) = 0 means that we are absolutely sure that X stays with the living (but can we ever be sure of such a thing?),

  • P(A) = 0.1 means that we are pretty sure that X lives,

  • P(A) = 0.5 means that we think that both outcomes are equally likely.

  • P(A) = 0.9 means that we are as sure that X dies as we were sure that X lives, when P(A) was 0.1,

  • P(A) = 1 means that we believe that it is a sure thing that X is dead within the week.

Now, after the week is up the P(A) can only be 0 or 1 (unless we lost track of X), but until that time, or until X dies, the P(A) will move between these extremes, subject to X’s condition and our knowledge of it.

Why we need numerical probabilities? The answer is twofold: (i) we can do formal calculations with numerical probabilities, which is basically what statistics is, and (ii) it has proved patently impossible to make different people understand the non-numerical representations like very likely, extremely likely, somewhat likely, the same way. This holds even for professionals.

Now, suppose that we live in a world, where truly stochastic stuff happens (be it in a quantum level or in weather prediction, or wherever). This world is only partially causal, meaning that (i) cause-effect relations can come with built-in randomness (sometimes they work, sometimes they don’t, like smoking causing cancer), or (ii) some relations are inherently acausal (completely unpredictable, like radioactive decay) Does the meaning of P(A) change? The answer is, no. In this world we still have measurement uncertainty, which we try to reduce as much we can, and on top of this is the real, irreducible uncertainty, which comes from how the world itself works. They converge, and can never be disentangled, in our final probability P(A), which is still epistemic.

Another example: the weather report says that P(rain) is 60%. What does it mean? It either rains or it doesn’t, so P(rain) cannot describe a physical feature of the world. “60% rain” cannot be objective in the sense of “being really there”. Usually P(rain) is calculated by running a weather model many times with slightly differing input data and counting the relative fraction of runs where the model predicts rain. If 6 models out of 10 predict rain, then the weatherman says that “P(rain) = 0.6, or there’s a 60% chance of rain”. Although the 60% was derived from a relative frequency, this does not matter for the interpretation – the probability is still epistemic. As a recent development we now have AI-driven weather forecasts, such as DeepMind’s GraphCast3 and GenCast, which do not solve the physical equations of the atmosphere when producing a forecast. They are neural networks trained on historical atmospheric states, which were themselves produced by physics-based models. GenCast produces an ensemble of stochastic forecasts by repeatedly sampling from a learned distribution, and P(rain) is read off that ensemble as the fraction of members that produce rain4 Although this is the same operation the traditional weather model performed above, the source of the number is different: perturbed physics in one case, samples from a learned distribution in the other. But the interpretation is not: in both cases P(rain) is a degree of belief about a single, non-repeatable event, and it remains epistemic. The AIs network’s distribution is still the forecaster’s distribution, learned from past data, not a physical property of tomorrow’s atmosphere.

I’m writing this because often it is taught to students that probability is a long-run relative frequency of events that really happen, or at least could happen in principle, and that therefore P(A) is “objective” and “a real thing”. It has been understood for a century that this thinking is fundamentally flawed, as it ascribes reality to fictional objects.5 So aleatoric probability is a bit like a unicorn: a beautiful fiction. The “objective” interpretation is more objectively called “aleatoric”, and the box below offers a deeper dive into this.

The aleatoric interpretation of probability: chance, positivism, and frequentist statistics

The position taken above is epistemic: \(P(A)\) grades a state of knowledge, and even a number read off a frequency — the weather ensemble, a shuffled deck — is interpreted that way. The rival interpretation is aleatoric (from Latin alea, dice): probability is a physical property of a repeatable setup, a feature of the world that holds whether or not anyone is counting events. On this reading the probability of heads is a constant of the whole coin tossing arrangement (the coin, the tosser, the laws of physics, and so on) – an objective fact to be found out rather than a number to be assigned.

Probability emerged around 1660 with both aspects already present — one concerned with degrees of belief and evidence, the other with stable frequencies in games of chance — and the aleatoric aspect is the one this book sets aside.6 It is set out here because it is the philosophical basis of the frequentist statistics most readers met first, and its motivation explains both its appeal and its limits.

The principal aleatoric interpretation is the frequency theory: the probability of an attribute is the limiting relative frequency with which it occurs in an infinitely long sequence of trials. Venn gave the first systematic statement; von Mises and Reichenbach made it precise, von Mises basing it on the collective (Kollektiv) — an idealised infinite sequence in which the limiting frequency exists and does not change under any admissible rule for selecting a subsequence.7 Probability so defined is estimated, it is a quantity one converges on by observation. A second aleatoric interpretation, Popper’s propensity theory, treats probability as a physical disposition (propensity) of the experimental arrangement to produce outcomes in a fixed proportion, introduced partly to give single, non-repeated events an objective probability, which the frequency theory denies them.8 However, the propensity move has a price. As propensity is a causal disposition, attaching it to \(P(E \mid C)\) imports the directionality of cause-effect relationship into a calculus that has none: Bayes’ theorem freely inverts \(P(E \mid C)\), the tendency of a condition \(C\) to produce a later outcome \(E\), into \(P(C \mid E)\), which the propensity reading would then have to construe as a tendency of the effect to produce its own cause. Humphreys used this to show that propensities cannot satisfy the probability axioms and therefore are not probabilities.9

The frequency theory fitted well with the dominant science philosophical wordlview of early 20th century, which saw science as at least in principle an objective endeavor, and thus answered a demand of positivist philosophy of science. Comte’s positivism admitted as legitimate only knowledge grounded in observable phenomena and their law-like regularities, excluding metaphysical and psychological entities; its twentieth-century successor, the logical empiricism of the Vienna Circle and the allied Berlin group, held that the meaning of a term is fixed by its method of empirical verification. A degree of belief, being not directly observable, is suspect on this standard; a limiting frequency, defined by counting observable outcomes, is not. The frequency theory is in this sense the positivist account of probability — it makes probability an empirical science of mass phenomena rather than a logic of partial belief. The link was not incidental: von Mises, the theory’s chief architect, belonged to the movement and wrote a treatise defending positivism itself.10 It reverses the position of the previous section: where de Finetti held that objective probability in the world does not exist, the frequentist holds that only the frequency is real and the degree of belief is the fiction.

This reading is the foundation of frequentist inference — Fisher’s significance tests and p-values, and the hypothesis tests and confidence intervals of Neyman and Pearson.11 Its defining feature follows directly from the aleatoric commitment: probability attaches to procedures evaluated over hypothetical repetitions of the sampling, never to a hypothesis or a parameter, because a fixed unknown constant has no frequency and \(P(\text{hypothesis} \mid \text{data})\) is therefore undefined. A 95% confidence interval reports a property of the interval-construction procedure — that 95% of the intervals it generates across repeated samples would contain the true value — not a 95% probability that the parameter is in the one interval computed; this is the source of the near-universal misreading of the interval. The same commitment fixes both the scope of the theory and its limit. Where a genuine repeatable setup exists the aleatoric number is well defined, and the two readings agree; but for a one-off event or a fixed parameter there is no frequency to be had, and it is there — the single case of the weather forecast and the dying patient at the start of this chapter — that the aleatoric probability falters while the epistemic probability doesn’t miss a step. The frequentist machinery, and the null-hypothesis logic built on it, are taken up in the appendix on null hypothesis testing; this book takes the epistemic road for exactly the reason that it keeps working where the frequency reading stops. For a proponent of epistemic interpretation, when all the relevant background (prior) information consitst of relative frequencies, then the frequentist stance is perfectly fine and quietly subsumes into perfectly ordinary epistemic probability – and per Occam’s razor, we have no need to bring any other entity into this world to guide our work. As for making science itself more objective (into a truth-generating machine, perhaps) via statistics, 200 years of probability theorizing has shown very nicely that this is a pipe dream. The chapter on Jaynes robot will take this up.

7.1.1 Subjective and objective Bayesians: de Finetti and Jaynes

Within the epistemic camp there are two schools, and the difference between them concerns how strongly the available information constrains the prior probability P(A). Both deny that P(A) is a physical property of the world, and both accept the rules of probability that this chapter derives. They part on one question: must two rational agents who hold the same information assign the same probability to P(A)?

The subjective (or personalist) school — Ramsey, de Finetti, Savage12 — answers no. Here P(A) is a personal degree of belief, measured operationally as the price at which the agent is willing to bet on A, and the only requirement that rationality imposes on it is coherence: the agent’s probabilities, taken together, must obey the rules of the calculus, so that no combination of bets the agent accepts as fair produces a guaranteed loss (the Dutch book argument, developed later in this chapter). Coherence constrains the relations between probabilities, not their starting values: two coherent agents may price the same proposition at 0.2 and at 0.6 without either making an error. What the subjectivist offers instead of initial agreement is convergence: agents who begin from different priors and condition on the same accumulating data end up with similar posterior probabilities, provided neither assigned probability zero to possibilities the other takes seriously. Consensus is then an output of inference, not a precondition for it. De Finetti’s representation theorem (1937) supplies the school’s substitute for “objective chance”: if an agent judges a sequence of events exchangeable — the order in which the observations arrive carries no information — then her beliefs are mathematically equivalent to beliefs about an unknown limiting frequency with a prior distribution over its values. The “true probability of heads” can therefore be used as a calculating device by someone who denies that any such quantity exists.

When posteriors need not merge: a point null

The proviso is not a technicality; it settles real cases. Take a single parameter \(\theta\) and the point null \(H_0: \theta = 0\). One analyst places a continuous prior on \(\theta\) — a density, with no point mass anywhere. A second places a spike-and-slab prior: a lump of probability \(\pi_0\) on the single value \(\theta = 0\), with the remaining \(1 - \pi_0\) spread over a continuous slab. These are not two distributions over the same space; they disagree about whether \(\theta = 0\) is a possibility at all. The first assigns the event \(\{\theta = 0\}\) probability zero, the second assigns it \(\pi_0 > 0\).

Bayes’ rule makes every posterior absolutely continuous with respect to its prior, so an event of prior probability zero retains posterior probability zero whatever the data. The first analyst therefore has \(P(H_0 \mid \text{data}) = 0\) at every sample size: the point null is never in play for her, and she would assess it only through an interval around zero, or a region of practical equivalence. The second analyst has a posterior that moves — \(P(H_0 \mid \text{data})\) rises towards \(1\) as data accumulate if \(\theta = 0\) holds and falls towards \(0\) if it does not, the quantity reported as a local false discovery rate. The two assessments of the point null never converge, because the two priors are mutually singular on the event \(\{\theta = 0\}\) itself: each gives zero weight to what, for the other, carries the whole question.13 This is the proviso failing in the most direct way, and the resulting non-convergence is what the convergence result predicts, not a counterexample to it.

The divergence is confined to that one point. On any interval the two posteriors agree in the limit — \(P(|\theta| < \varepsilon \mid \text{data}) \to 1\) under both priors when \(\theta = 0\) is true — and their predictions for future observations merge as the general result requires. What does not merge is the atom, which is the whole of the disagreement. The two analysts are not running one model with different settings; they hold different hypothesis spaces in Jaynes’s sense — different sets of entertained alternatives, part of the prior information on which every probability is conditioned. For one, \(\theta = 0\) is a distinguished hypothesis with standing of its own; for the other it is a value no different from any neighbouring one. Which to adopt is a question about the science — whether an effect can be a true zero — and not one the data can settle. The same point mass produces the Jeffreys–Lindley paradox, in which a result significant by a frequentist test can leave the point-null posterior favouring the null as the sample grows.14 It also marks the limit of the convergence consolation: posteriors merge for parameters within a shared model, but a structural commitment — a point mass, a model indicator, the dimension of the model — need not merge, and a conclusion that turns on it stays prior-dependent at every sample size.

The objective school — Jeffreys and Cox before him, Jaynes most explicitly — answers yes. Here probability is the degree of belief that a rational agent ought to hold given specified information, and it is a function of that information alone: two agents with the same information must assign the same P(A), and a disagreement between them indicates either an error or an undisclosed difference in information. The calculus is therefore supplemented with rules for translating information into a unique assignment: symmetry arguments (information that does not distinguish between cases assigns them equal probability — desideratum 3.3 of the next section), invariance arguments (the assignment must not change under a mere relabelling of the problem), and the principle of maximum entropy, which selects, among all distributions compatible with the stated constraints, the one that assumes nothing beyond them.15 “Objective” does not mean physical. The probability still describes a state of knowledge; the claim is that the state of knowledge, fully stated, fixes the number, so that probability assignments become interpersonal in the way that logic is interpersonal. This sets the objective probability against both of the other readings at once: where the frequency interpretation treats the probability as a physical constant to be estimated from data, and the subjectivist treats it as a personal degree of belief the agent is free to assign, the objectivist holds it to be derived — computed from the stated information by a rule, and so neither measured against the world nor chosen at will.

The subjective and objective schools do not weigh rationality on the same scale. For the subjectivist it is coherence: any internally consistent belief is rational, so two agents having the same information may differ on priors and both be beyond reproach; a miscalibrated agent is merely unlucky, not irrational. For the objectivist, rationality includes a second clause — the prior must be the unique function of the information — so systematically different priors from identical information means that one agent has made an inferential error.16

The schools differ on more than the division of labour. The objective programme presupposes that stated information fixes a single prior; the subjectivist holds that in general it does not. The rules for converting information into a distribution — symmetry, invariance, maximum entropy — return a unique answer only relative to further choices that the information does not itself determine, so two analysts who agree on everything they know can still derive different “objective” priors.17 The disagreement the objectivist set out to remove reappears at an earlier step. On this view the subjective position is not the concession that the correct prior is hard to find; it is the claim that, in general, there is no unique correct prior to find.

There is also a positive advantage to the subjective prior, though it must be set against the right target. Maximum entropy is not a device for producing an uninformative prior: it returns the most noncommittal distribution consistent with the stated constraints, and where those constraints encode substantial knowledge — a known mean, a conservation law, a symmetry — the resulting prior is informative to the same degree. The Jaynesian objectivist therefore uses subject-matter information as fully as the subjectivist does; the two part on whether that information fixes a unique distribution, not on whether it is used. The subjectivist’s advantage over this position is one of expression: an elicited prior can carry partial, qualitative, or comparative knowledge — earlier studies, biological limits, the range a quantity has taken in comparable settings — that does not reduce to the moment constraints and symmetry groups the formal rules require, and it does not presuppose that a unique prior exists. The prior so obtained is also the one a coherent agent’s own preferences already imply (the Savage construction, below). Its cost is that it must be defended rather than read from a rule, which is the work of elicitation, examined below.

A different practice also travels under the name “objective”, and should not be run together with the Jaynesian programme. Reference priors and the weakly informative default priors of applied Bayesian work are constructed to limit the information the prior contributes, so that the data dominate and an analysis is reproducible between investigators.18 Their justification is pragmatic — regularisation, stable computation, a sensible prior predictive distribution — rather than the claim that information fixes the prior; “noninformative” is in any case a misnomer, since a prior flat in one parametrisation is informative in another, the difficulty already noted. These default priors occupy a position between the two schools: with the subjectivist they accept that no unique correct prior exists, and with the objectivist they try to keep the analyst’s particular beliefs out of the result.

The disagreement has practical consequences at one point in the workflow: the prior (Chapters 8 and 9). The subjectivist elicits a prior — it records what someone actually believes, and its defence is that it has been stated in public and can be varied. The objectivist derives a prior — from invariance or maximum entropy19 — and its defence is that anyone accepting the same constraints obtains the same distribution. Neither programme closes the gap completely. The objectivist must still choose the constraints, the parametrisation, and the reference measure before the formal machinery can run, so judgement enters one step earlier rather than not at all. The subjectivist working in science cannot in practice hold an arbitrary prior, because a published prior must survive the criticism of colleagues and sensitivity analysis. This book takes the working position on which the two schools converge: probability is epistemic; assignments should follow from stated information as far as the information goes; and the prior is a declared component of the model, open to criticism and revision like every other component.

Elicitation: the subjective prior made public

Elicitation is the procedure the subjective programme uses to build a prior: a structured interview that converts what an expert knows into a probability distribution, with steps designed to counter the cognitive biases catalogued earlier — overconfidence, anchoring — and to check that the expert’s stated quantiles are mutually coherent.20 It takes the personalist definition of probability at face value: the prior is someone’s degree of belief, and elicitation makes that belief explicit, recorded, and open to revision.

Pooling several experts can look like a passage from the subjective to the objective, and in one sense it is. It raises the intersubjectivity of the prior — agreement across assessors — without making the prior a function of the evidence alone. A consensus prior is still a subjective prior; it is the degree of belief of a group rather than of one person. The two senses of “objective” are distinct: elicitation can deliver agreement, not evidence-determination. Aggregation also introduces choices of its own. Combining distributions by a weighted average (linear pooling) and by a weighted geometric mean (logarithmic pooling) give different results with different properties, and the weights assigned to experts are a further judgement; the subjectivity is relocated, not removed.21

There is nonetheless a real gain. If the experts’ errors are partly independent, their combination can be better calibrated than any single assessment — the averaging effect that also improves a forecast built from many models. This is objectivity in the calibration sense, agreement with observed frequencies, obtained empirically, and it is the sense in which a pooled prior may be “more objective” than its parts. A well-run elicitation represents the experts’ disagreement rather than concealing it: wide disagreement should yield a wide prior, which a later sensitivity analysis can probe. Elicitation is in this way the working compromise between the two schools — a subjective prior made public, checkable, and often better calibrated, at the price of new judgements about whom to consult and how to combine them.

QBism: subjective probability where chance seemed safest

A footnote earlier in this section allowed one possible exception to the claim that all relevant uncertainty is epistemic: quantum mechanics. There is a school of physics that removes the exception. QBism (from “quantum Bayesianism”), proposed by Caves, Fuchs, and Schack and developed by Fuchs, Mermin, and Schack, interprets the probabilities of quantum theory — including those delivered by the Born rule, the rule that converts a quantum state into outcome probabilities22 — as an agent’s personal degrees of belief, in de Finetti’s sense, about the outcomes of her own future measurements.23 On this reading the quantum state is not a property of the physical system but a representation of the agent’s information about it, his personal degrees of belief about the consequences of his own actions on the world. The Born rule is an additional coherence requirement: a constraint, supplementing the Dutch book standard, that a rational agent’s betting odds on measurement outcomes must satisfy. “Collapse” is just Bayesian updating on a new experience; there’s no observer-independent fact about an outcome. The probability that a given nucleus decays within the hour becomes, on the QBist reading, epistemic like every other probability.
QBism goes further than de Finetti’s programme applied to classical problems – it is arguably the most thoroughgoing application of de Finettian personalism ever attempted. For de Finetti, the probability is personal but the underlying fact — whether it rains tomorrow — is agent-independent; in QBism even a measurement outcome is an experience of the agent who makes it. The standard objection is that this amounts to instrumentalism: a theory that organises an agent’s expectations without attempting to describe the world. QBists reply that the position is a realism of an unusual kind, which Fuchs calls participatory realism: the world is real and constrains the agent’s experience; other agents are real; and the fact that the Born rule — this rule and not some other — is the coherence norm that succeeds empirically is an objective discovery about the world, which the quantum formalism therefore characterises indirectly.24 Whether this reply secures enough realism is an open dispute. QBism is a minority interpretation among physicists; it is included here not as settled physics but as evidence of reach: the subjective programme can be stated coherently even in the domain where objective chance appeared unavoidable.

7.2 Road 1: probability as the logic of belief

7.2.1 The robot returns

In Chapter 3 the robot had two design specifications: bivalence (every proposition is TRUE or FALSE) and truth-functionality (a compound’s value depends only on its parts’ values). We now weaken the first — not about the world, but about the robot’s information. Propositions are still either true or false; the robot, however, usually does not know which, and must grade its confidence instead of declaring a verdict. The question is what rules the graded confidence must follow.

We define rationality narrowly as the best way of converting evidence into beliefs, and beliefs into decisions. Probability theory deals with the first part of this definition. Philosophically speaking, one could think of probability theory as a model of rational thinking, rather than the real thing, thus admitting that there might well be more to rationality than is captured by it, while stressing that probability theory does capture an important part of what it means to be rational.

How would you describe the thinking of a narrowly rational robot? What would we want to be the basic rules of reasoning, or desiderata, of such a robot, which we could then formalise into a mathematical description of thinking under uncertainty? In other words, can we present a full list of norms of common sense, from which we can derive a rational thinking machine?25 These rules should be such that a rational person would not want to consciously violate any of them.

The robot reasons about unambiguous Aristotelian propositions, which have truth values (but we don’t need to actually know, whether a proposition is true or false, it suffices that it in principle has this truth-property). So it cannot help with much of ordinary human communication, to which unchanging truth values cannot be assigned.

7.2.2 The desiderata

The robot reasons by assigning degrees of plausibility to propositions, based on evidence given to it. Each piece of new evidence leads to shifting of these plausibilities. The desiderata:

  1. Degrees of plausibility are represented by real numbers. We represent a greater plausibility with a greater number.

  2. Qualitative correspondence with common sense — if the robot gets information that supports hypothesis A, then the plausibility of A increases, and vice versa.

  3. Consistency:

    3.1. If a conclusion can be reasoned in many ways, then each must lead to the same result.

    3.2. The principle of total information — all relevant information must be taken into account when shifting plausibilities. Also note that while causal physical influences propagate only forward in time, logical information works in both directions, so it does not matter when the evidence was collected in relation to the phenomenon it describes.

    3.3. Equivalent states of knowledge are represented by equivalent plausibility numbers.

Surprisingly, these rules are enough to ensure a rational brain. Mathematical probability theory is fully consistent with these desiderata and requires no more than them.

7.2.3 The rules the desiderata force

Cox proved that any system of graded plausibilities satisfying the desiderata can be rescaled, without loss of information, into numbers between 0 and 1 that obey two rules:26

  • The sum rule. \(P(A) + P(\lnot A) = 1\). The plausibilities of a proposition and its negation are not independent quantities; fixing one fixes the other.

  • The product rule. \(P(A \land B) = P(A) \cdot P(B \mid A)\). The plausibility that both A and B are true is the plausibility of A, multiplied by the plausibility of B on the assumption that A is true. The quantity \(P(B \mid A)\) is the conditional probability of B given A.

The proof works by turning each desideratum into a functional equation and solving for the only functions that satisfy them; it is beyond the mathematical level of this book, and we take the result as given. What matters here is its shape: the rules of probability are not one convention among many. Given the desiderata, any calculus of graded belief either is probability theory (after rescaling) or violates one of the desiderata somewhere. This is the sense in which probability theory is the unique consistent extension of the logic of Chapter 3 to reasoning under uncertainty.

7.2.4 No sure loss: the betting road to the same rules

The desiderata route may look abstract, so we now reach the same two rules by the operational argument promised in Chapter 3. There, an assertion was a contract: a penalty of one unit, payable if the asserted proposition turns out false, and inconsistency meant a guaranteed loss. The generalisation under uncertainty replaces all-or-nothing assertion with a graded commitment: your probability for A is the price \(P(A)\), between 0 and 1, at which you are willing to trade a ticket that pays 1 unit if A turns out true and 0 if it turns out false. A price of 1 reproduces full assertion, a price of 0 full denial; the prices in between are the new territory. A set of prices is coherent when no combination of trades, each acceptable at your stated prices, produces a guaranteed loss.

Suppose you price a ticket on A at 0.70 and a ticket on NOT A at 0.50. The prices sum to 1.20, and a bookie sells you both tickets:

tf_money <- function(x) sprintf("%+.2f", x)

buy_notA <- c(0 - 0.50, 1 - 0.50)   # pay 0.50; ticket pays 1 if A false
tibble(world = c("A true", "A false"),
       `buy ticket on A at 0.70` = tf_money(buy_A),
       `buy ticket on NOT A at 0.50` = tf_money(buy_notA),
       `net payoff` = tf_money(buy_A + buy_notA)) %>%
  kable()

Whichever way the world turns out, one ticket pays and the other does not: you collect 1 unit, having paid 1.20 — a guaranteed loss of 0.20, which is the amount by which your prices violate the sum rule. Had the prices summed to less than 1, the bookie would have bought both tickets from you instead. Only prices with \(P(A) + P(\lnot A) = 1\) leave no guaranteed loss. This is the penalty table of Chapter 3 with graded stakes; the sure-loss argument is the same, and only the prices have become adjustable.

The product rule is forced by the called-off bet, foreshadowed in the historical aside of Chapter 3. A conditional ticket on “B given A” at price \(q\) pays 1 unit if A and B are both true, pays nothing if A is true and B false, and is called off — the price \(q\) is refunded — if A is false. The called-off bet is the operational meaning of conditional probability: a commitment about B that is only activated when A turns out true.

Now compare the conditional ticket with a portfolio of unconditional tickets. In every possible world, the conditional ticket’s payoff equals that of holding one ticket on “A AND B” plus \(q\) tickets on “NOT A”. Check the three cases: if A and B are both true, the joint ticket pays 1 and the NOT-A tickets pay nothing; if A is true and B false, every position pays nothing; if A is false, the refund \(q\) equals the payoff of the \(q\) NOT-A tickets. Two positions with identical payoffs in every world must have the same price, otherwise a bookie trades the dear one against the cheap one and pockets the difference risk-free. Suppose your prices are \(P(A) = 0.5\) and \(P(A \land B) = 0.2\), so the matching conditional price is \(0.2 + q \cdot 0.5\), i.e. \(q = 0.4\) — but you price the conditional ticket at \(q = 0.6\):

q <- 0.6; p_A <- 0.5; p_AB <- 0.2

sell_joint <- c(p_AB - 1, p_AB - 0, p_AB - 0)           # receive 0.20; pay 1 if A and B

The loss is 0.10 in every row — the gap between your conditional price (\(0.6 \times 0.5 = 0.30\) per unit stake on A) and your direct price for the joint event (0.20). Coherence requires \(P(B \mid A) \cdot P(A) = P(A \land B)\), which is the product rule. Taken together: a person whose graded commitments violate the sum rule or the product rule can be made to lose money with certainty, by a finite set of trades each of which they consider fair. This result is the Dutch book argument.27 Inconsistency is a sure loss under certainty (Chapter 3); incoherence is a sure loss under uncertainty.

7.2.5 Promises kept: the syllogisms

Chapter 3 required the expanded logic to reduce to classical logic under certainty and to license the weak syllogisms. Both promises can now be kept with the two rules in hand.

Reduction. Set the probabilities to their extremes. If \(P(B \mid A) = 1\) and \(P(A) = 1\), the product rule gives \(P(A \land B) = 1\), so \(P(B) = 1\): modus ponens. If \(P(B \mid A) = 1\) and \(P(B) = 0\), then \(P(A \land B) = 0\), and since \(P(A \land B) = P(A) \cdot 1\), we get \(P(A) = 0\): modus tollens. At the endpoints 0 and 1 the calculus is the propositional logic of Chapter 3.

The weak syllogisms. Suppose A entails B, so that \(P(B \mid A) = 1\), and suppose \(0 < P(A)\) and \(P(B) < 1\). Writing the product rule in both directions, \(P(A \mid B) \cdot P(B) = P(A \land B) = P(A) \cdot P(B \mid A) = P(A)\), so

\[P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A)}{P(B)} \geq P(A).\]

Observing B raises the plausibility of A, and by a definite factor, \(1/P(B)\): the less expected the evidence, the larger the boost. The first weak syllogism is now a theorem, with a number attached. The second — learning NOT A makes B less plausible — follows by a similar two-line argument (Exercise 2). Chapter 8 develops the bookkeeping of such evidence shifts (the likelihood ratio as the weight of evidence) into the engine of Bayesian inference.

The fundamental principle of all probabilistic inference: to judge the truth of proposition A, calculate the probability that A is true, conditional on all the evidence on hand: \(P(A \vert E_1E_2…)\).

7.2.6 What the desiderata do not force

Both derivations establish the same rules — the sum rule, the product rule, and Bayes’ theorem built from them — and they stop there. They fix the relations a set of probabilities must satisfy, not the values placed into those relations. This is where the two epistemic schools described earlier divide, and the division can be located in the desiderata themselves. The calculus follows from desideratum 3.1 — a conclusion reached by several routes must come out the same — together with 1 and 2; a subjectivist grants these without reservation, because they govern the consistency of an assignment and not its starting point. Desideratum 3.3 is the additional clause. Read narrowly, as the requirement that one agent not attach different numbers to relabelled versions of a single problem, it is uncontroversial. Read strongly, as the requirement that any two agents with the same information attach the same numbers, it is the objectivist uniqueness thesis, and it is what licenses the symmetry and maximum-entropy arguments of the objective school. The strong reading is detachable from the rest: adopting the robot commits a reader to the calculus, not to objectivism. Whether the strong form can be met is the open question raised earlier — symmetry and maximum entropy do not single out a prior without a further choice of parametrisation and reference measure.28

7.2.7 Symmetric information and the limits of indifference

The symmetry argument needs a precise account of what “symmetric information” is. The principle of indifference, in its crude form, says: if there is no reason to favour one possibility over another, assign them equal probability. Keynes and others found this embarrassing, because “no reason to favour” is vague and generates contradictions. Jaynes’s contribution was to replace the crude version with a precise, group-theoretic one, and to derive it rather than postulate it.

Information is symmetric with respect to a transformation when (i) the transformation permutes the possibilities, mapping the hypothesis space onto itself, and (ii) the state of knowledge \(I\) is left invariant by it — after relabelling, the problem is word-for-word the same, and nothing in \(I\) distinguishes the original from the transformed version. The consistency desideratum 3.3 then forces the conclusion: if a transformation carries possibility \(A\) into possibility \(B\) while leaving \(I\) unchanged, \(P(A \mid I) = P(B \mid I)\). If the transformations form a group that can carry any outcome into any other, every outcome receives the same probability, and the uniform distribution follows. The principle of indifference becomes a theorem about invariant information, not a bare postulate.

Two extensions complete the bridge to maximum entropy:

  • For a continuous parameter, “equal probability for related cases” becomes “a prior invariant under the group.” A location parameter, for which the problem is unchanged by shifting the origin, forces a uniform prior; a scale parameter, unchanged by multiplication by a positive constant, forces \(p(\sigma) \propto 1/\sigma\). These are Jaynes’s transformation-group priors.
  • With only partial symmetry — some constraints, such as a known mean, but no full invariance — maximum entropy generalises the construction: take the distribution that is as non-committal as possible subject to the constraints, measured against the invariant base. With no constraints it returns the uniform answer; with constraints, the least-committal distribution consistent with them.

The chain is therefore: symmetric information, through desideratum 3.3, gives equal probabilities; transformation groups extend this to continuous parameters; maximum entropy extends it again to the constrained case.

The construction is contestable even for one actor, because it depends on naming the right transformation, and what counts as “symmetric” is relative to how the problem is parametrised. A state of knowledge that is invariant in one parametrisation is not invariant in another, because a non-linear change of variable distorts the measure (the Jacobian is not constant). “I know nothing about \(\theta\)” gives a uniform prior on \(\theta\); but the same ignorance equally licenses a uniform prior on \(\theta^2\) or on \(\log\theta\), and these are different distributions. Nothing in the phrase “I have no information” fixes the coordinate in which ignorance is to equal uniformity. No second agent is needed: one agent, contemplating one state of ignorance, finds that “symmetric information” points to several incompatible priors according to the description chosen.

The cleanest illustration is van Fraassen’s cube factory.29 A factory makes cubes with side length somewhere in \([0,1]\), and nothing further is known. What is \(P(\text{side} \le \tfrac12)\)?

  • Indifference on side length: \(P = \tfrac12\).
  • Indifference on face area (also ranging over \([0,1]\)): side \(\le \tfrac12 \Leftrightarrow\) area \(\le \tfrac14\), giving \(P = \tfrac14\).
  • Indifference on volume (\([0,1]\)): side \(\le \tfrac12 \Leftrightarrow\) volume \(\le \tfrac18\), giving \(P = \tfrac18\).

Three monotone reparametrisations of the same quantity, the same ignorance, three answers (\(\tfrac12\), \(\tfrac14\), \(\tfrac18\)); the principle of indifference does not choose among them. Bertrand’s problem, in the box below, makes the same point in a geometric setting.

Bertrand’s paradox: ‘no information’ has no single answer

The symmetry argument assumes that “symmetric information” picks out one distribution; it need not. Bertrand’s problem asks for the probability that a chord drawn “at random” in a circle of radius \(R\) is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle, which has length \(R\sqrt{3}\). A chord at distance \(d\) from the centre has length \(2\sqrt{R^2 - d^2}\), so it is longer than the side just when \(d < R/2\), and every construction below agrees on that. They disagree on what “at random” means. Drawing the two endpoints uniformly on the circumference gives \(1/3\); placing the midpoint uniformly along a radius gives \(1/2\); placing the midpoint uniformly over the area of the disk gives \(1/4\). Each is a uniform distribution, but over a different parametrisation of the space of chords, and because the maps between those parametrisations are non-linear, uniform in one is not uniform in another.30

So “no information favouring any chord” does not fix a probability; it fixes one only after a parametrisation has been chosen, and indifference does not choose it. Jaynes argued that the problem becomes well posed once the answer is required to be invariant under the full group a physical chord-generating process would respect — rotation, translation, and rescaling — which singles out the \(1/2\) solution. The rescue works, and in doing so makes the point: uniqueness is recovered by adding symmetries that the bare problem does not state, so “symmetric information” determines a prior only relative to a group that must itself be supplied. Maximum entropy inherits the same dependence, since on a continuous space it is defined only against a base measure — a stipulation of what counts as uniform.

7.3 The two roads meet

A small example shows the two roads computing the same number by different routes. A clinic has 10 patients, of whom 3 carry an infection; 2 patients are selected at random for a follow-up visit. What is the probability that both selected patients are carriers?

Road 2 (counting). Count the equally possible cases: there are \(\binom{10}{2} = 45\) ways to select 2 patients out of 10, of which \(\binom{3}{2} = 3\) select 2 of the 3 carriers. The probability is \(3/45 = 1/15\).

Road 1 (the robot). The robot updates plausibilities sequentially with the product rule. Before the first selection, its information is symmetric over the 10 patients, so desideratum 3.3 (equivalent states of knowledge receive equivalent plausibilities) forces \(P(\text{first is a carrier}) = 3/10\). On the assumption that the first selected patient is a carrier, the same symmetry over the remaining 9 patients forces \(P(\text{second is a carrier} \mid \text{first is}) = 2/9\). The product rule gives \(3/10 \times 2/9 = 1/15\).

A Monte Carlo simulation arbitrates:

set.seed(2024)
counting <- choose(3, 2) / choose(10, 2)
robot    <- (3 / 10) * (2 / 9)
mc       <- mean(replicate(1e5,

tibble(route = c("Road 2: counting the cases",
                 "Road 1: product rule, sequential update",
                 "Monte Carlo (100,000 draws)"),
       `P(both carriers)` = round(c(counting, robot, mc), 4)) %>%

Road 1: product rule, sequential update 0.0667

The agreement is not a coincidence, and it shows where counting fits inside the belief reading: counting is what the robot’s calculus reduces to when the available information is symmetric over a finite set of cases. Desideratum 3.3 converts “I have no reason to distinguish the cases” into equal probabilities, and from there the favourable-over-possible ratio of the classical definition follows. The counting road is, on this view, a special case — but a special case of enormous practical importance, because experiments are deliberately built (by randomisation) to make information symmetric. Chapter 5 took the counting road to the binomial distribution.

7.4 The same calculus? The fine print

The two roads agree on the working rules — sum rule, product rule, and everything derived from them. The book uses the rules freely from either direction. The agreement nevertheless has fine print, and stating it is part of the deal.

First, the roads disagree about what carries probability. On the belief road, probabilities attach to propositions, given a state of knowledge; it is meaningful to ask for the probability of a single event — that this patient dies within a week, that it rains tomorrow — and the section on understanding uncertainty above already used probabilities this way. On the counting road, probability is most naturally a property of a repeatable setup: a ratio over a sample space of cases. A single-case probability then needs an auxiliary device (a hypothetical infinite population of tomorrows, of which a fraction are rainy), and the weather example earlier in this chapter shows how strained that device can become. The two roads can price the same calculations while telling different stories about what was calculated.

Second, the formal agreement is not a perfect overlap. Cox’s theorem requires technical assumptions beyond the verbal desiderata (notably that plausibilities vary smoothly enough for the functional equations to have unique solutions); making the argument fully rigorous is its own research literature.31 And the agreement covers Kolmogorov’s axioms only in their finite form: the belief-road arguments — desiderata and Dutch book alike — force additivity over finitely many mutually exclusive alternatives, but do not force Kolmogorov’s countable additivity, the extension to infinite collections that underpins the measure-theoretic limit theorems. De Finetti, the author of the Dutch book argument in its developed form, regarded countable additivity as a useful convention rather than a requirement of coherence. For everything done in this book — finite data sets, finite simulations, finite parameter grids — the distinction has no practical consequence, and we will not mention it again.

The position taken in this book is the one already implicit in this chapter’s opening: probability is epistemic — degrees of belief constrained to be coherent — and counting and long-run frequency are the special cases that arise when information is symmetric or data are plentiful. When the two readings both apply, they agree; when only one applies (single cases, unique hypotheses, parameters that have one true value), the belief reading keeps working. That is the practical reason this book is Bayesian, ahead of any philosophical one.

7.5 Challenges to the Bayesian position

This book has committed to a position that probability is epistemic, the prior is a declared component of the model open to criticism, and counting and frequency are the special cases that arise when information is symmetric or data are plentiful. That position has been attacked from several directions. Some rest on a confusion the earlier sections already remove; others identify genuine open problems, on which alternative theories of rational choice have been built. Setting them out is part of stating the position fairly. The aim throughout is still to honestly quantify the uncertainty that can be quantified, and not to claim more than available information supports.

7.5.1 A single-event probability “says nothing”

The first objection holds that a probability attached to a one-off event — the result of one election, whether a particular patient survives the year — is empty, because a single event has no long-run frequency. As a claim about meaning the earlier sections have answered it: on the belief road \(P(A)\) grades a state of knowledge, and the weather and patient examples used such probabilities throughout. Importing the frequency definition and then noting that a single case has no frequency assumes the conclusion.

A real point concerns evaluation, not meaning. A single probabilistic forecast cannot be checked against its single outcome: a forecast of 0.7 for an event that then fails to occur is not thereby shown wrong, because events assigned probability 0.3 occur about thirty per cent of the time. Judging whether a source’s probabilities are any good requires many of them, and a score that rewards calibration — the long-run agreement between stated probabilities and observed frequencies — of which the Brier score is the original example.32 The 2016 United States presidential election, often offered as a failure of probabilistic forecasting, marks the distinction: the better-calibrated forecasts gave the eventual winner, Donald Trump, the higher probability of 0.3, and the public error was to read a probability of 0.7 for the losing candidate, Hilary Clinton, as near-certainty. Stated carefully, the objection is not that single-event probabilities are meaningless but that they cannot be validated one at a time. That is correct — calibration is assessed over a forecaster’s whole record (Chapter 2).

7.5.2 Radical uncertainty and the prior

A deeper objection holds that in many real settings a prior cannot be set at all, so the calculation never starts. It has a serious lineage: Knight separated risk, where the odds are known, from uncertainty, where they are not33; Keynes held that for some questions “we simply do not know”34; and Kay and King argue that economic and political decisions are typically non-repeatable and non-stationary, so attaching a number to them is false precision rather than information35.

The part of this objection with force is a result, which needs a bit of background. While a hard-to-elicit prior is merely a practical hurdle, the more serious attack claims that there are reasonable preferences which cannot be represented by a prior at all. Understanding its bite requires a result that points the other way: that a rational agent’s choices already contain a prior, stated or not. That result is Savage’s, whose construction does not begin with probabilities, but with an agent’s preferences among acts — options whose payoff depends on which state of the world turns out to hold — together with a small set of axioms those preferences are required to obey. A representation theorem follows: any agent whose preferences obey these axioms behaves as if she had attached a single probability to each state and a utility to each payoff, and then ranked acts by expected utility. The prior is therefore not an input to the theory but a consequence of it. In other words, the existence of a subjective prior is a theorem about coherent preferences, not an ad hoc assumption.

One of those axioms is the sure-thing principle: if two acts yield the same payoff across some set of states, the preference between the acts must not depend on what that shared payoff is — so the states on which the acts agree can be set aside, and the choice made on the states where they differ.36 Ellsberg built a wager on which reasonable people violate it.37

An urn holds 90 balls: 30 are red, and the other 60 are black or yellow in a proportion no one is told. The states are the colour drawn; an act is a bet that pays £100 on the colours it names and £0 otherwise. Consider four such bets:

Bet pays £100 on… Red (30 balls) Black (?) Yellow (?)
red 100 0 0
black 0 100 0
red or yellow 100 0 100
black or yellow 0 100 100

Only the share of red is known (30/90), so the bets on red and on black or yellow carry known odds, while the bets on black and on red or yellow carry unknown ones. Offered the choice, most people take the bet on red over the bet on black, and the bet on black or yellow over the bet on red or yellow — the known odds each time.

The two ideas now bite. By the representation theorem, anyone holding a single prior for the urn — treating the 60 unknown balls as, say, 30 black and 30 yellow — would score each bet by its chance of paying and rank the four once and for all. By the sure-thing principle, the first two bets agree on yellow (both pay 0), and the last two are those same two bets with the yellow payoff raised to 100 for both; yellow is a shared payoff within each pair, so it should cancel, and the red-versus-black ranking should carry over to the (red-or-yellow)-versus-(black-or-yellow) ranking. The observed choices reverse it. Equivalently, preferring the bet on red requires \(P(\text{red}) > P(\text{black})\), while preferring the bet on black-or-yellow requires \(P(\text{black}) > P(\text{red})\); no prior satisfies both. The preferences are stable and widely shared, yet no single prior represents them — the gap the objection points to. This ambiguity aversion is reproducible and arguably rational, so it places such preferences outside the scope of Savage’s theorem: “I cannot fix a sharp prior” is then a position the axioms do not exclude, not a confusion about probability.

The orthodox reply is to add a level. If the composition of the urn is unknown, put a prior over it — and in this symmetric problem any prior that treats black and yellow alike forces the predictive probabilities to \(P(\text{red}) = P(\text{black}) = P(\text{yellow}) = 1/3\), whereupon the ambiguity disappears and the problem reduces to ordinary risk. This is de Finetti’s reduction: beliefs about an unknown frequency, weighted by a prior over its values, collapse to a single predictive distribution. The move is available, and here the choice of second-order prior does not even matter, because symmetry fixes the answer. Two things limit it. First, the reduced model predicts indifference between the urns, which is not what people do; it recommends against the phenomenon rather than accounting for it. Second, the symmetry is special — in an asymmetric problem the predictive probabilities depend on the parametrisation of the “ignorance” prior, the difficulty the objective-Bayes section already noted. Whether ambiguity may always be reduced to risk in this way is the contested step, and the models below take the other branch.

7.5.3 Sets of priors: imprecise probability and robust Bayes

If a single sharp prior overstates what is known, the response that keeps the calculus is to carry a set of priors rather than one. A closed set of distributions — a credal set — assigns each event a lower and an upper probability, \(\underline P(A) = \inf_p p(A)\) and \(\overline P(A) = \sup_p p(A)\) over the set; the width \(\overline P(A) - \underline P(A)\) records how little is known, and is zero only when the set collapses to a point and probability becomes precise. Total ignorance is the vacuous set, \(\underline P(A) = 0\) and \(\overline P(A) = 1\) — which is what ignorance looks like, in contrast to the uniform prior, a definite symmetric commitment that the objective-Bayes section warned against confusing with it.

Two traditions develop this with the same mathematics and different emphasis. Imprecise probability rebuilds the foundations: where de Finetti’s agent posts one fair price for a gamble, Walley’s agent posts two — a highest buying price and a lowest selling price — and declines to trade in between, so the betting argument of Road 1 generalises by dropping the assumption that every gamble has a single fair price.38 A concrete instance is the imprecise Dirichlet model for proportions, which begins vacuous and contracts as data accumulate.39 Robust Bayes keeps ordinary Bayes but, conceding that one prior cannot be pinned down, specifies a class of priors, recomputes the posterior quantity for each, and reports the range; a narrow range means the conclusion is insensitive to the prior, a wide one that the data do not determine it.40 This is the global form of the sensitivity analysis the chapter has already endorsed. A decision still has to be made from a set, and one rule is maxmin expected utility: score each option by its expected utility under the least favourable prior in the set — its worst case over the priors entertained — and choose the option whose worst case is best.41 In Ellsberg’s urn this reproduces the preference for known odds, because a bet on a known colour has a value every prior agrees on, whereas a bet on an unknown colour is judged by the composition least favourable to it. A separate limit is worth keeping in view: every guarantee here holds relative to the set of models entertained, and if the data-generating process lies outside all of them, coherence no longer ensures the conclusion is correct.42

7.5.4 Dilation

Carrying a set of priors has a consequence with no analogue in single-prior analysis, and it bears directly on later chapters. Ordinarily, data narrow beliefs. With a set of priors, conditioning on an observation can widen the interval: for some partitions one can know in advance that, whichever cell is observed, the posterior interval for a proposition will be wider than the prior interval. This is dilation.43

A fair coin is to be tossed, so the probability of heads is \(1/2\). A second person announces a prediction of the toss, but nothing is known of their reliability — the credal set admits everything from a perfect predictor to a perfect anti-predictor. Before the prediction is revealed the probability of heads is \(1/2\); after it is revealed, the conditional probability of heads ranges over the whole interval from near 0 to near 1, because the set contains distributions under which the prediction is highly informative in either direction. An uninterpretable signal from a source of unknown reliability widens a probability that was previously sharp, and one could have foreseen that any prediction would do so.

Dilation is not an inconsistency: the model stays coherent, and no sure loss can be made against it. Whether it is a defect or a virtue is disputed. Read as a defect, an inference that becomes less certain on data looks pathological. Read as a virtue, the precise \(1/2\) was the artefact — it relied on an unstated assumption that the prediction was independent of the coin, and once that assumption is withdrawn the wide interval is the faithful report. The phenomenon also corrects a natural assumption about robust Bayes: because robust Bayes updates a set of priors by the same rule, it dilates too. Where a robust-Bayes analysis shows no dilation, it is because the class was anchored tightly to an informative base prior — that is, more was assumed than the radical-uncertainty objection grants. One cannot claim both deep ignorance, which requires a wide set, and immunity from dilation, which requires a narrow one.

The phenomenon has a direct counterpart in applied work. When the sign or magnitude of a bias — unmeasured confounding, an unknown selection mechanism — is genuinely not known, an analysis that represents that ignorance faithfully can yield bounds the data fail to narrow, and that may widen on further conditioning. This is the logic of partial identification and of the sensitivity analyses developed in Chapter 19; dilation is the same fact stated at the level of belief.

7.5.5 Is ambiguity aversion irrational?

The biases catalogued in Chapter 2 — base-rate neglect, the conjunction fallacy, anchoring — are departures from probability or expected-utility theory that people renounce once the structure is pointed out. It is tempting to file ambiguity aversion alongside them, as one more failure to reason coherently. There is a strong argument for doing so, due to Raiffa: if you are indifferent between betting on red and betting on black in the unknown urn, decide the colour by a fair coin; the resulting mixture has a known one-half chance of paying, matching the known urn, so a coherent agent should not prefer one urn to the other.44 On this view ambiguity aversion forgoes a sure equivalence, and it does attenuate with incentives and learning.

Two features distinguish it from the Chapter 2 biases. It survives reflection: sophisticated agents shown the argument frequently continue to prefer known odds and defend the preference, whereas the mark of a fallacy is that one abandons it once it is seen. And it has been given normative, not merely descriptive, axiomatisations — Choquet expected utility45, maxmin expected utility46, and smooth-ambiguity preferences47 are theories of rational choice that weaken the sure-thing principle deliberately, and it has been argued that committing to a precise prior one has no information to justify is itself the less rational course.48 Whether ambiguity aversion is irrational therefore turns on whether Savage’s axioms are taken as constitutive of rationality or as one model of it — and this book has already taken the modest view, that probability theory captures an important part of rational thinking rather than the whole of it. On that view the cleaner classification is not that ambiguity aversion fails to meet the standard, but that it disputes which standard applies.

7.5.6 The position taken here

None of these challenges refutes the calculus; each bounds the scope of one way of using it. Single-event probabilities are meaningful and are validated over many forecasts, not one. Under radical uncertainty a sharp prior may not be available, and a set of priors with a stated decision rule is the disciplined response, at the price of phenomena such as dilation. Ambiguity aversion is better read as a rival standard than as a mistake. For the work in this book — finite data, models with declared structure, priors that must survive criticism and sensitivity analysis — a single prior, varied and checked, is adequate, and is what the later chapters use; the apparatus of sets of priors is held in reserve for the cases, flagged where they arise, in which a single prior would assert more than is known. The aim is unchanged from the chapter’s first page: to quantify the uncertainty that can be quantified, and to mark plainly the uncertainty that cannot.

7.6 Key points

  • Probability theory is constructed here twice. Road 1 extends the logic of Chapter 3 to degrees of belief: the robot’s desiderata force the calculus (Cox), and so does the requirement of no sure loss on graded commitments (the Dutch book). Road 2 formalises counting: equally possible cases, stated in full generality by the Kolmogorov axioms.
  • The whole calculus is two rules: the sum rule \(P(A) + P(\lnot A) = 1\) and the product rule \(P(A \land B) = P(A)\,P(B \mid A)\).
  • The called-off bet is the operational meaning of conditional probability: a commitment about B activated only when A turns out true — the three-valued structure of Chapter 3’s historical aside, put to work.
  • The calculus keeps Chapter 3’s promises: at probabilities 0 and 1 it reproduces the strong syllogisms, and it licenses the weak ones quantitatively — if A entails B, then \(P(A \mid B) = P(A)/P(B) \geq P(A)\).
  • Counting is the symmetric special case of the belief calculus: when information does not distinguish a finite set of cases, desideratum 3.3 makes them equally probable, and probability reduces to favourable-over-possible.
  • Additivity (axiom 3) requires mutual exclusivity, not independence.
  • The fine print of the agreement: Cox’s theorem needs technical assumptions, and the belief road forces only finite — not countable — additivity. The roads also differ on what carries probability (propositions and single cases vs repeatable setups). None of this affects the working rules used in this book.
  • Within the epistemic reading, the subjective school (Ramsey, de Finetti, Savage) requires only coherence and expects agreement to emerge from shared data, while the objective school (Jeffreys, Jaynes) requires that the same information yield the same probability, and supplies symmetry, invariance, and maximum-entropy rules for the assignment. The dispute concerns where prior probabilities come from; both schools deny that probability is physical.
  • The Bayesian position faces genuine challenges as well as misunderstandings. A single-event probability is meaningful but is validated only over many forecasts, through calibration. Under radical uncertainty a sharp prior may not be elicitable, and Ellsberg’s experiment exhibits reproducible ambiguity aversion that violates Savage’s sure-thing principle, so no single prior represents the preferences. The disciplined response carries a set of priors (imprecise probability, robust Bayes) with a decision rule such as maxmin expected utility — at the cost of dilation, in which conditioning can widen rather than narrow the interval. These bound the scope of the single-prior workflow without refuting the calculus.
