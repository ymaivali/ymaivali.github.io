Appendix H — Challenges to the Bayesian position

In Chapter 8 the book committed to a position: probability is epistemic, the prior is a declared component of the model open to criticism, and counting and frequency are the special cases that arise when information is symmetric or data are plentiful. That position has been attacked from several directions. Some rest on a confusion the sections of that chapter already remove; others identify genuine open problems, on which alternative theories of rational choice have been built. Setting them out is part of stating the position fairly. The aim throughout is still to honestly quantify the uncertainty that can be quantified, and not to claim more than available information supports.

H.1 A single-event probability “says nothing”

The first objection holds that a probability attached to a one-off event — the result of one election, whether a particular patient survives the year — is empty, because a single event has no long-run frequency. As a claim about meaning, Chapter 8 has answered it: on the belief road \(P(A)\) grades a state of knowledge, and the weather and patient examples of that chapter used such probabilities throughout. Importing the frequency definition and then noting that a single case has no frequency assumes the conclusion.

A real point concerns evaluation, not meaning. A single probabilistic forecast cannot be checked against its single outcome: a forecast of 0.7 for an event that then fails to occur is not thereby shown wrong, because events assigned probability 0.3 occur about thirty per cent of the time. Judging whether a source’s probabilities are any good requires many of them, and a score that rewards calibration — the long-run agreement between stated probabilities and observed frequencies — of which the Brier score is the original example.1 The 2016 United States presidential election, often offered as a failure of probabilistic forecasting, marks the distinction: the better-calibrated forecasts gave the eventual winner, Donald Trump, the higher probability of 0.3, and the public error was to read a probability of 0.7 for the losing candidate, Hilary Clinton, as near-certainty. Stated carefully, the objection is not that single-event probabilities are meaningless but that they cannot be validated one at a time. That is correct — calibration is assessed over a forecaster’s whole record (Chapter 2), where the interval version of the same measurement, and the reasons an interval-production task cannot be scored properly at all, are set out in detail.

H.2 Radical uncertainty and the prior

A deeper objection holds that in many real settings a prior cannot be set at all, so the calculation never starts. It has a serious lineage: Knight separated risk, where the odds are known, from uncertainty, where they are not2; Keynes held that for some questions “we simply do not know”3; and Kay and King argue that economic and political decisions are typically non-repeatable and non-stationary, so attaching a number to them is false precision rather than information4.

The part of this objection with force is a result, which needs a bit of background. While a hard-to-elicit prior is merely a practical hurdle, the more serious attack claims that there are reasonable preferences which cannot be represented by a prior at all. Understanding its bite requires a result that points the other way: that a rational agent’s choices already contain a prior, stated or not. That result is Savage’s, whose construction does not begin with probabilities, but with an agent’s preferences among acts — options whose payoff depends on which state of the world turns out to hold — together with a small set of axioms those preferences are required to obey. A representation theorem follows: any agent whose preferences obey these axioms behaves as if they had attached a single probability to each state and a utility to each payoff, and then ranked acts by expected utility. The prior is therefore not an input to the theory but a consequence of it. In other words, the existence of a subjective prior is a theorem about coherent preferences, not an ad hoc assumption.

One of those axioms is the sure-thing principle: if two acts yield the same payoff across some set of states, the preference between the acts must not depend on what that shared payoff is — so the states on which the acts agree can be set aside, and the choice made on the states where they differ.5 Ellsberg built a wager on which reasonable people violate it.6

An urn holds 90 balls: 30 are red, and the other 60 are black or yellow in a proportion no one is told. The states are the colour drawn; an act is a bet that pays £100 on the colours it names and £0 otherwise. Consider four such bets (Table H.1):

Only the share of red is known (30/90), so the bets on red and on black or yellow carry known odds, while the bets on black and on red or yellow carry unknown ones. Offered the choice, most people take the bet on red over the bet on black, and the bet on black or yellow over the bet on red or yellow — the known odds each time.

The two ideas now bite. By the representation theorem, anyone holding a single prior for the urn — treating the 60 unknown balls as, say, 30 black and 30 yellow — would score each bet by its chance of paying and rank the four once and for all. By the sure-thing principle, the first two bets agree on yellow (both pay 0), and the last two are those same two bets with the yellow payoff raised to 100 for both; yellow is a shared payoff within each pair, so it should cancel, and the red-versus-black ranking should carry over to the (red-or-yellow)-versus-(black-or-yellow) ranking. The observed choices reverse it. Equivalently, preferring the bet on red requires \(P(\text{red}) > P(\text{black})\), while preferring the bet on black-or-yellow requires \(P(\text{black}) > P(\text{red})\); no prior satisfies both. The preferences are stable and widely shared, yet no single prior represents them — the gap the objection points to. This ambiguity aversion is reproducible and arguably rational, so it places such preferences outside the scope of Savage’s theorem: “I cannot fix a sharp prior” is then a position the axioms do not exclude, not a confusion about probability.

The orthodox reply is to add a level. If the composition of the urn is unknown, put a prior over it — and in this symmetric problem any prior that treats black and yellow alike forces the predictive probabilities to \(P(\text{red}) = P(\text{black}) = P(\text{yellow}) = 1/3\), whereupon the ambiguity disappears and the problem reduces to ordinary risk. This is de Finetti’s reduction: beliefs about an unknown frequency, weighted by a prior over its values, collapse to a single predictive distribution. The move is available, and here the choice of second-order prior does not even matter, because symmetry fixes the answer. Two things limit it. First, the reduced model predicts indifference between the urns, which is not what people do; it recommends against the phenomenon rather than accounting for it. Second, the symmetry is special — in an asymmetric problem the predictive probabilities depend on the parametrisation of the “ignorance” prior, the difficulty Chapter 8 noted for objective priors. Whether ambiguity may always be reduced to risk in this way is the contested step, and the models below take the other branch.

H.3 Sets of priors: imprecise probability and robust Bayes

If a single sharp prior overstates what is known, the response that keeps the calculus is to carry a set of priors rather than one. A closed set of distributions — a credal set — assigns each event a lower and an upper probability, \(\underline P(A) = \inf_p p(A)\) and \(\overline P(A) = \sup_p p(A)\) over the set; the width \(\overline P(A) - \underline P(A)\) records how little is known, and is zero only when the set collapses to a point and probability becomes precise. Total ignorance is the vacuous set, \(\underline P(A) = 0\) and \(\overline P(A) = 1\) — which is what ignorance looks like, in contrast to the uniform prior, a definite symmetric commitment that Chapter 8 warned against confusing with it.

Two traditions develop this with the same mathematics and different emphasis. Imprecise probability rebuilds the foundations: where de Finetti’s agent posts one fair price for a gamble, Walley’s agent posts two — a highest buying price and a lowest selling price — and declines to trade in between, so the betting argument of Chapter 8 generalises by dropping the assumption that every gamble has a single fair price.7 A concrete instance is the imprecise Dirichlet model for proportions, which begins vacuous and contracts as data accumulate.8 Robust Bayes keeps ordinary Bayes but, conceding that one prior cannot be pinned down, specifies a class of priors, recomputes the posterior quantity for each, and reports the range; a narrow range means the conclusion is insensitive to the prior, a wide one that the data do not determine it.9 This is the global form of the sensitivity analysis Chapter 8 endorsed. A decision still has to be made from a set, and one rule is maxmin expected utility: score each option by its expected utility under the least favourable prior in the set — its worst case over the priors entertained — and choose the option whose worst case is best.10 In Ellsberg’s urn this reproduces the preference for known odds, because a bet on a known colour has a value every prior agrees on, whereas a bet on an unknown colour is judged by the composition least favourable to it. A separate limit is worth keeping in view: every guarantee here holds relative to the set of models entertained, and if the data-generating process lies outside all of them, coherence no longer ensures the conclusion is correct.11

H.4 Dilation

Carrying a set of priors has a consequence with no analogue in single-prior analysis, and it bears directly on later chapters. Ordinarily, data narrow beliefs. With a set of priors, conditioning on an observation can widen the interval: for some partitions one can know in advance that, whichever cell is observed, the posterior interval for a proposition will be wider than the prior interval. This is dilation.12

A fair coin is to be tossed, so the probability of heads is \(1/2\). A second person announces a prediction of the toss, but nothing is known of their reliability — the credal set admits everything from a perfect predictor to a perfect anti-predictor. Before the prediction is revealed the probability of heads is \(1/2\); after it is revealed, the conditional probability of heads ranges over the whole interval from near 0 to near 1, because the set contains distributions under which the prediction is highly informative in either direction. An uninterpretable signal from a source of unknown reliability widens a probability that was previously sharp, and one could have foreseen that any prediction would do so.

Dilation is not an inconsistency: the model stays coherent, and no sure loss can be made against it. Whether it is a defect or a virtue is disputed. Read as a defect, an inference that becomes less certain on data looks pathological. Read as a virtue, the precise \(1/2\) was the artefact — it relied on an unstated assumption that the prediction was independent of the coin, and once that assumption is withdrawn the wide interval is the faithful report. The phenomenon also corrects a natural assumption about robust Bayes: because robust Bayes updates a set of priors by the same rule, it dilates too. Where a robust-Bayes analysis shows no dilation, it is because the class was anchored tightly to an informative base prior — that is, more was assumed than the radical-uncertainty objection grants. One cannot claim both deep ignorance, which requires a wide set, and immunity from dilation, which requires a narrow one.

The phenomenon has a direct counterpart in applied work. When the sign or magnitude of a bias — unmeasured confounding, an unknown selection mechanism — is genuinely not known, an analysis that represents that ignorance faithfully can yield bounds the data fail to narrow, and that may widen on further conditioning. This is the logic of partial identification and of the sensitivity analyses developed in Chapter 20; dilation is the same fact stated at the level of belief.

H.5 Is ambiguity aversion irrational?

The biases catalogued in Chapter 2 — base-rate neglect, the conjunction fallacy, anchoring — are departures from probability or expected-utility theory that people renounce once the structure is pointed out. It is tempting to file ambiguity aversion alongside them, as one more failure to reason coherently. There is a strong argument for doing so, due to Raiffa: if you are indifferent between betting on red and betting on black in the unknown urn, decide the colour by a fair coin; the resulting mixture has a known one-half chance of paying, matching the known urn, so a coherent agent should not prefer one urn to the other.13 On this view ambiguity aversion forgoes a sure equivalence, and it does attenuate with incentives and learning.

Two features distinguish it from the Chapter 2 biases. It survives reflection: sophisticated agents shown the argument frequently continue to prefer known odds and defend the preference, whereas the mark of a fallacy is that one abandons it once it is seen. And it has been given normative, not merely descriptive, axiomatisations — Choquet expected utility14, maxmin expected utility15, and smooth-ambiguity preferences16 are theories of rational choice that weaken the sure-thing principle deliberately, and it has been argued that committing to a precise prior one has no information to justify is itself the less rational course.17 Whether ambiguity aversion is irrational therefore turns on whether Savage’s axioms are taken as constitutive of rationality or as one model of it — and this book has already taken the modest view, that probability theory captures an important part of rational thinking rather than the whole of it. On that view the cleaner classification is not that ambiguity aversion fails to meet the standard, but that it disputes which standard applies.

H.6 The position taken here

None of these challenges refutes the calculus; each bounds the scope of one way of using it. Single-event probabilities are meaningful and are validated over many forecasts, not one. Under radical uncertainty a sharp prior may not be available, and a set of priors with a stated decision rule is the disciplined response, at the price of phenomena such as dilation. Ambiguity aversion is better read as a rival standard than as a mistake. For the work in this book — finite data, models with declared structure, priors that must survive criticism and sensitivity analysis — a single prior, varied and checked, is adequate, and is what the later chapters use; the apparatus of sets of priors is held in reserve for the cases, flagged where they arise, in which a single prior would assert more than is known. The aim is unchanged from the first page of Chapter 8: to quantify the uncertainty that can be quantified, and to mark plainly the uncertainty that cannot.

  1. The second-order prior in Ellsberg’s urn. Take the 90-ball urn (30 red; 60 black or yellow, unknown split). Put a prior on the number of black balls that is symmetric in black and yellow, and show that the predictive probabilities are \(P(\text{red}) = P(\text{black}) = P(\text{yellow}) = 1/3\), so an expected-utility agent is indifferent within each pair of bets in the table. State which empirical fact this prediction fails to match, and name a decision rule that reproduces the observed preference for known odds.

  2. Dilation. In the coin-and-predictor example, explain why learning the prediction widens the interval for “heads” from the point \(1/2\) to almost all of \([0, 1]\), and identify the single assumption a precise-prior analysis would have to add to keep the probability at \(1/2\). Does robust Bayes avoid the widening, and why?
