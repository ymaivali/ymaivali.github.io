25  Generalised linear models and the scale of effects

Every regression so far in Part V has had a continuous outcome, while the master cohort’s actual question asks whether a blood-pressure-lowering drug prevents stroke, which is a binary (0/1) event. Part IV fitted models to binary outcome taking their form on trust; this chapter takes up the binary outcome, and with it the family of models that handles outcomes that are counts, proportions and events rather than unbounded quantities.

Two of the changes are technical. The outcome is no longer normally distributed, so the likelihood changes; and its mean is confined to \([0, 1]\), so a linear predictor cannot be equated with the mean directly.

The third change is conceptual. While with a continuous outcome there is only one natural way to express an effect: a difference of means, in the outcome’s own units, with a binary outcome there are at least three — a risk difference, a risk ratio and an odds ratio — which are not restatements of a single quantity. They can rank subgroups differently (Chapter 17), they behave differently under adjustment, and only some of them answer the question a decision-maker asks. The link function of a generalised linear model commits the model to a scale on which covariate effects add up. The coefficient it reports is on that scale. However, what the model reports need not follow the link: in this book every effect is obtained as a standardised contrast of predictions on the risk-difference and risk-ratio scales (Chapter 11, Chapter 13), whatever link the model was fitted with. The chapter therefore has two tasks. To show why a coefficient is not what we report and to explain what the choice of link is about.

25.1 The three parts of a generalised linear model

A GLM has three components, one of them new.

The random component is the stochastic data model or likelihood, a.k.a. distribution assumed for the outcome given the predictors: normal, Bernoulli, binomial, Poisson, gamma, beta. These are the exponential-family distributions of Chapter 7, and membership in that family is what binds these into a single framework.

The systematic component is the deterministic process model of the earlier chapters, the linear predictor,

\[\eta_i = \alpha + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \beta_2 x_{i2} + \dots + \beta_K x_{iK}\]

Everything that has been said about it applies: the adjustment set is read off a DAG and not chosen by model fit, a continuous confounder still needs a spline rather than a straight line and including interactions is still the default strategy.

The link function \(g\) is the new part. It connects the mean of the outcome to the linear predictor,

\[g(\mu_i) = \eta_i, \qquad \mu_i = g^{-1}(\eta_i),\]

so that a mean confined to a restricted range can be modelled by an unrestricted linear predictor. The identity link, \(g(\mu) = \mu\), is the linear model of the earlier chapters. For a probability \(\mu \in (0,1)\) the logit \(\log\{\mu/(1-\mu)\}\) maps to the whole real line; for a positive rate the log does the same. It is the model’s mean that is transformed, not the data, which is why a GLM is not the same as regressing \(\log(y)\) on \(x\), and why zeroes in the outcome cause no difficulty. The families and links the book uses are listed in Table 25.1.

Two rows of Table 25.1 use the same family with different links, which makes the point that the link is not determined by the outcome type. A binary outcome can be modelled with a logit link, a log link, or an identity link. All three are generalised linear models for the same data; they differ in which effect measure their coefficients report and, more importantly, in how covariate effects combine in predictions for fitted models. The widespread convention of calling the logit “canonical” for the Bernoulli likelihood is really a statement about the algebra of the exponential family and about the convenience of maximum-likelihood estimation. It is not a statement that the odds ratio is the effect measure the question calls for. The last column of the table says what a coefficient is; what the book reports is the same in every row, a standardised risk difference and risk ratio, and the next section shows the three links for a binary outcome delivering it.

What does not change

A GLM changes the likelihood and inserts a link. It changes nothing about causal identification. The adjustment set is still whatever the DAG says blocks the back-door paths (Chapter 12); a mediator is still ruinous to adjust for (Chapter 16); a collider is still ruinous to condition on (Chapter 12); and no choice of family or link converts an unidentified contrast into an identified one. Nor does it change the reporting convention: the effect is still obtained as a contrast of model predictions, standardised over the covariate distribution with marginaleffects, and not read off a coefficient. As will be seen, the non-identity link makes that convention matter more than it did in Chapter 21, where the coefficient and the marginal contrast happened to coincide.

The cohort is loaded as before. Its answer key is the set of true marginal contrasts computed from the potential-outcome risks.

Under no treatment 6.8% of the cohort would have a stroke and under universal treatment 4.1%. The same effect can be reported as a risk difference of -0.0272, a risk ratio of 0.600, or an odds ratio of 0.583. These are three descriptions of one intervention, and each of the three is a target a differently-linked model can be aimed at. The crude association points the other way, as it has since Chapter 4: among the treated 5.8% had a stroke against 3.5% of the untreated, because the patients who get the drug are the sicker ones.

25.2 One outcome, three links

The stroke outcome is now fitted with the DAG-based adjustment set separately three times - with the identity, the logit and the log link. Each fit is standardised to the marginal risk difference and risk ratio with avg_comparisons(), as the linear models of Chapter 21 were. (The coefficients are looked at afterwards, in a section of their own.) A reader who wants the recipe and not the lesson can stop at the end of this section: fit with the link the mechanism suggests, standardise, report.

25.2.1 A straight line for a probability

The simplest option is to ignore the boundedness of probabilities and fit the model of Chapter 22 to the 0/1 outcome. This is the linear probability model: an identity link with a normal likelihood, so that

\[P(Y_i = 1 \mid X_i) = \alpha + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \dots + \beta_K x_{iK}.\]

Its coefficients are in probability units, so \(\beta_1\) is the change in the probability of stroke per unit of \(x_1\) and, for a binary treatment, a risk difference. This is the one place in the chapter where the coefficient and the standardised contrast coincide, and they coincide only because the model has neither interaction nor curvature: with either, the slope for drug would differ between patients and the coefficient would be an average of nothing in particular.

The priors are reasoned in probability units. The intercept is the stroke risk of an untreated patient at the reference covariate values, a few per cent in a hypertensive cohort, so \(normal(0.05, 0.10)\) allows anything from zero to about a quarter. Each coefficient is a change in risk per unit of its predictor, and \(normal(0, 0.10)\) puts most of its mass within twenty percentage points either way. The residual sd of a 0/1 outcome with mean near 0.05 is about 0.22, so an exponential prior with mean 0.25 covers it without constraining it.

pri_lpm <- c(prior(normal(0.05, 0.10), class = "Intercept"),
             prior(normal(0, 0.10),    class = "b"),
             prior(exponential(4),     class = "sigma"))

m_lpm <- brm(stroke ~ drug + severity + age_c + diabetes,
             family = gaussian(), data = df, prior = pri_lpm,
             chains = 4, iter = 800, warmup = 400, seed = 1,
             file = "models/ch24_lpm", file_refit = "on_change")
rd_lpm  <- avg_comparisons(m_lpm, variables = "drug")

Two things are true at once. The standardised risk difference, -0.0251 with a credible interval of [-0.0295, -0.0208], is close to the true -0.0272. And 12.5% of the fitted values are negative probabilities, the lowest being -0.131. The model is internally incoherent about individuals while being roughly right about the population average.

That combination is characteristic and explains why the linear probability model survives in econometrics despite the obvious objection. What is being estimated is a weighted average of slopes of the true risk function, and if that function is not badly curved over the region the data occupy, the average is close to the average causal effect. What fails is everything that depends on the individual predictions: a predicted risk of -0.131 cannot be used for counselling a patient, cannot be calibrated, and cannot be extrapolated beyond the observed covariate range at all. The model also asserts, through its normal likelihood, a constant residual variance, which for a Bernoulli outcome is false by construction — the variance is \(\mu(1-\mu)\) and therefore depends on the predictors.

The reasonable use of the linear probability model is as a working model for a population-average risk difference in a setting where the risks are not near 0 or 1 and the covariates are few — for instance a randomised trial with a treatment indicator and little else. Outside that setting it is better to fit a model that respects the boundary and then to standardise it, which is what the two links that follow do.

25.2.2 Logistic regression

The second strategy keeps the linear predictor and bends it. We want the fitted probability to stay inside \((0,1)\) for every covariate pattern, including patterns far from the data. Many functions map the real line into the unit interval; the one used almost universally in regression is the logistic function.

The model is

\[P(Y_i = 1 \mid X_i) = \frac{\exp(\eta_i)}{1 + \exp(\eta_i)}, \qquad \eta_i = \alpha + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \dots + \beta_K x_{iK},\]

where \(\exp(a)\) denotes \(e^a\).1 Its inverse is the logit, which is the log of the odds:

\[\mathrm{logit}(\mu) = \log\!\left(\frac{\mu}{1-\mu}\right) = \log(\mu) - \log(1 - \mu) = \eta.\]

Describing the model as logistic-transformed or as logit-transformed makes no difference; they are the same model written from the two ends. On the logit scale the predictors combine additively, as in Chapter 22; on the probability scale the prediction is the S-shaped curve above. The odds \(\mu/(1-\mu)\) run from 0 to infinity as the probability runs from 0 to 1, and their logarithm runs over the whole real line, symmetrically about \(\mu = 0.5\).

A difference of two logits is the log of an odds ratio,

\[\log(\mathrm{OR}) = \mathrm{logit}(\mu_1) - \mathrm{logit}(\mu_0),\]

so a logistic coefficient exponentiates to an odds ratio. For a binary treatment in a two-by-two table with \(a\) treated cases, \(b\) treated non-cases, \(c\) untreated cases and \(d\) untreated non-cases, this is the familiar \(\mathrm{OR} = (a/b)/(c/d)\); a logistic regression generalises it to continuous predictors and to adjustment for covariates. Since the model asserts that logits add, \(\exp(\beta_1)\) is the odds ratio for a one-unit change in \(x_1\) at fixed values of every other predictor — a conditional odds ratio.

Fit it to the cohort with the adjustment set the DAG licenses. The priors are now on the logit scale. The intercept is the log-odds of the baseline risk: a 5% risk has log-odds -2.94, and \(normal(-3, 1)\) spans baseline risks from about 0.7% to 27%. A coefficient is a log odds ratio per unit of its predictor, and \(normal(0, 0.5)\) puts 95% of its mass on odds ratios between 0.37 and 2.7.

pri_link <- c(prior(normal(-3, 1),  class = "Intercept"),
              prior(normal(0, 0.5), class = "b"))

m_logit <- brm(stroke ~ drug + severity + age_c + diabetes,
               family = bernoulli(), data = df, prior = pri_link,
               chains = 4, iter = 800, warmup = 400, seed = 1,
               file = "models/ch24_logit", file_refit = "on_change")

The prior predictive check of Chapter 23 is run before reading the fit: the same model with the likelihood switched off gives the stroke risks the priors alone imply.

m_logit_prior <- brm(stroke ~ drug + severity + age_c + diabetes,
                     family = bernoulli(), data = df, prior = pri_link,
                     sample_prior = "only",
                     chains = 4, iter = 800, warmup = 400, seed = 1,
                     file = "models/ch24_logit_prior", file_refit = "on_change")

Under the priors alone, the risk of an untreated reference patient has a median of 4.6% and a 95% range from 0.6% to 29.6%, and across the cohort’s covariate patterns 1.6% of prior-simulated risks exceed one half. The priors say that stroke is uncommon but not how uncommon, and they rule out nothing the data could plausibly show, which is what a weakly informative prior on this scale should do.

The fit is standardised at once, on the scales the question is asked in: predict every patient’s risk with drug set to 1 and to 0, average each set of predictions over the cohort, and contrast the two averages.

The standardised risk difference is -0.0253 with a credible interval of [-0.0305, -0.0204], and the standardised risk ratio is 0.620 [0.568, 0.675], against the answer key’s -0.0272 and 0.600. Nothing about the logit link stood in the way of a risk ratio. The link decided how the model’s predictions were formed; the standardisation decided what was asked of them. The coefficient for drug, which exponentiates to 0.581, has not been looked at, and what it is and is not has a section of its own below.

25.2.3 The log link

The third link is the logarithm,

\[\log \mu_i = \eta_i, \qquad \mu_i = \exp(\eta_i),\]

under which the predictors combine multiplicatively on the risk itself: a one-unit change in \(x_1\) multiplies every patient’s risk by \(\exp(\beta_1)\) whatever their other covariates, so the model asserts a constant risk ratio across covariate patterns, where the logit asserts a constant odds ratio and the identity a constant risk difference. That is the sense in which the link is a scientific decision. It is a working assumption about how effects combine, to be judged like any other functional-form assumption, and the mechanism that generates the cohort’s stroke risk happens to be multiplicative in risk, which is why the log link has been the book’s outcome model for this cohort since the callout of Chapter 13, and why Chapter 16, Chapter 20 and Chapter 29 fit it. It is not the route to the risk ratio; the logistic fit above delivered one. The obvious implementation, a Bernoulli likelihood with a log link, is badly behaved: it requires \(\exp(\eta_i) \le 1\) for every row, and enforcing that constraint makes the posterior geometry pathological — divergent transitions, failed initialisation — in a way no choice of prior repairs. The same difficulty appears in maximum-likelihood log-binomial regression as a failure to converge.2

The standard evasion is to fit a Poisson likelihood with a log link to the 0/1 outcome. The Poisson has no ceiling at 1, so it samples without difficulty, and its log-link coefficient is a log risk ratio. Because the Poisson variance assumption is wrong for a binary outcome — it assumes variance equal to the mean, where the truth is \(\mu(1-\mu)\), which is smaller — the intervals come out conservative rather than anti-conservative. In the frequentist literature this is the modified Poisson estimator, ordinarily paired with a robust variance estimator to correct the interval width.3 Its Bayesian counterpart is simply family = poisson() on the 0/1 outcome; the model is a working model for the mean and is treated as such.

The same prior set serves, and not by accident. For a rare outcome the log of the risk and its logit nearly coincide (-3.00 against -2.94 at 5%), so the intercept prior asserts the same baseline risks as before, and a coefficient of \(\pm 1\) is now a risk ratio of 0.37 or 2.7, the range the logit prior allowed for the odds ratio.

m_pois <- brm(stroke ~ drug + severity + age_c + diabetes,
              family = poisson(), data = df, prior = pri_link,
              chains = 4, iter = 800, warmup = 400, seed = 1,
              file = "models/ch24_pois", file_refit = "on_change")

Standardised, the log-link fit gives a risk difference of -0.0257 and a risk ratio of 0.618, close to the answers the logistic fit gave; the next subsection lays the fits side by side. Here the drug coefficient, which exponentiates to 0.618, agrees with the standardised risk ratio to the second decimal, and this is what collapsibility of the risk ratio means: with a log link and no interaction terms the model asserts a constant risk ratio across covariate patterns, and the average of constant ratios is that ratio. The two part company only when the model contains an interaction, in which case the constant-ratio assertion has been dropped deliberately.

The poisson() fit is also the model used for the cohort in Chapter 16, which is why the mediation results there are on the risk-ratio scale.

25.2.4 The same report from every link

Because effects are obtained as contrasts of predictions rather than read off coefficients, the three fits can be laid side by side on the scales the question is asked in. Figure 25.3 does so, in the form the book uses for a reported effect: the standardised risk difference and risk ratio as posterior means with their credible intervals, against the answer key where one exists.

One fitted model can also report all three measures, each with its posterior interval: the scale of the report and the scale of the link have come apart (Table 25.2).

scale_row <- function(label, cmp, back = identity) {
  a <- avg_comparisons(m_pois, variables = "drug", comparison = cmp)
  tibble(measure = label, estimate = back(a$estimate),
         lower = back(a$conf.low), upper = back(a$conf.high))
}
bind_rows(
  scale_row("risk difference", "difference"),
  scale_row("risk ratio",      "ratioavg"),
  scale_row("odds ratio",      "lnoravg", exp)
) |>
  mutate(truth = c(truth$ATE_RD, truth$ATE_RR, truth$ATE_OR)) |>
  kbl(digits = 4) |>
  kable_styling(full_width = FALSE)

Note that the ratio measures use the ratioavg and lnoravg comparisons, which form the ratio of the averaged risks. The average of the individual ratios is a different number and is not the marginal risk ratio.4

Which should be reported? The measures answer different questions.

The risk difference is what a decision needs, because it is the only one that carries information about how much disease there is to prevent. Its reciprocal is the number needed to treat: here 39 patients treated to avert one stroke, with the posterior interval 31 to 51.5 A risk ratio of 0.62 is the same whether it halves a risk of 40% or a risk of 0.004%, and only in the first case is it worth acting on.

The risk ratio is the measure that transports best between populations with different baseline risks, and is the usual choice when an effect estimated in one population is to be applied to another. It is also the scale on which biological mechanisms most often combine as constant factors, which is a reason to fit on the log scale and is taken up below, where the link is chosen.

The odds ratio is what a logistic coefficient exponentiates to and what much of the literature reports, and it is best treated as an intermediate quantity to be converted before reporting: read as though it were a risk ratio it overstates the effect, increasingly so as the outcome becomes common (Chapter 11). The one design in which it is the natural sample quantity is the case-control study, and even there it is a way-station rather than the destination, for reasons given when the link is chosen.

25.3 What the coefficient is, and why it is not the report

The three fits agreed on the report. Their coefficients for drug do not agree with one another, and none of them is the report.

rbind(`identity link`      = fixef(m_lpm)["drug", c("Estimate", "Q2.5", "Q97.5")],
      `logit link, exp()`  = exp(fixef(m_logit)["drug", c("Estimate", "Q2.5", "Q97.5")]),
      `log link, exp()`    = exp(fixef(m_pois)["drug", c("Estimate", "Q2.5", "Q97.5")])) |>
  round(4)
                  Estimate    Q2.5   Q97.5
identity link      -0.0251 -0.0295 -0.0208
logit link, exp()   0.5814  0.5232  0.6418
log link, exp()     0.6175  0.5611  0.6851

The identity-link coefficient is the risk difference, for the reason given when it was fitted. The exponentiated log-link coefficient, 0.618, is a risk ratio and nearly the marginal one. The exponentiated logistic coefficient, 0.581, is an odds ratio, and it is an odds ratio at fixed values of every other predictor, a conditional odds ratio, where the answer key’s 0.583 is the marginal odds ratio of the whole cohort treated against the whole cohort untreated. The two are close, yet they are not estimates of the same thing, and the reason is the first of two facts about the odds ratio that this section establishes. The rows for the other covariates, which fixef() prints alongside, are not shown: they are adjusted associations and not the effect of anything, which is the Table 2 fallacy of Chapter 22.

25.3.1 The odds ratio is not collapsible

Consider a quantity that ought to be a property of the treatment alone: how much the drug changes the odds of stroke. Add to a correctly specified model a covariate that predicts the outcome but has no relation to the treatment whatsoever — under randomisation, every covariate is of this kind. Adding it cannot remove confounding, because there is none. It should therefore not change the estimated effect.

For the risk difference and the risk ratio it does not. For the odds ratio it does. To see this without any confounding at all, use the randomised variant of the cohort, in which treatment is assigned by a coin toss and severity, diabetes and prescribing preference play no part in it. (The simulations of this chapter are fitted by glm maximum likelihood rather than by brms: they are studies of how estimators behave, in which the point estimate is the object, run at sample sizes and in numbers that a sampler would make impractical, and nothing is inferred from any one of them.)

params_rct <- modifyList(default_params(),
                         list(d0 = 0, d_sev = 0, d_dm = 0, d_pref = 0))

collapse_grid <- function(n) {
  d <- simulate_cohort(n = n, seed = 2024, params = params_rct)
  d$age_c <- (d$age - 62) / 10
  fams <- list(identity = gaussian(), log = poisson(), logit = binomial())
  out <- lapply(names(fams), function(nm) {
    fit_c <- glm(stroke ~ drug, family = fams[[nm]], data = d)
    fit_a <- glm(stroke ~ drug + severity + age_c + diabetes,
                 family = fams[[nm]], data = d)
    b_c <- unname(coef(fit_c)["drug"])
    b_a <- unname(coef(fit_a)["drug"])
    s_c <- summary(fit_c)$coefficients["drug", "Std. Error"]
    tibble(n = n, link = nm, crude = b_c, adjusted = b_a,
           gap = b_a - b_c, se = s_c, `gap/se` = abs(b_a - b_c) / s_c)
  })
  bind_rows(out)
}
grid <- bind_rows(collapse_grid(50000), collapse_grid(400000))

In Table 25.3 the two coefficients agree under the identity link to four decimal places. Under the log link they agree to within a fraction of a standard error. Under the logit link they do not: adjusting for outcome predictors that are unrelated to treatment moves the log odds ratio away from zero, and therefore the odds ratio away from 1. At n = 50,000 the gap is 0.0545, about 1.4 standard errors; at n = 400,000 the gap is of similar size but the standard error is a third as large, so it is now 2.8 standard errors. It is a systematic difference, not a fluctuation.

This is non-collapsibility.6 A measure of effect is collapsible if the population-average (marginal) value is a weighted average of the stratum-specific values. The risk difference and the risk ratio are; the odds ratio is not. Averaging risks across strata and then forming the odds ratio of the averages gives a number closer to 1 than the common within-stratum odds ratio, because the map from risk to odds is convex. Conditioning on a strong outcome predictor therefore reports a different estimand, not a less biased version of the same one.

Four consequences follow, and they are practical.

  • A conditional odds ratio and a marginal odds ratio are different quantities. The logistic coefficient is the former. If the question is what would happen if the whole population were treated, the latter is wanted, and it must be obtained by standardisation.
  • Two analyses of the same randomised trial, both correct, will report different odds ratios if they adjust for different covariates. Neither is biased.
  • Odds ratios are not comparable across studies that adjusted for different things, which makes a meta-analysis of adjusted odds ratios an average over a set of distinct estimands.
  • Attenuation of logistic coefficients when covariates are omitted — a much-discussed problem in the social sciences, where it is framed as an unobserved-heterogeneity rescaling of the latent variable — is the same phenomenon seen from the other side.7 The recommended remedy in that literature is now the one this book uses throughout: report average marginal effects or predicted probabilities rather than coefficients.8

That remedy works here. Standardising the logistic fit — predicting each patient’s risk under drug = 1 and under drug = 0, averaging, and contrasting — recovers marginal quantities on whatever scale is asked for (Table 25.4).

The marginal odds ratio standardised from the fit is 0.604, nearer the true 0.583 than the conditional coefficient 0.581; the residual discrepancy is model misspecification, since the cohort’s true outcome mechanism is multiplicative in risk rather than in odds and involves a drug-by-blood-pressure interaction the model does not contain.

There is a dissenting position worth stating rather than suppressing. Some authors argue that the odds ratio should be preferred precisely because it is portable: an odds ratio transfers across populations with different baseline risks in a way a risk ratio cannot, since a risk ratio far from 1 is arithmetically impossible at high baseline risk. On that view non-collapsibility is a mathematical property to be understood rather than a defect, and the answer is to convert the odds ratio into a risk for reporting.9 The conversion step is the part both camps agree on, and it is what standardisation does. The disagreement is about which parameter to carry between studies, not about what to report to a reader.

25.3.2 Unmeasured causes of the outcome

The previous section added a measured covariate to the model. The situation that matters more in a causal analysis is the one in which a cause of the outcome is never measured at all. Let \(U\) be such a cause: it affects \(Y\), and it is independent of treatment, so it opens no back-door path and is not a confounder. The DAG says no adjustment for \(U\) is required, and none is possible. The question is whether the logistic model is nevertheless damaged by the \(U \rightarrow Y\) path in a way the identity-link model is not.

It is, and the size of the effect can be read off a simulation in which treatment is randomised and the true conditional log odds ratio is held fixed at 0.8 while the strength \(\gamma_U\) of the \(U \rightarrow Y\) path is varied.

n_uy <- 2e5

atten <- purrr::map_dfr(c(0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3), function(gU) {
  set.seed(100 + 10 * gU)
  X <- rbinom(n_uy, 1, 0.5)               # randomised treatment
  U <- rnorm(n_uy)                        # unmeasured cause of Y only
  Y <- rbinom(n_uy, 1, plogis(-1 + 0.8 * X + gU * U))
  d <- data.frame(X, U, Y)
  e0 <- -1 + gU * U                       # linear predictor under no treatment
  r0 <- mean(plogis(e0)); r1 <- mean(plogis(e0 + 0.8))
  tibble(`$\\gamma_U$`            = gU,
         `logit, $U$ omitted`     = coef(glm(Y ~ X, family = binomial, data = d))["X"],
         `logit, $U$ included`    = coef(glm(Y ~ X + U, family = binomial, data = d))["X"],
         `true marginal log OR`   = log((r1 / (1 - r1)) / (r0 / (1 - r0))),
         `identity, $U$ omitted`  = coef(lm(Y ~ X, data = d))["X"],
         `true marginal RD`       = r1 - r0)
})

Three readings of Table 25.5. The logistic coefficient falls from 0.793 to 0.376, an attenuation of 53%, driven by a path that touches treatment nowhere. Including \(U\) recovers 0.8 in every row. And the identity-link coefficient agrees with the true marginal risk difference to three decimal places at every value of \(\gamma_U\).

Whether this is bias depends on what the coefficient is taken to estimate, and the two readings need to be kept apart.

  • Read as the conditional log odds ratio — the effect of treatment on an individual, or within a stratum homogeneous in \(U\) — the coefficient is biased, by a factor that cannot be estimated from data lacking \(U\). This is the unobserved-heterogeneity rescaling of the previous section, now with no measured covariate available to remove it.
  • Read as the marginal log odds ratio, it is not biased: the fitted value and the true marginal value agree in every row. It is a consistent estimate of a quantity that is a property of the population as much as of the treatment.

The size of the rescaling depends on the link as well as on \(\gamma_U\). For the logit it has no closed form, because a mixture of logistic curves over a normal \(U\) is not itself logistic, though the discrepancy is undetectable at the sample sizes used here.

Two practical consequences follow that the measured-covariate case does not make obvious.

  • The change-in-estimate criterion for confounder selection is not valid under a logit link. The gap between a crude and an adjusted odds ratio is the sum of a confounding component and a non-collapsibility component, and the two can point in opposite directions, so the size of the shift carries no clean information about confounding. The decomposition is given analytically by Pang, Kaufman and Platt.10 Under an identity link the same shift is a confounding signal, because there is no second component.
  • Adjustment in a nonlinear model therefore changes which effect is being estimated and not only how precisely it is estimated, which is the sense in which omitting prognostic covariates from a trial analysis has been described as changing the treatment effect under study.11

The table also bears on the portability argument recorded at the end of the previous section. Here the conditional odds ratio is the stable parameter and the marginal measures move with \(\gamma_U\) — but that is true by construction, because the simulation generates the data on the logit scale. Had the mechanism been additive in risk, the conditional risk difference would have been the stable parameter instead. The simulation shows that marginal measures depend on the amount of unmeasured outcome variation in the population; it does not adjudicate which conditional measure is the portable one.

25.3.3 Standardisation is not damaged by the same paths

If omitting \(U\) attenuates the coefficient, it might be expected to corrupt the standardised marginal contrast as well, since a logistic model with \(U\) omitted is not itself a logistic model and g-computation from it inherits that misspecification. It does not, in any of four scenarios: a normal \(U\); a bimodal \(U\), representing an unmeasured high-risk subgroup; a \(U\) that modifies the effect of treatment on the logit scale; and a high baseline risk. Because the residual errors are small, each scenario is run ten times so that they can be compared with Monte Carlo variation rather than eyeballed.

Every mean error in Table 25.6 is smaller than the Monte Carlo standard deviation of a single replicate, and — the more informative comparison — the errors are almost identical across the two links within each scenario. Whatever residual discrepancy there is, it is not attributable to the link.

The conclusion is narrower than “the logistic model is biased by unmeasured causes of the outcome”, and it is more useful. The \(U \rightarrow Y\) path damages the coefficient, severely, and damages it in a way that has no counterpart under an identity link. It does not measurably damage the standardised marginal contrast obtained from the same fit. The threat is to reading coefficients, which is the practice this book avoids throughout, and not to the model’s capacity to deliver a causal estimand.

25.4 Choosing the link

Once the report is a standardised contrast, the link is no longer a decision about what to report. It remains a decision, and a scientific one, about three things.

The first is how effects are assumed to combine. A model without interaction terms asserts, under the identity link, that the drug removes the same number of percentage points of risk from every patient; under the log link, that it multiplies every patient’s risk by the same factor; under the logit, that it multiplies every patient’s odds by the same factor. These are different claims about the world, and they are functional-form assumptions like any other, judged by fit, by posterior predictive checks (Chapter 23) and by what is known of the mechanism, within limits set out at the end of this section. The mechanism that generates the cohort’s stroke risk is multiplicative in risk, which is why the log link is the specification that matches it and has been the book’s outcome model for this cohort since the callout of Chapter 13. The logistic model fitted to the same cohort is the misspecified one; its standardised contrasts absorbed the misspecification (Table 25.4), which is one more reason to report those and not the coefficient. Where the mechanism is unknown, a product term between treatment and the strongest baseline risk factor, fitted on any link, lets the data say how the effect varies, and Chapter 17 is the chapter on reading the answer.

The logit’s claim is the one readers most often say they cannot picture, and it has a picture the book has already drawn. In the odds form of Bayes’ theorem (Chapter 9) the posterior log-odds of an outcome are the prior log-odds plus the weight of evidence, the log likelihood ratio, and the weights of independent pieces of evidence add. If the covariates were conditionally independent given the outcome, the log-odds of stroke would be a baseline plus the sum of each covariate’s weight of evidence; and where a covariate’s log likelihood ratio is linear in the covariate, as it is for a normally distributed covariate with the same spread among those who will and will not have the event, that sum is a logistic regression, with each coefficient a weight of evidence per unit. Logistic regression is that model with the independence assumption relaxed: the coefficients are fitted jointly, so each is the evidence a covariate adds given the others. Linear on the logit scale therefore means evidence accumulates additively, a model of how an outcome is predicted or diagnosed from what is known about a patient, which is why the logit is the natural scale for the predictive models of Chapter 27. Linear on the log scale means causes multiply risk, a model of how an intervention acts. For the outcome model of a g-computation, which has only to predict each patient’s risk well under both treatments, either reading serves, and a reader need believe neither to use the fit.

The second is the boundary. The identity link’s predictions leave \([0, 1]\) at the covariate extremes, as the linear probability model showed. The log link’s leave it from above when the outcome is common: a risk ratio of 2 cannot apply to a baseline risk above one half, and a Poisson working model asked to fit such data puts fitted risks above 1 on some rows. Only the logit respects both boundaries for every covariate pattern, which is what makes it the link for a common outcome and for prediction (Chapter 27), where the individual fitted probabilities are the product.

The third is the design, and where it applies it is decisive.

25.4.1 Outcome-dependent sampling

In a case-control study every case is recruited and only a sample of the non-cases, so the proportion of cases in the data is fixed by the investigator and says nothing about the risk in the population. Under such outcome-dependent sampling only the intercept of a logistic model is altered; the slopes are unchanged.12 No other link has this property, and the Poisson working model in particular does not: the risk ratio in a sample where half the rows are cases is not the risk ratio in a population where five per cent are. The demonstration uses data generated on the logit scale, so that the logistic model is correctly specified and the comparison is clean.

set.seed(7)
n_lk <- 4e5
L_lk <- rnorm(n_lk, 0, 1.5); X_lk <- rbinom(n_lk, 1, 0.5)
Y_lk <- rbinom(n_lk, 1, plogis(-1.5 + 0.8 * X_lk + 1.2 * L_lk))
d_lk <- data.frame(X = X_lk, L = L_lk, Y = Y_lk)

f_pois  <- glm(Y ~ X + L, family = poisson,  data = d_lk)   # slopes are log risk ratios

Every case is kept and about one control per case is drawn, and because a case-control subsample is much smaller than the cohort the sampling is repeated 25 times, so that a shift can be told from subsample noise. Two things are fitted to each subsample: the two working models as they stand, and a logistic model in which each control is weighted by the reciprocal of its sampling fraction, which is known here because the investigator set it.

frac_ctrl <- 1 / 3                                   # sampling fraction of the controls
cc <- purrr::map_dfr(1:25, function(r) {
  set.seed(500 + r)
  keep <- d_lk$Y == 1 | (d_lk$Y == 0 & runif(n_lk) < frac_ctrl)
  dcc  <- d_lk[keep, ]
  w    <- ifelse(dcc$Y == 1, 1, 1 / frac_ctrl)         # known sampling weights
  gl <- glm(Y ~ X + L, family = binomial, data = dcc)
  gp <- glm(Y ~ X + L, family = poisson,  data = dcc)
  gw <- glm(Y ~ X + L, family = binomial, data = dcc, weights = w)
  d1 <- dcc; d1$X <- 1; d0 <- dcc; d0$X <- 0            # standardise the weighted fit
  r1 <- weighted.mean(predict(gw, newdata = d1, type = "response"), w)
  r0 <- weighted.mean(predict(gw, newdata = d0, type = "response"), w)
  tibble(logit_X = coef(gl)["X"], pois_X = coef(gp)["X"],
         RD_w = r1 - r0, RR_w = r1 / r0)
})

The log-link slope shrinks towards zero by 37%, which is 531 standard errors. A Poisson working model fitted to case-control data estimates neither the cohort’s risk ratio nor anything else interpretable, and the identity link fares no better. This is why logistic regression is the analysis of a case-control study, and why a conditional likelihood for matched designs exists only for the logit.

What the case-control odds ratio estimates, and how to get past it. The sentence “risks are not estimable from a case-control study, only the odds ratio is” is true of the sample taken by itself and false as a general rule, and the exceptions are the useful part. Which quantity the sample odds ratio estimates depends on how the controls were drawn.13 Controls sampled from the person-time at risk as the cases arise (density sampling) make it a rate ratio; controls sampled from everyone at risk at the start of follow-up (case-cohort sampling) make it a risk ratio; only controls sampled from the non-cases remaining at the end of follow-up make it an odds ratio, and no rare-disease assumption enters the first two.14 A survey of 150 published case-control studies found that most could have read their odds ratio as a rate ratio, and that almost none said what it estimated.15 Beyond the choice of controls, a little extra information turns the odds ratio back into risks. If the sampling fractions are known, as they are when the study is nested in a defined cohort or drawn from a registry, weighting each control by the reciprocal of its sampling fraction rebuilds the cohort’s risks, and the weighted logistic fit standardises to a risk difference and a risk ratio like any other: across the 25 samples above the weighted risk difference averages 0.1166 against the cohort’s 0.1156, and the weighted risk ratio 1.427 against 1.423. If instead the baseline risk \(p_0\) is known from outside the study, \(\mathrm{RR} = \mathrm{OR} / (1 - p_0 + p_0\,\mathrm{OR})\) converts the one into the other, and in the rare-outcome limit the two coincide. The odds ratio is the case-control study’s natural sample quantity, then, and the design fixes the link. It does not fix what is reported.

25.4.2 Can loo choose the link?

A natural proposal, and the one this book makes for the likelihood, is to let out-of-sample predictive accuracy decide: fit the candidate links, compare them with loo as Chapter 23 did, and keep the best. For the likelihood family the proposal is right. Whether counts are Poisson or negative binomial, and whether a continuous outcome has heavier tails than the normal allows, are questions about the distribution of the observed outcome, and leave-one-out prediction of the observed outcome is the right judge; the posterior predictive check usually shows the failure before loo scores it (exercise 5). For the link the proposal is legitimate but weaker than it looks, for two reasons that the book’s own conventions make specific.

The Poisson working model cannot enter the comparison. Its likelihood puts probability on counts of 2, 3 and more, values a 0/1 outcome never takes, so for the same fitted risks its pointwise log score is lower than a Bernoulli model’s by an amount that has nothing to do with the link. The comparison below uses 400 posterior draws per model, which is enough for a comparison of this kind and keeps the pointwise log-likelihood matrix small.

loo_compare puts the log-link model 129 units of expected log predictive density behind the logistic model, with a standard error of 6.8, a margin any rule of thumb would call decisive. Scored fairly, as the Bernoulli log score of each model’s fitted risk function, the log-link model is 2.4 units ahead, which is what a mechanism that is multiplicative in risk should produce. The whole of the loo_compare gap is the mechanical penalty: taking the logistic model’s own risks and scoring them with the Poisson log-likelihood instead of the Bernoulli one loses 130 units, the same amount. A Bernoulli likelihood with a log link would make the comparison fair, and it is the model that does not sample. The same output shows the boundary at work even at a 5% baseline risk: 0.01% of the log-link model’s fitted risks exceed 1. Comparing links with loo is therefore confined to the Bernoulli family, logit against probit against complementary log-log, and there the candidates are so close that the differences loo returns are small for a good reason and should not be read as verdicts.

loo scores the outcome that was observed, and the estimand needs the one that was not. Leave-one-out prediction asks how well a model predicts each patient’s outcome under the treatment they received. The standardised contrast also needs every treated patient’s risk under no treatment and every untreated patient’s risk under treatment, and where the treated and the untreated occupy different regions of covariate space those predictions are extrapolations that no held-out observation can score. Under good overlap the two tasks coincide and loo is a fair guide to the link. Under poor overlap they come apart, and a simulation shows how far. Treatment is strongly confounded by \(L\), so that the propensity runs from near 0 to near 1; the outcome depends on \(L\) either through a quadratic or linearly; four models are fitted to one half of the data and scored on the other half by the Bernoulli log score of their predicted risks, with identity-link predictions outside the unit interval pulled to its edge before scoring.

score_vs_estimand <- function(quad, seed, conf = 2.5, n = 2e5) {
  set.seed(seed)
  L  <- rnorm(n); X <- rbinom(n, 1, plogis(-0.2 + conf * L))   # conf = 2.5: poor overlap
  U  <- rnorm(n)                                               # unmeasured cause of Y only
  e0 <- -1 + 1.2 * L + quad * L^2 + 1.5 * U
  Y  <- rbinom(n, 1, plogis(e0 + 0.8 * X))
  d  <- data.frame(X, L, Y); rd_true <- mean(plogis(e0 + 0.8)) - mean(plogis(e0))
  tr <- d[seq_len(n / 2), ]; te <- d[-seq_len(n / 2), ]         # fit on one half, score on the other
  fits <- list(`logit, X + L`            = glm(Y ~ X + L,            binomial, tr),
               `logit, X * (L + L^2)`    = glm(Y ~ X * (L + I(L^2)), binomial, tr),
               `identity, X + L`         = glm(Y ~ X + L,            gaussian, tr),
               `identity, X * (L + L^2)` = glm(Y ~ X * (L + I(L^2)), gaussian, tr))
  pw <- sapply(fits, function(f) {                             # pointwise held-out log score
    p <- pmin(pmax(predict(f, te, type = "response"), 1e-3), 1 - 1e-3)
    dbinom(te$Y, 1, p, log = TRUE)
  })
  tot <- colSums(pw); best <- which.max(tot)
  tibble(scenario = if (quad > 0) "quadratic in L" else "linear in L",
         model = names(fits),
         `held-out log score` = tot,
         `difference to best` = tot - tot[best],
         `se of difference`   = apply(pw, 2, function(v) sd(v - pw[, best])) * sqrt(nrow(te)),
         `error in RD (%)`    = sapply(fits, function(f) 100 * (std_rd(f, d) - rd_true) / rd_true))
}
sve <- bind_rows(score_vs_estimand(quad = 0.9, seed = 31), score_vs_estimand(quad = 0, seed = 32))

In the upper block of Table 25.8 the score ranks the flexible logistic model first and the flexible identity model second, 453 units behind it. On the estimand the order is different: the flexible identity model is the worst of the four, off by 61%, while the flexible logistic model is off by 8% and the simple logistic model, which the score places third, by 50%. The score’s second favourite is the estimand’s last. In the lower block the truth is linear in \(L\), and here the ranking by score agrees with the ranking by estimand error, so loo would choose correctly. What it cannot convey is the stakes. The flexible identity model is 40 units behind the best score over 100,000 held-out observations, 0.0004 per observation and 3.3 standard errors of the difference, and it is off by 18% on the estimand where both logistic models are within 3.3%. In the upper block a score gap of 453 units went with a difference of 53 points in estimand error; here a gap about a tenth of that size goes with one of 17 points. The reason is the same in both blocks. The flexible identity model fits the observed risks well inside each treatment group’s own region of \(L\) and extrapolates a polynomial into the other group’s region, where no observation checks it and the estimand lives. A leave-one-out score orders the models by how well they predict the outcomes that were observed, and neither its order nor its margins say how far the counterfactual predictions are off, which is the failure that matters most for a causal contrast.

loo keeps its place, then, in this order. Use it on the likelihood family, where it answers the question asked. Use it on the covariate specification within the fixed adjustment set, after overlap has been checked (Chapter 11), because that is where the large errors live. Use it on the link within the Bernoulli family as a check that nothing is badly wrong, expecting small differences for a rare outcome, and let mechanism and design settle what remains. Never let it near the adjustment set: a mediator or a collider predicts the outcome well, and loo rewards including it. And where two links tie, the Bayesian alternative to choosing is not to choose: weight the models by loo_model_weights(), average their predictions with the weights (pp_average() does this), and standardise the averaged prediction, which carries the uncertainty about the link into the contrast instead of discarding it.16

That settles the recommendation, and it is the one the book has been following. Fit the cohort’s outcome models with the log link, because its mechanism combines multiplicatively in risk, so a constant risk ratio is the working assumption that matches it; this is the Poisson working model of the callout of Chapter 13. Use the logit where the boundary matters, for a common outcome and for prediction, for a case-control or matched design, and when a literature written on that scale has to be read or contributed to. Use the identity only as the linear probability model of a randomised comparison with few covariates. Whichever link is fitted, report the standardised contrast, and the choice has little consequence for it (Figure 25.3). Two further links, the probit and the complementary log-log, are reached for only when a latent normal quantity or a grouped hazard is part of the substantive claim.17

25.5 Counts and rates

The Poisson family earns its place in the framework on genuine counts, where the outcome is the number of events and the natural effect measure is a rate ratio. Two features of count data have no analogue in the models so far: the events accrue over an amount of exposure that differs between units, and the Poisson’s single-parameter variance is often too small for real data.

Take a small simulation with a known answer: an infection-control protocol is introduced on some wards, patients are followed for differing lengths of time, and the true rate ratio is 0.65.

What the question asks about is the number of events per unit of observation time, and that is expressed by an offset: a term in the linear predictor whose coefficient is fixed at 1, so that

\[\log \mathbb{E}[Y_i] = \log(t_i) + \alpha + \beta\,x_i \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \log\!\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}[Y_i]}{t_i}\right) = \alpha + \beta\,x_i,\]

which is to say that the model is a model of the rate \(\mathbb{E}[Y_i]/t_i\) while the likelihood remains a likelihood for the count.

      0.6500000       0.6391182       1.0281315       1.2109882       1.0970083

With the offset the estimate is 0.639 against a true 0.65. Without it the estimate is 1.028 — no effect at all, because it is a ratio of counts and the counts were accumulated over unequal periods. In brms the same term is written events ~ protocol + offset(log(pt)) in the formula. Unequal follow-up is the rule rather than the exception in cohort studies, and omitting the offset is a way of getting the estimand wrong that no amount of adjustment repairs.

The logarithm in that offset is forced rather than conventional. What an offset asserts is that expected events are proportional to exposure, which is a multiplicative statement, while the linear predictor is additive; the logarithm is the function that converts the one into the other. For a general link \(g\), writing \(g(\mathbb{E}[Y_i]) = g(t_i) + \eta_i\) gives \(\mathbb{E}[Y_i] = g^{-1}\{g(t_i) + \eta_i\}\), and only for \(g = \log\) is the result proportional to \(t_i\). An additive offset can express proportionality to exposure only under a log link.

Using \(t_i\) itself is therefore not a mild misspecification. It asserts \(\mathbb{E}[Y_i] = e^{t_i} e^{\eta_i}\), so that extending observation from two years to four multiplies the expected count by \(e^2 \approx 7.4\) rather than by 2.

The wrong offset returns 0.026 against the true 0.65 — worse than leaving the offset out altogether, because follow-up is longer in the protocol arm, so the error is confounded with the exposure.

Two variants are worth knowing. The first releases the coefficient on \(\log t\) rather than fixing it at 1, fitting \(\mathbb{E}[Y_i] \propto t_i^{\gamma}\); the offset is the restriction \(\gamma = 1\), and estimating \(\gamma\) instead is a check on it. Here \(\hat\gamma\) = 0.997 with a 95% interval of [0.954, 1.041], as it should be, since the simulation generated counts proportional to time. Read a departure from 1 with care: with recurrent events and unmeasured heterogeneity, \(\hat\gamma\) below 1 is produced by frailty and by depletion of susceptibles rather than by a genuinely non-constant rate. Where the rate does vary over follow-up, the standard remedy is not a different offset but finer rows — split each patient’s time into intervals, each with its own \(\log t\) offset and its own baseline, which is Poisson regression used as a piecewise-exponential survival model (Chapter 26).

The second variant makes the link the explicit choice. Drop the offset and let exposure multiply the whole linear predictor, \(\mathbb{E}[Y_i] = t_i(\alpha + \beta x_i)\): this is an identity-link model, and its coefficient is a rate difference of -0.437 events per person-year against a true \(1.2 \times 0.65 - 1.2 = -0.42\), rather than a rate ratio. Nothing about counts requires the multiplicative scale. The form of the offset and the effect measure the model reports are one decision and not two, which is the subject of the next section. One practical restriction: \(\log 0\) is undefined, so rows with zero exposure have to be dropped, and they carry no information anyway because their expected count is zero whatever the rate.

The second feature is over-dispersion. The Poisson distribution has one parameter, and its variance is forced equal to its mean; if unmeasured heterogeneity between units inflates the variance, the point estimate is still reasonable but the intervals are too narrow. We repeat the simulation with ward-level frailty.

set.seed(12)

The two point estimates in Table 25.9 are almost identical; the standard errors differ by a factor of 2.3. Over-dispersion is a statement about precision, not about bias, and the remedy is a likelihood with a second parameter: the negative binomial, which is a gamma mixture of Poissons (Chapter 7) and in brms is family = negbinomial(). A posterior predictive check of the count distribution is the diagnostic that finds this, which is the general instruction of Chapter 23 applied to a family whose variance is not free.

This section has treated follow-up time as a single number per patient. Rates that change over follow-up, the baseline hazard as a smooth, and the expansion of a cohort into person-time rows are the subject of Chapter 26, which begins where the offset leaves off.

25.6 Fitting two links is a weak check on bias

If the logistic coefficient is distorted by non-collapsibility and the identity-link coefficient is not, a natural proposal follows: fit both and treat their disagreement as a warning that the logistic estimate is biased. The proposal does not work, for two separate reasons.

The first is that an odds ratio and a risk difference differ even when both models are correct, as Table 25.4 shows. A comparison of the two coefficients fires whether or not anything is wrong, so it carries no information. The comparison has to be made between the two models’ standardised marginal contrasts, which are estimates of the same quantity.

The second reason survives that repair. The following simulation confounds treatment by a covariate \(L\) whose effect on the outcome is partly quadratic, and fits models that omit the quadratic term. Confounding strength is varied, which also varies the degree of overlap between the treated and untreated covariate distributions.

Show code 
n_sp <- 5e5

spread_row <- function(conf, quad, label, seed) {
  set.seed(seed)
  L  <- rnorm(n_sp)
  ps <- plogis(-0.2 + conf * L)              # propensity: conf sets the confounding
  X  <- rbinom(n_sp, 1, ps)
  U  <- rnorm(n_sp)                          # unmeasured cause of Y only
  e0 <- -1 + 1.2 * L + quad * L^2 + 1.5 * U
  Y  <- rbinom(n_sp, 1, plogis(e0 + 0.8 * X))
  d  <- data.frame(X, L, Y)
  truth <- mean(plogis(e0 + 0.8)) - mean(plogis(e0))

  mis <- c(logit    = std_rd(glm(Y ~ X + L, family = binomial, data = d), d),
           identity = std_rd(glm(Y ~ X + L, family = gaussian, data = d), d))
  cor <- c(logit    = std_rd(glm(Y ~ X * (L + I(L^2)), family = binomial, data = d), d),
           identity = std_rd(glm(Y ~ X * (L + I(L^2)), family = gaussian, data = d), d))

  tibble(scenario = label,
         `propensity, 1st-99th pctile` =
           paste0(fmt(quantile(ps, .01), 3), "-", fmt(quantile(ps, .99), 3)),
         `logit (%)`    = 100 * (mis["logit"] - truth) / truth,
         `identity (%)` = 100 * (mis["identity"] - truth) / truth,
         `spread across links (%)` = 100 * diff(range(mis)) / truth,
         `logit, correct form (%)`    = 100 * (cor["logit"] - truth) / truth,
         `identity, correct form (%)` = 100 * (cor["identity"] - truth) / truth)
}

spread <- bind_rows(
  spread_row(0.0, 0.9, "no confounding, omitted $L^2$", 11),
  spread_row(1.0, 0.9, "moderate confounding, omitted $L^2$", 12),
  spread_row(2.5, 0.9, "strong confounding, omitted $L^2$", 13),
  spread_row(1.0, 0.0, "moderate confounding, correct form", 14),
  spread_row(2.5, 0.0, "strong confounding, correct form", 15)
)

Read the middle rows of Table 25.10 first. With moderate confounding and the quadratic term omitted, the two links are biased by 11% and 14%, while the spread between them is 3.2%. With strong confounding the biases are 47% to 57% and the spread is still only 10%. The reason is structural: functional-form bias is largely common to the two links, so differencing them removes the very thing the check was meant to detect. The disagreement understates the bias by a factor of three to five.

The last two rows fail in the opposite direction. With the functional form correct, the logistic model is accurate to 2.2% under strong confounding while the identity model is off by 22%, and the spread is 24%. A large disagreement here would be read as evidence against the logistic model, and the logistic model is the one that is right. The identity link fails because a linear risk model extrapolates without bound into the covariate regions where one treatment group is nearly absent.

The spread between links is therefore neither an upper nor a lower bound on the bias, and it is not monotone in it. As a diagnostic it has no calibration.

The final two columns of Table 25.10 carry a warning about the remedy as well. Restoring the quadratic term and its interactions repairs the logistic model, but it makes the identity-link model far worse — an error of -72% under strong confounding. Added flexibility helps only where there are data to constrain it; where overlap fails, it amplifies extrapolation instead. Flexibility is not a substitute for positivity (Chapter 11).

There is one circumstance in which the original instinct is sound. When the true mechanism is additive in risk and the risks are high, the logistic model is the misspecified one and the identity model recovers the marginal risk difference.

set.seed(21)
n_ad <- 4e5
L_ad <- rnorm(n_ad)
X_ad <- rbinom(n_ad, 1, plogis(-0.2 + 2.0 * L_ad))
p0   <- pmin(pmax(0.55 + 0.16 * L_ad + runif(n_ad, -0.15, 0.15), 0.01), 0.99)
p1   <- pmin(p0 + 0.12, 0.995)                       # additive in risk, by construction
d_ad <- data.frame(X = X_ad, L = L_ad,
                   Y = rbinom(n_ad, 1, ifelse(X_ad == 1, p1, p0)))

c(truth    = mean(p1) - mean(p0),
  logit    = std_rd(glm(Y ~ X + L, family = binomial, data = d_ad), d_ad),
  identity = std_rd(glm(Y ~ X + L, family = gaussian, data = d_ad), d_ad))
    truth     logit  identity 
0.1178073 0.1107498 0.1224254

The identity link wins here, and it wins because the link matches the mechanism, not because it is immune to non-collapsibility. Nothing in the output identifies which model is the matched one; that has to come from knowing something about the mechanism, or from a diagnostic that examines fit rather than comparing estimates.

What to do instead, in order of expected return:

  1. Fix the estimand before the model — a marginal risk difference or risk ratio in a named population, with the adjustment set taken from the DAG. Once the reported quantity is a standardised contrast, the link becomes a device for fitting a conditional mean and non-collapsibility ceases to be a live problem.
  2. Spend the effort on the covariate specification, not on the link. In the simulation above, correcting the functional form of \(L\) moved the answer from 47% to 1.9%; changing the link moved it by 10%.
  3. Check overlap before adding flexibility, for the reason in the final two columns of the table.
  4. If the estimate matters, use an estimator that does not depend on one outcome model being correct — the doubly robust and targeted maximum likelihood estimators, which combine an outcome model with a treatment model and are consistent if either is right (Chapter 29).18
  5. Report a link comparison, if at all, as a note on specification, and only when it was planned in advance. Chosen after the fact, the link is an unusually clean forking path (Appendix G). If links or families are compared on fit, the rule of Chapter 23 applies unchanged: loo ranks functional forms within a fixed adjustment set and cannot audit the covariates, and it scores only the outcomes that were observed, as the previous section showed.

One conflation to guard against, because it is the likeliest thing a reader will take from a recommendation to fit two models. Agreement between links is not evidence against unmeasured confounding. Every model in this section shares the same adjustment set and therefore the same identification assumptions; all of them would be wrong together if a back-door path were open. Link robustness is a statement about functional form, and functional form is the smaller of the two problems.

25.7 What the multiplicative scale hides

Whether an effect “varies” between subgroups depends on the scale it is measured on, as Chapter 17 established. A generalised linear model with a non-identity link puts that fact inside the model, because the product term it estimates is a product term on the link scale only. Fit an interaction with diabetes on the log scale.

m_int <- brm(stroke ~ drug * diabetes + severity + age_c,

  kbl(digits = 4) |>
  kable_styling(full_width = FALSE)

The product-term coefficient is 0.041 with a credible interval of [-0.132, 0.213], which contains zero comfortably: on the model’s own multiplicative scale there is no appreciable interaction. The same fitted model, standardised within strata (Table 25.11), gives risk differences of -0.0181 and -0.0491 — the drug averts about 2.7 times as many strokes per treated patient among the diabetic. On the additive scale the interaction is large.

Nothing is inconsistent here. A constant risk ratio applied to a higher baseline risk removes a larger absolute amount, so a model that fits a constant ratio implies an additive interaction wherever a second factor moves the baseline. The consequence for practice is that a logistic or Poisson model reporting a non-significant product term has established nothing about the scale on which the decision will be taken, and the recommendation in the methodological literature is to report both scales with the stratum-specific effects alongside.19 The standardisation above is how the second scale is obtained from a model fitted on the first.

25.8 Outcomes that are proportions

An outcome that is already a proportion — the fraction of a ward’s beds occupied, a score expressed on a 0-to-1 scale, the share of visits at which a treatment was delivered — is bounded like a probability but is not a count of successes out of a known number of trials. If the denominator is known, the outcome is a binomial count and belongs in a binomial() model with trials(); the cases below are those where it is not.

Linear model. As with the linear probability model, an identity link gives coefficients in the units of the proportion and predictions that can leave \([0,1]\). Adequate when the observed proportions occupy the middle of the range, say 0.2 to 0.8, and the target is a population average.

Fractional logistic regression. A logistic model fitted by quasi-likelihood to a non-integer outcome, glm(y ~ x, family = quasibinomial(link = "logit")). It estimates the mean proportion consistently without asserting a full likelihood, at the cost of not being a generative model — which in a Bayesian workflow means there is nothing to put a prior on and nothing to check with a posterior predictive draw.

Beta regression. The beta distribution is the natural likelihood on the open interval \((0,1)\), and beta regression models its mean and precision, optionally as functions of predictors.20 In brms, brm(bf(y ~ x, phi ~ x), family = Beta()). Modelling \(\phi\) as well as \(\mu\) is distributional regression: it lets the spread of the proportion depend on the predictors, which is often the substantive point. The restriction is that the beta density is zero at 0 and 1, so a data set containing exact zeroes or ones cannot be fitted.

Zero- and one-inflated beta regression. Where exact boundary values occur, they are usually generated by a different process from the interior values — a ward with no occupied beds is not the low tail of a continuum. The remedy is a mixture: a logistic sub-model for whether the observation is at a boundary, and a beta sub-model for the interior. With zeroes only, family = zero_inflated_beta() adds one parameter, zi. With both zeroes and ones, family = zero_one_inflated_beta() adds two: zoi, the probability that an observation is at either boundary, and coi, the probability that a boundary observation is a one rather than a zero. Each may have its own linear predictor.21

brm(
  bf(outcome ~ covariates,   # mu:  the mean of the interior values
     phi     ~ covariates,   # phi: their precision
     zoi     ~ covariates,   # zero-or-one inflation
     coi     ~ covariates),  # of the inflated values, the share that are ones
  data = d, family = zero_one_inflated_beta()
)

With ones but no zeroes, either model \(1 - y\) with zero_inflated_beta(), or fix coi at 1 in the zero-one-inflated model.

25.9 Discrimination is not identification

The remaining classical apparatus of logistic regression — the ROC curve, the area under it, the confusion matrix — measures something the rest of this chapter has not been about. It measures how well the fitted probabilities rank patients, which is a property of predictions and is silent on whether any coefficient is a causal effect. This section keeps the apparatus and puts it where it belongs; Chapter 27 takes up prediction as a subject in its own right.

The example is real data: hip-fracture patients in Estonia, and whether they received post-acute physical therapy.

A ROC curve plots, for every possible classification cut-off, the proportion of true cases correctly called cases (sensitivity, the true-positive rate) against the proportion of true non-cases incorrectly called cases (one minus specificity). Fitted probabilities take any value in \((0,1)\); classifying requires choosing a threshold, and the curve traces what every threshold would give. The area under the curve summarises the whole curve into one number: the probability that a randomly chosen case is assigned a higher predicted risk than a randomly chosen non-case. It ranges from 0.5, for a model that ranks no better than a coin, to 1.

p1 <- fitted(m_HF_binom1)[, "Estimate"]
p2 <- fitted(m_HF_binom2)[, "Estimate"]

The age-only model has an AUC of 0.530. Its age effect was clearly determined and it discriminates barely better than chance. This is not a contradiction: a predictor can shift everyone’s risk by a well-established amount and still leave the risk distributions of cases and non-cases almost entirely overlapping. It is the reason the AUC is a poor criterion for deciding whether a risk factor belongs in a model — established risk factors routinely fail to move it.22

The full model reaches 0.783, and this is where the recasting matters. Among its predictors is postacute_LOS, the length of the post-acute care stay, which is a consequence of receiving post-acute therapy: the median is 23 days among those who received therapy and 0 among those who did not. It is an excellent predictor and it cannot appear in any model whose coefficients are to be read causally: conditioning on a descendant of the outcome is the error of Chapter 12, and no coefficient in this fit survives it. The AUC cannot detect any of this. Discrimination and identification are separate properties, and a model can have as much of the first as one likes while having none of the second.

Two further tools of the same family, the confusion matrix at a chosen cut-off and the calibration plot, are properties of predictions as well, and they belong with the predictive question. The in-sample versions that could be shown here would demonstrate the diagnostics without validating anything; Chapter 27 computes both on held-out data, where they carry evidence, and sets out the hierarchy of calibration that a risk model is judged by.23

25.10 Where this leads

The GLM changed the likelihood and the link and left the causal logic where it was. Two extensions follow. The offset of the rate model treated each patient’s follow-up as one number; Chapter 26 lets the rate vary over time, expands the cohort into person-time rows, and returns to the cohort’s stroke outcome as a time to event. And the last section opened the predictive question, in which the model is judged by its predictions and not by the meaning of its coefficients; Chapter 27 treats it on its own terms, with the discrimination and calibration measures introduced here computed where they carry evidence, out of sample.

25.11 Key points

  • A generalised linear model is three things: an exponential-family distribution for the outcome, a linear predictor, and a link joining the mean to the linear predictor. Only the link is new. Everything from Chapter 22 to Chapter 24 — adjustment sets from a DAG, splines for continuous confounders, interaction by default, priors reasoned about in the units of the predictor — carries over unchanged.
  • The link is a choice of scale for the model, not a property of the outcome and not a choice of what to report. A binary outcome can be fitted with an identity, log or logit link; the coefficients are then a risk difference, a log risk ratio and a log odds ratio, and the standardised risk difference and risk ratio come out the same from all three (Figure 25.3). “Canonical” describes the algebra of the exponential family, not the scientific relevance of the measure.
  • The linear probability model can give a reasonable population-average risk difference while asserting negative probabilities for individuals. Use it, if at all, for an average in a setting with few covariates and risks away from the boundaries, not for prediction or extrapolation.
  • The odds ratio is not collapsible: adjusting for a covariate that predicts the outcome but is unrelated to treatment moves it away from 1 even under randomisation. It is a change of estimand, not a correction of bias. Consequently adjusted odds ratios from studies with different covariate sets are not comparable, and a logistic coefficient is not a population-average effect.
  • An unmeasured cause of the outcome that is not a confounder still attenuates the logistic coefficient, severely — by 28% when the path has coefficient 1.5 — while leaving the identity-link coefficient equal to the marginal risk difference. It does not measurably damage the standardised contrast from the same logistic fit. The threat is to reading coefficients, not to the estimand. One consequence is that the change-in-estimate criterion for confounder selection is invalid under a logit link, because the crude-to-adjusted shift mixes confounding with non-collapsibility.
  • Outcome-dependent sampling leaves the logistic slopes unchanged and displaces every other link’s, the log link’s included, which is why logistic regression is the analysis of a case-control study. What the sample odds ratio then estimates depends on how the controls were drawn — a rate ratio under density sampling, a risk ratio under case-cohort sampling — and known sampling fractions or an external baseline risk turn it back into risks. The design fixes the link, not the report.
  • Fitting two links is a weak check on bias. Compared as coefficients the two differ even when both are correct; compared as standardised contrasts the spread between links is neither an upper nor a lower bound on the bias, because functional-form error is largely shared across links. Correcting the covariate specification returns far more than changing the link, and agreement across links is no evidence at all against unmeasured confounding.
  • loo can choose the likelihood family and not, by itself, the link. The Poisson working model cannot enter a loo comparison against a Bernoulli model, because its likelihood spends probability on counts a 0/1 outcome never takes and the gap that opens is mechanical. And loo scores the outcome that was observed, while the standardised contrast needs the one that was not: under poor overlap the ranking by predictive score need not follow the estimand error, and its margins never scale with it. Use loo on the family and on the covariate specification, on the link only within the Bernoulli family and as a check, and never on the adjustment set; where links tie, stack rather than choose.
  • The book fits the cohort’s outcome models with a log link because the mechanism combines multiplicatively in risk, so a constant risk ratio is the working assumption that matches it; the logit is the link for a common outcome, for prediction and for case-control designs, and the identity is the linear probability model only. A Bernoulli likelihood with a log link does not sample; a Poisson working model on the 0/1 outcome does, and returns risk ratios with mildly conservative intervals. The probit is interchangeable with the logit for almost every purpose, and the complementary log-log is a grouped-survival model reached for on those grounds only.
  • For genuine counts, unequal observation time enters as an offset \(\log(t_i)\), which turns a model of counts into a model of rates. Omitting it estimates a ratio of counts, which is a different quantity. The logarithm is forced: proportionality of the mean to exposure is multiplicative and the linear predictor is additive, so an additive offset can express it only under a log link. Using \(t_i\) in place of \(\log t_i\) asserts exponential growth in follow-up and is worse than no offset at all. Fixing the coefficient at 1 is itself the assumption; estimating it tests \(\mathbb{E}[Y] \propto t^{\gamma}\). Over-dispersion widens intervals without moving point estimates; the negative binomial supplies the second parameter.
  • Because effects are reported as contrasts of predictions, one fitted model reports all three measures. Report the risk difference for decisions (its reciprocal is the number needed to treat), the risk ratio for transport between populations, and the odds ratio as an intermediate quantity, converted before it is reported.
  • An outcome that is a proportion without a denominator is bounded but not binomial: fit it with a beta likelihood, with zero- or one-inflation where the boundaries are occupied by a different process, and model its precision as well as its mean when the spread is the substantive point.
  • A product term is an interaction on the link scale only. A log-link model can show no multiplicative interaction and a large additive one at the same time (Chapter 17); report both scales with the stratum-specific effects.
  • ROC curves, AUC and confusion matrices measure discrimination, which is a property of predictions. A strong, well-determined risk factor may barely move the AUC, and a variable that is a consequence of the outcome may raise it substantially while destroying every causal reading of the model. For risk outputs, calibration matters more than discrimination.

Do the two models agree about the additive interaction even though their product terms differ? What does the answer say about reading interaction off a coefficient?

Take the spread_row() function and add a scenario in which the outcome model is correctly specified but a confounder is omitted altogether from the adjustment set, so that a back-door path is left open. Then, using the known sampling fraction, weight each non-stroke row by ten and standardise the weighted logistic fit to the risk difference and risk ratio; compare with the answer key.
